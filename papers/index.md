## Abramson_Whalen_2017_VOTat50  (voice-onset-time, stops, acoustics, phoneme-acoustics)
This paper provides a comprehensive retrospective on Voice Onset Time (VOT) measurement after 50 years of use, clarifying the original definition of VOT as the temporal relation between stop release and the onset of glottal pulsing, and extending the framework to intervocalic positions, fricatives, and affricates. The key findings show that VOT values fall into three main categories (prevoiced, short lag, long lag) with systematic variation by place of articulation, and the authors propose standardized Praat labeling conventions (VDCLO, VLCLO, REL, ASP) with automated tools for consistent measurement across studies. Understanding VOT timing and the ancillary acoustic cues that accompany voicing distinctions is critical for accurate stop consonant synthesis, particularly for capturing the aspiration duration and burst characteristics that differentiate voiced and voiceless stops in English and other languages.

## Alku_1997_ParabolicSpectralParameter  (glottal-source, voice-quality, spectral-analysis, inverse-filtering)
Introduces Parabolic Spectral Parameter (PSP), a frequency-domain measure that quantifies glottal flow spectral decay by fitting a parabola to the low-frequency portion of the pitch-synchronous power spectrum and normalizing against the theoretical maximum decay (DC-flow). PSP is F0-invariant and was the only parameter tested that monotonically distinguished breathy, normal, and pressed phonation for all 10 speakers, outperforming time-domain parameters (OQ, SQ, CQ) and the harmonic richness factor (HRF). The method uses adaptive frequency range selection (NE < 0.01 threshold) and requires inverse-filtered glottal flow as input.

## Alku_1999_SPL_DpeakLinearity  (glottal-source, amplitude, lf-model, inverse-filtering)
This paper demonstrates that the relationship between sound pressure level (SPL) and the negative peak amplitude of the differentiated glottal flow (dpeak/Ee) is better modeled by two linear segments than a single line, with a "knee" occurring at the soft-to-normal phonation transition. The key finding is that the slope for soft phonation is consistently steeper than for normal/loud speech across all speakers, because rising intensity at the transition point is achieved through changes in glottal waveform shape (reduced spectral decay) rather than increased dpeak amplitude alone. For formant synthesis, this refines how voice source amplitude (Ee) maps to radiated intensity, indicating that accurate SPL control across a wide dynamic range requires accounting for spectral tilt changes alongside the primary excitation amplitude parameter.

## Alku_2002_NormalizedAmplitudeQuotient  (glottal-source, voice-quality, spectrum-estimation, inverse-filtering)
Introduces the Normalized Amplitude Quotient (NAQ), a voice source parameter computed from the ratio of ac flow amplitude to negative peak amplitude of the flow derivative, normalized by the fundamental period. NAQ provides a robust alternative to the conventional closing quotient (CQ) for characterizing the glottal closing phase, requiring only amplitude-domain measurements rather than error-prone time-instant extraction. Experiments with breathy, normal, and pressed vowels from 10 speakers show NAQ monotonically tracks phonation type and is more noise-robust than CQ, with a direct mathematical relationship to Fant's Rd parameter (Rd = NAQ/110).

## Allen_1973_SpeechSynthesisUnrestrictedText  (tts, letter-to-sound, text-normalization, prosody, klatt, g2p)
Allen presents the complete architecture for converting unrestricted English text to speech, arguing that both text and speech are surface representations of a shared underlying linguistic structure and that TTS should proceed by analyzing text into this abstract representation then synthesizing speech from it. The paper details the full pipeline: morph decomposition using a 12,000-entry lexicon derived from the Brown Corpus, letter-to-sound rules for unknown words, morphophonemic adjustment rules, lexical stress assignment based on Chomsky-Halle generative phonology, sentence-level parsing via phrase detection, and prosodic control of duration and F0 contours including Halliday's functional model of discourse. This is the theoretical blueprint for the MITalk system (Allen et al. 1987), with the Klatt formant synthesizer explicitly shown as the vocal-tract model backend.

## Allen_1977_ModularAudioResponse  (tts, speech-synthesis, vocal-tract)
This paper presents the modular architecture of MIT's text-to-speech system, defining a pipeline of eight interconnected modules (FORMAT, DECOMP, PARSER, SOUND1, SOUND2, PROSE, SYNTHS, SPEAK) that process text through morphological analysis, parsing, phonological rules, prosody computation, and synthesis-by-rule. The key contribution is establishing a flexible, exportable framework where each linguistic domain has well-defined data structures and can be developed independently, along with special-purpose hardware designs for real-time vocal tract modeling using 32 digital resonators. This 1977 paper documents the precursor to MITalk and establishes the canonical TTS pipeline architecture that influenced all subsequent formant synthesis systems including the Klatt synthesizer.

## Allen_1987_MITalk_TTS  (tts, speech-synthesis, klatt, duration, prosody)
This book documents the complete MITalk text-to-speech system, a production-ready implementation that converts unrestricted text into intelligible speech through a cascade of text analysis, prosody modeling, and Klatt formant synthesis modules. Allen, Hunnicutt, and Klatt present explicit algorithms for morphological analysis, letter-to-sound conversion, duration and intonation rules, and the full specification of the 39-parameter Klatt synthesizer. The work is foundational for practical TTS implementation, providing detailed phonetic rules, formant targets, transition templates, and evaluation results (6.9% error rate) that directly inform modern concatenative and parametric speech synthesis systems.

## Anumanchipalli_KLATTSTAT  (klatt, speech-synthesis, formants, source-filter)
KLATTSTAT demonstrates automatic extraction of all 40 Klatt formant synthesizer parameters from natural speech signals and their integration into a statistical parametric speech synthesis framework, bridging classical knowledge-based synthesis with modern machine learning approaches. The paper achieves completely intelligible TTS output by training CART decision trees on extracted Klatt parameters (including GMM-based detection of articulatory features like nasality, aspiration, and frication) and predicting these parameters from text at synthesis time. This work is significant for speech synthesis because it proves that knowledge-based Klatt parameters are both automatically extractable and predictable, enabling interpretable parameter manipulation for style and voice quality control while establishing Klatt's viability as an alternative to spectral parameterizations like MCEPs.

## Assmann_1990_ConcurrentVowelPerception  (perception, speech-synthesis, vowels, klatt)
Assmann & Summerfield (1990) evaluate four computational models of how listeners identify the constituents of simultaneously presented synthetic vowels with different fundamental frequencies. The models share a common architecture of auditory filtering, pitch determination, source segregation, and template-based vowel classification, but differ in whether they use spectral (place) or temporal (place-time) representations and whether they incorporate compressive nonlinearity. The nonlinear place-time model best predicts both the overall accuracy and detailed pattern of listeners' identification responses, supporting the role of temporal analysis in segregating concurrent sound sources.

## Babel_2014_VocalAttractiveness  (vocal-attractiveness, voice-quality, perception, breathiness)
This paper examines which acoustic parameters predict vocal attractiveness ratings for 60 Californian English speakers, using an expanded feature set including f0, formant frequencies, spectral tilt (H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A2, H1-A3), HNR, jitter, shimmer, CPP, duration, and energy, analyzed through PCA and stepwise regression. The key findings are that vocal attractiveness is multidimensional: for female voices, breathier voice quality and conformity to community vowel norms (/u/-fronting) predict attractiveness (r^2 = 0.54), while for male voices, shorter durations and lower F1 indicating larger apparent vocal tract size are the main predictors (r^2 = 0.60), with f0 and formant position alone being poor predictors. For speech synthesis, this work provides background on which voice quality parameters listeners attend to when evaluating voices, with the spectral tilt and breathiness findings offering some guidance for designing perceptually appealing voice presets, though the paper's primary contribution is to voice perception research rather than synthesis implementation.

## Badin_1989_FricativeProductionModelling  (fricatives, acoustics, vocal-tract, source-filter, aerodynamics)
This paper presents experimental measurements and acoustic modeling of voiceless fricative production, establishing quantitative relationships between intra-oral pressure, constriction area, and radiated sound pressure level for [f], [s], and [ʃ]. The key contributions are empirical exponent values (p≈1.3, q≈0.3 for sibilants; p≈0.8, q≈0.2 for labiodentals) in the SPL∝(Δp)^p·(A_c)^q relationship, plus demonstration that spectral tilt increases with overall amplitude and that the "obstacle effect" can be explained by source location relative to the front cavity. This work is directly relevant for Klatt-based fricative synthesis as it provides guidance on amplitude scaling differences between fricative classes and suggests that noise source spectrum should vary with amplitude level.

## Balasubramanium_2012_CepstralSexuallyAppealingVoice  (vocal-attractiveness, voice-quality, mate-selection, perception)
This paper investigates the acoustic characteristics of sexually appealing voices using cepstral analysis. Two hundred Malayalam-speaking adults (100 male, 100 female, aged 18-24) produced spontaneous speech samples that were perceptually rated for sexual attractiveness by six heterosexual Hindi-speaking judges. Voices consistently rated as very attractive (39 male, 28 female) were compared against unattractive voices using Cepstral Peak Prominence (CPP) extracted via Computerized Speech Lab. Results showed significantly higher CPP values in attractive voices for both genders (males: attractive M=2.64 vs unattractive M=2.56; females: attractive M=2.45 vs unattractive M=2.39; both p<0.05), suggesting that well-defined harmonic organization -- greater periodicity and stronger harmonic energy -- characterizes sexually appealing voice. The finding links CPP, a measure of harmonic-to-noise ratio in the cepstral domain, to perceived vocal attractiveness, though the authors caution against overreliance on CPP as a sole indicator.

## Banse_1996_VocalEmotionAcousticProfiles  (emotion, acoustics, fundamental-frequency, speaking-style, emotional-prosody)
This paper presents a comprehensive empirical study of acoustic profiles for 14 distinct emotions, measuring 29 acoustic parameters (fundamental frequency, energy, spectral distribution, speech rate, articulation duration) across 224 actor portrayals to establish quantitative acoustic correlates of emotional speech. Key findings show that high-arousal emotions like hot anger and panic fear exhibit elevated mean F0, increased energy, and enhanced high-frequency content, while low-arousal emotions like sadness and boredom show reduced F0, lower intensity, and flattened spectra, with recognition accuracy ranging from 78% for hot anger to 15% for disgust. For speech synthesis, these emotion-specific acoustic profiles provide concrete targets for modulating F0 contours, spectral tilt, energy distribution, and speech rate to convey distinct emotional expressions in synthetic speech.

## Barreda_2015_FormantSpeakerSize  (formants, perception, speaker-characteristics, vowels)
This paper investigates whether listeners estimate speaker size by recovering vocal-tract length (VTL) from speech or by responding directly to formant frequencies, using synthetic vowels with systematically varied formant patterns. The key finding is that listeners rely primarily on formant frequencies themselves rather than deriving a phoneme-independent VTL estimate, with higher formants (F4, F5) improving consistency and strong phoneme-specific biases persisting even when they contradict VTL cues. For speech synthesis, this means that perceived speaker size is inherently tied to vowel identity rather than being a purely speaker-specific parameter, and that maintaining realistic F4/F5 values is important for coherent speaker characterization across phonemes.

## Bartkova_1987_ModelSegmentalDurationFrench  (duration, cross-linguistic, speech-synthesis, prosody)
Presents a multiplicative duration model for French speech synthesis where each phoneme's duration equals its intrinsic duration (speaker-independent) multiplied by position-dependent coefficients (speaker-dependent) reflecting prosodic context, syllable position, and phonetic class of neighboring segments. The model achieves prediction accuracy within the just-noticeable difference threshold (~20 ms) and explicitly separates speaker-independent from speaker-dependent parameters, enabling adaptation to different articulation rates. Validated on 2 speakers reading multiple corpora, with comparison to O'Shaughnessy's (1984) French duration model.

## Beckers_2021_TTSGenderTrustworthiness  (tts, perception, gender-differences, speech-synthesis)
This master's thesis investigates how TTS voice gender (male vs female) and accent (national Dutch vs foreign Belgian) affect perceived trustworthiness and listening experience of news content, using a 2x2 between-subjects design with 326 Dutch participants and Google WaveNet voices. The key finding is that male TTS voices are rated significantly more trustworthy than female voices (M=5.09 vs M=4.00, p<.001) for news reading, while national accent voices produce a better listening experience mediated by perceived similarity-attraction, with qualitative interviews revealing that listeners associate male voices with "seriousness" and "expertise" despite acknowledging the gender bias. For speech synthesis voice persona design, this establishes that voice gender and regional accent are significant social variables affecting listener trust and engagement, though the thesis provides no acoustic parameter data as it uses commercial black-box TTS.

## Beckman_1990_LengtheningsShorteningsProsodic  (duration, prosody, boundary-lengthening, intonation, word-boundary)
Experimental study distinguishing two durational effects in English: phrase-final lengthening at intonational phrase boundaries and word-final lengthening at lexical item edges. Through three experiments varying prosodic context and accent placement, Beckman and Edwards show that phrase-final lengthening is large and consistent across speakers, while word-final lengthening is smaller and speaker-dependent. The paper establishes that these effects imply different prosodic constituency models and should be modeled independently.

## Beckman_2005_ToBISystemEvolution  (prosody, intonation, tobi, annotation, phrasing)
This chapter provides the definitive account of the MAE_ToBI prosodic annotation system, specifying the complete inventory of pitch accents (H*, L*, L+H*, L*+H, H+!H*), phrase accents (H-, L-), boundary tones (H%, L%), and break indices (0-4) for Mainstream American English, along with their alignment rules and inter-tier constraints. The authors chronicle the four Prosodic Transcription Workshops (1991-1994) that produced the conventions, ground them in the autosegmental-metrical theory of Pierrehumbert (1980), and articulate design principles for extending the framework to other languages. For speech synthesis, this is the primary reference for mapping phonological prosodic labels to F0 targets and phrase boundary effects, providing the standard notation that Qlatt's prosody rules are designed to generate.

## Beckman_2022_ToBISystem  (prosody, intonation, tobi, annotation, phrasing)
This paper presents the ToBI (Tones and Break Indices) transcription system, a standardized phonological framework for annotating prosodic features including pitch accents, boundary tones, and juncture strength in spoken English. The key contributions include the complete MAE_ToBI annotation conventions with a 4-point break index scale, an inventory of pitch accents (H*, L*, L+H*, L*+H, H+!H*) and boundary tones, and the AM+ theoretical extension proposing five pitch levels (EH, H, M, L, EL) with feature specifications. For speech synthesis, ToBI provides the essential mapping between phonological prosodic labels and phonetic F0 targets, enabling principled generation of intonation patterns, phrase boundaries, and pitch range dynamics.

## Beddor_1986_NasalVowelHeight  (nasals, vowels, perception, formants, coarticulation)
This paper investigates whether listener misperceptions of the acoustic effects of vowel nasalisation can explain cross-language phonological patterns of nasal vowel height shifts (lowering of high vowels, raising of low vowels), using articulatory synthesis, natural speech acoustics, and perceptual experiments with American English listeners. The key finding is that nasalisation introduces a pole-zero pair near F1 that shifts the spectral centre of gravity in the F1-F2 region in ways consistent with the phonological patterns, but perceptual height shifts only occur when nasalisation is phonetically inappropriate — contextual nasalisation in expected environments is correctly factored out by listeners. Directly relevant to nasalisation modeling in formant synthesis: the pole-zero pair model (FZ, FN, F1') and the importance of coupling strength for perceptual naturalness constrain how nasal parameters should be set in the Klatt cascade/parallel architecture.

## Behrens_Blumstein_1988_FricativeAmplitude  (fricatives, perception, amplitude, phoneme-acoustics)
This paper investigates whether fricative noise amplitude or spectral properties are the primary perceptual cues for distinguishing voiceless fricatives like [f], [θ], [s], and [ʃ]. The authors found that spectral properties of the fricative noise and formant transitions are the dominant cues for place of articulation, while amplitude manipulations have minimal perceptual effect when spectral cues are congruent. For speech synthesis, this means getting fricative spectral shapes right is more important than precise amplitude control, and the sibilants [s] and [ʃ] are particularly robust to amplitude variation.

## Belin_2017_SoundOfTrustworthiness  (vocal-attractiveness, fundamental-frequency, perception, prosody)
This paper demonstrates that perceived voice trustworthiness can be parametrically controlled through acoustic manipulation, achieving an r=0.99 correlation between acoustic continuum position and listener ratings. The key finding is a specific f0 contour pattern for trustworthy-sounding speech: high starting pitch, marked mid-utterance drop, and strong terminal rise—with caricaturing (exaggerating these features) amplifying the effect. For Qlatt's prosody system, this provides empirically-validated f0 contour targets for generating speech that sounds friendly and trustworthy, though the formant-specific parameters would need separate investigation.

## Belyk_2014_AcousticValenceEmotion  (emotion, fundamental-frequency, amplitude, emotional-prosody)
This paper demonstrates that the acoustic correlates of emotional valence (positive vs negative) in vocal exclamations depend on emotion family: motivational emotions follow a "high-loud" positive pattern, moral emotions follow a "high-soft" pattern, and aesthetic emotions follow a "low-soft" pattern. The authors propose a 2×2 Pitch × Loudness framework of four "expression rules" that systematize how different emotion categories map onto vocal acoustics, resolving prior inconsistencies in the valence-coding literature. For Qlatt, this provides an empirically grounded rule set for emotion-dependent prosody modification — specifically, family-specific F0 and amplitude adjustments rather than a naive "happy=high, sad=low" mapping — should emotional speech synthesis be implemented.

## Bjorklund_2016_SubglottalPressureSPL  (acoustics, gender-differences, amplitude, laryngeal-source)
Bjorklund and Sundberg (2016) establish normative regression data relating subglottal pressure (Ps) to sound pressure level (SPL) in 31 untrained healthy speakers, finding an average correlation of r = 0.83 and quantifying that a doubling of Ps produces 11.1 dB (female) or 9.3 dB (male) increase in SPL. The study demonstrates gender differences in phonatory efficiency: males produce ~2 dB higher SPL at equivalent pressure but show a shallower slope, and pitch does not significantly affect the Ps-SPL relationship. For speaker personality modeling, this provides the quantitative mapping from respiratory effort to acoustic output level, with gender-specific parameters that should be reflected in any physiologically-grounded voice synthesis system.

## Black_1998_LTS_Rules  (tts, speech-synthesis, g2p)
This paper presents a general framework for automatically building letter-to-sound (LTS) rules from pronunciation lexicons using CART decision trees, enabling TTS systems to synthesize out-of-vocabulary words. The key contribution is a two-stage approach combining letter-phone alignment (via hand-seeded allowables tables or automatic EM-based epsilon scattering) with CART tree training, achieving 74.56% word accuracy on British English and demonstrating generalization across multiple languages. LTS rules are essential for robust speech synthesis because they allow TTS systems to handle the infinite space of possible input words despite having finite pronunciation lexicons, making this work foundational for practical TTS deployment.

## Blumstein_1977_PropertyDetectorsBurstsTransitions  (stops, phoneme-acoustics, formant-transitions, perception)
Blumstein, Stevens, and Nigro use selective adaptation experiments to investigate how the auditory system processes burst and formant transition cues for stop consonant place of articulation. The results establish that burst and transition information are combined into a single integrated acoustic property at consonantal release, rather than being processed independently as simple cues.

## Blumstein_Stevens_1979_AcousticInvariance  (stops, acoustics, phoneme-acoustics)
This paper demonstrates that stop consonant place of articulation can be classified with approximately 85% accuracy using three invariant spectral templates applied at consonantal release, independent of vowel context, voicing, syllable position, and speaker. For speech synthesis, these findings establish that burst spectra must match specific spectral shapes to convey place of articulation correctly, making them essential design constraints for Klatt stop consonant synthesis.

## Bonada_2008_VoiceSynthesisSpectralModels  (speech-synthesis, source-filter, glottal-source, formants)

## Borkowska_2011_F0DominanceAttractiveness  (fundamental-frequency, perception, vocal-attractiveness, gender-differences)

## Browman_1989_ArticulatoryGesturesPhonologicalUnits  (articulatory-phonology, coarticulation, vocal-tract, phoneme-acoustics)

## Browman_Goldstein_1992_ArticulatoryPhonologyOverview  (articulatory-phonology, coarticulation, vocal-tract)

## Caballero_2018_SoundOfImpoliteness  (prosody, fundamental-frequency, speaking-style, pragmatics)

## Campbell_Isard_1991_SegmentDurationsSyllable  (duration, speech-synthesis, prosody)

## Carlson_1979_VowelPerceptionSalience  (vowels, perception, formants, formant-bandwidths)

## Fry_1958_PerceptionStress  (prosody, perception, duration, fundamental-frequency, amplitude, english)

The Motor Theory of Speech Perception proposes that listeners perceive speech by recovering the intended articulatory gestures of the speaker rather than by analyzing acoustic patterns directly. The theory explains how perception remains robust despite acoustic variability, trading relations between cues, and the absence of invariant acoustic features for individual phonemes. For speech synthesis, the theory validates formant-based approaches by showing that listeners accept any acoustic pattern that plausibly represents motor gestures, making context-appropriate formant trajectories and proper timing more important than acoustic exactness.

## Lienard_1999_VocalEffortVowelSpectral  (vocal-effort, vowels, fundamental-frequency, formants)
This paper quantifies how vocal effort (soft to loud everyday speech) systematically affects vowel acoustics, establishing that F0 increases linearly at 5 Hz/dB and F1 at ~3.5 Hz/dB while F2 and F3 remain stable. The key finding is differential spectral tilt: formant amplitudes A1, A2, A3 increase at rates of 1.10, 1.24, and 1.30 dB per dB of overall amplitude increase, making higher frequencies relatively more prominent in loud speech. For Klatt synthesis, these relationships enable modeling natural loudness variation, stress/prominence, and emotional intensity by coupling the master gain parameter to systematic F0, F1, and formant amplitude adjustments.

## Lin_1995_CascadeIntoParallel  (klatt, speech-synthesis, formants)
This ASA conference abstract describes a mathematical method for converting cascade formant filter configurations into equivalent parallel configurations by computing the complex amplitudes (residues) from formant frequencies, bandwidths, and higher-pole correction using partial fraction expansion. ## Lisker_1964_CrossLanguageVoicingInitialStops  (stops, voice-onset-time, cross-linguistic, acoustics)
The foundational study defining Voice Onset Time (VOT) as the primary acoustic measure for distinguishing stop consonant categories across languages. Through spectrographic analysis of stops in 11 languages (including English, Thai, Hindi, Korean, and Dutch), Lisker and Abramson demonstrate that stop categories universally cluster around three VOT modes: voicing lead (~-100 ms), short lag (~+10 ms), and long lag (~+75 ms). The paper provides extensive VOT measurement tables for all languages studied, establishing the quantitative framework used by all subsequent research on stop consonant voicing distinctions.

## Lisker_Abramson_1964_CrossLanguageVoicingStops  (voice-onset-time, stops, acoustics, phoneme-acoustics)
This landmark paper introduces Voice Onset Time (VOT) as a single acoustic dimension -- the time interval between stop release and the onset of glottal pulsing -- that effectively separates stop consonant categories across 11 typologically diverse languages, demonstrating that all languages draw their stop categories from approximately three universal modes on the VOT continuum (voicing lead at ~-100 ms, short lag at ~+10 ms, and long lag at ~+75 ms). The authors provide extensive spectrographic measurement data for isolated words and sentences showing that two-category languages select two of these modes, three-category languages use all three, and four-category languages require additional voice quality cues beyond VOT alone. For speech synthesis, VOT is the fundamental timing parameter governing the coordination of voicing source (AV), aspiration noise (AH), and burst (AF) parameters at stop consonant release, with systematic variation by place of articulation (velar > alveolar > labial) that must be implemented in duration rules.

## Lucero_1999_BifurcationsVoiceOnsetOffset  (glottal-source, laryngeal-source, voice-quality)
# Lucero 1999 — Bifurcations at Voice Onset-Offset

Lucero demonstrates that phonation onset and offset involve different dynamical bifurcations — a subcritical Hopf bifurcation at onset (where an unstable limit cycle is absorbed and the system jumps to a large-amplitude stable oscillation) and a saddle-node (fold) bifurcation of limit cycles at offset (where the stable and unstable cycles collide and annihilate). This pair of bifurcations produces oscillation hysteresis: onset requires higher subglottal pressure than offset, with a Ps_offset/Ps_onset ratio of approximately 0.45 in the two-mass model. The paper simulates this hysteresis using the Ishizaka-Flanagan two-mass model coupled to a two-tube vocal tract for /a/, and presents bifurcation diagrams showing that voice intensity at onset is roughly 10 dB higher than at offset across the F0 range. This framework explains why vocal fold configuration differs between onset and offset, a well-established experimental observation that cannot be accounted for by a supercritical Hopf bifurcation alone.

## Lucero_2005_VocalFoldBifurcations  (glottal-source, laryngeal-source, voice-quality)
This paper presents a complete bifurcation analysis of the Titze (1988) mucosal wave vocal fold oscillator model, deriving the Lyapunov number that determines whether phonation onset is soft (supercritical Hopf) or hard (subcritical Hopf with hysteresis). The key finding is that for physiologically typical parameters the bifurcation is always supercritical, but an extended model with quartic nonlinear damping can produce the oscillation hysteresis observed experimentally between phonation onset and offset pressures. This is relevant to the Qlatt synthesizer for physically motivated modeling of glottal source onset/offset behavior, phonation threshold pressure, and register transitions.

For Qlatt, the paper primarily contributes gender-specific physiological parameter ranges (F0, vocal fold length, thyroid angle) useful for male/female voice presets, though its main focus is pathology mechanics rather than acoustic synthesis parameters.

## Mannell_1998_FormantDiphoneExtraction  (formants, formant-bandwidths, speech-synthesis, klatt)

## Monson_2012_SpeechDirectivityHFE  (directivity, fricatives, acoustics)

For speech synthesis, these findings provide specific F2 targets (light ~1300 Hz, dark ~900 Hz) and F3 targets (light ~2900 Hz, dark ~2400 Hz) for implementing syllable-position-dependent /l/ allophony in Klatt formant synthesis, directly informing the dark-L rule in Qlatt's declarative frontend.

## Staib_2021_CorticalVoiceProcessing  (perception, acoustics, voice-quality)
This paper investigates how the temporal voice area (TVA) in auditory cortex processes voice and non-voice sounds, demonstrating that the TVA responds to synthetic textural sound patterns that share perceptual similarity with voices rather than being exclusively selective for voice signals. Using fMRI, the authors reconstructed TVA voice-processing patterns from synthetic sounds and showed that perceptual voice similarity ratings explain significant variance in neural activation beyond acoustic features alone. For speech synthesis, this work highlights that spectral flux and other acoustic properties contributing to perceived voice similarity are important for naturalness, and suggests that synthetic speech quality should be evaluated along a perceptual similarity gradient rather than discrete voice/non-voice categories.

## Starr_2015_SweetVoiceJapaneseFeminine  (voice-quality, gender-differences, speaking-style, fundamental-frequency)
This paper acoustically characterizes "sweet voice," a distinctive Japanese feminine vocal style used by professional voice actresses (seiyuu), analyzing six voice quality measures (H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3, CPP, HNR, 2k-4k) across ten anime performances and four intra-speaker sweet/nonsweet comparisons. The key finding is that sweet voice is produced through head register phonation — not high pitch or breathiness — yielding elevated spectral tilt (H1-H2 +3.4 dB), enhanced periodicity (HNR +2 dB), and stronger upper harmonic energy (2k-4k −1.1 dB), while mean F0 (~263 Hz) is actually lower than typical anime heroines. For speech synthesis, this work demonstrates that perceptually distinct vocal styles arise from specific phonation configurations (open quotient, spectral tilt, HNR) rather than F0 alone, providing quantitative parameter shifts that could inform voice quality presets in Klatt-style synthesizers.

## Stathopoulos_2011_VoiceAcrossLifespan  (fundamental-frequency, aging, gender-differences, speaker-characteristics)
This paper presents a comprehensive cross-sectional study of fundamental frequency (F0), sound pressure level (SPL), and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) across the full human life span (ages 4-93) using 192 speakers, revealing nonlinear age trajectories that differ by sex. Key findings include that male F0 follows a U-shaped curve with minimum around age 50, female F0 declines more gradually, SNR shows sex-specific patterns linked to hormonal changes at menopause, and acoustic variability is elevated at both ends of the life span. For the Qlatt synthesizer, this work provides the empirical foundation for age-dependent voice parameter profiles, particularly F0 defaults, noise characteristics, and variability settings across child, adult, and elderly speaker categories.

## Steinecke_1995_BifurcationsVocalFold  (glottal-source, voice-quality, laryngeal-source)
# Steinecke & Herzel (1995)

Analyzes bifurcations in a simplified two-mass vocal fold model (reduced from Ishizaka & Flanagan 1972) using methods from nonlinear dynamics. The paper maps out bifurcation diagrams in two physiologically meaningful parameter planes — subglottal pressure vs. stiffness (for phonation onset) and subglottal pressure vs. left/right tension asymmetry (for paralysis-induced voice disorders). Shows that sufficient tension imbalance between vocal folds produces a cascade of bifurcations: period doubling (diplophonia), higher-order frequency locking, toroidal oscillations, and chaos, with hysteresis and coexisting attractors at transition boundaries. The key result for synthesis is that the asymmetry ratio Q = f_right/f_left is a single scalar parameter that controls the transition from normal phonation through subharmonics to chaotic vibration, with bifurcation boundaries that depend on subglottal pressure.

## Steinecke_Herzel_1995_BifurcationsAsymmetricVocalFold  (laryngeal-source, voice-quality)
Steinecke & Herzel (1995) present a systematic bifurcation analysis of a simplified asymmetric two-mass vocal fold model derived from Ishizaka & Flanagan (1972). They show that left-right tension imbalance (parameterized by a ratio Q) produces a rich sequence of bifurcations in the (Q, P_s) parameter plane: normal 1:1 phonation transitions through period doubling, subharmonic regimes, toroidal oscillations, and deterministic chaos as asymmetry increases. The results provide a principled dynamical-systems explanation for voice disorders associated with unilateral laryngeal paralysis, including diplophonia, subharmonic phonation, and aperiodic voice quality.

## Stevens_1955_QuantitativeVowelArticulation  (vocal-tract, vowels, formants, articulatory-synthesis)
This paper develops a three-parameter articulatory model for vowels (tongue constriction position, constriction radius, and mouth opening ratio) and maps these parameters to the first three formant frequencies using a 35-section electrical vocal tract analog. The key contribution is comprehensive experimental data from 306 configurations showing how F1, F2, and F3 vary with articulatory dimensions, plus contours of vowel articulation derived by transforming Peterson & Barney's acoustic vowel data into articulatory coordinates. While foundational for understanding vowel production, this paper addresses articulatory-to-acoustic mapping for steady-state vowels rather than consonant-vowel transitions or formant locus values, which are covered in the follow-up Stevens & House (1956) paper on formant transitions.

## Stevens_1971_AirflowTurbulenceNoise  (aerodynamics, fricatives, aspiration-noise, acoustics)
This paper derives the aerodynamic and acoustic equations governing turbulence noise generation at vocal tract constrictions, establishing that radiated sound pressure from frication and aspiration is proportional to pressure drop raised to a power (β ≈ 1.0-1.5) and weakly dependent on constriction area. Stevens provides equivalent circuit models for the noise source, charts mapping constriction geometry to airflow and noise levels for different speech sound classes (vowels, fricatives, aspirated sounds), and validates the theory against experimental data from pipe-immersed spoiler studies. This framework is foundational for understanding how Klatt synthesizer frication (AF) and aspiration (AH) amplitude parameters relate to the underlying aerodynamics, explaining why sibilants are 15-30 dB louder than labiodentals and why voiced fricative noise is pitch-synchronously modulated.

## Stevens_1972_QuantalNatureSpeech  (phoneme-acoustics, articulatory-synthesis, formants, phonology, vocal-tract, aerodynamics, voice-onset-time, perception)
Stevens presents quantal theory: the articulatory-to-acoustic mapping contains nonlinear regions where small articulatory changes produce large acoustic shifts, separated by plateau regions of acoustic stability. Using coupled-resonator models, turbulence noise analysis, and vocal fold vibration data, he demonstrates that languages exploit the stable plateau regions for phonological categories, making speech robust to articulatory imprecision. The paper provides detailed analyses of vowel formant patterns, fricative noise generation, voicing onset, and auditory processing that predict the universal tendencies in phonological inventories.

## Stevens_1978_InvariantCuesPlaceArticulation  (stops, phoneme-acoustics, perception, formant-transitions, speech-synthesis)
This paper uses synthetic stop consonant stimuli (produced on a Klatt-type cascade synthesizer) to establish that listeners identify place of articulation from the gross spectral shape at consonantal release, not from individual acoustic parameters like burst frequency or transition direction. Three invariant spectral templates are perceptually validated: diffuse-rising for alveolars, diffuse-falling for labials, and a compact midfrequency peak for velars. For Klatt synthesis, these results establish that the onset spectrum shape — determined jointly by burst characteristics and the first 20-30 ms of formant transitions — must match the appropriate template for correct place perception, and that bursts isolated from transitions are insufficient cues.

## Stevens_1989_QuantalNatureSpeech  (acoustics, phoneme-acoustics, perception, vocal-tract)
This paper presents the quantal theory of speech, proposing that the human vocal tract and auditory system exhibit non-linear articulatory-acoustic relationships that create stable "plateau" regions separated by rapid transition zones. Stevens demonstrates how specific phonetic features (vowel height, backness, rounding, nasality, consonant place and manner) correspond to these naturally stable acoustic configurations through analysis of coupled resonators, turbulence noise generation, vocal fold vibration modes, and auditory critical boundaries. For speech synthesis, this work provides principled acoustic targets based on physics rather than arbitrary parameters—formant frequencies can be selected from stability regions where small production errors don't dramatically alter the acoustic output.

## Stevens_1991_HL_Parameters  (klatt, speech-synthesis, source-filter, aerodynamics)
This paper presents a 10-parameter higher-level control system for the Klatt synthesizer that maps articulatory and aerodynamic parameters to the 40+ low-level Klatt parameters, automatically incorporating physical production constraints. The key contribution demonstrates that stop consonants and nasals with the same articulation differ only in 2-3 higher-level parameters, and provides explicit aerodynamic calculations for airflow, pressure, and noise sources that derive complex Klatt timing automatically. This framework is critical for speech synthesis because it reduces the control complexity from 40+ interdependent parameters to 10 intuitive articulatory-acoustic parameters, making formant synthesis practical and enabling analysis-by-synthesis approaches for matching natural speech.

## Stevens_1993_ModelsProductionAcousticsStop  (stops, aerodynamics, phoneme-acoustics, vocal-tract)
Stevens (1993) presents theoretical models of the mechanical, aerodynamic, and acoustic events during stop consonant production, using a two-constriction tube model to calculate airflows, pressures, and the four sequential sound sources at consonantal release (transient, frication burst, aspiration, voicing). The paper provides quantitative predictions of absolute sound levels and spectral shapes for labial, alveolar, and velar places of articulation, including constriction area trajectories and turbulence noise source amplitudes. This is directly relevant to synthesizer burst generation, AF/AH parameter scheduling, and place-dependent spectral shaping of stop consonant releases.

## Stevens_1998_AcousticPhonetics  (acoustics, phoneme-acoustics, source-filter, vocal-tract, speech-synthesis)
This comprehensive 607-page textbook by Kenneth N. Stevens establishes the quantitative relationship between articulatory movements, acoustic transfer functions, and voice source mechanisms for all major speech sound classes, providing theoretical foundations for understanding how speakers produce different phonemes. The work details precise measurements and equations for glottal flow derivatives, vocal tract resonances, formant frequencies and bandwidths, source-filter interactions, and acoustic landmarks for vowels, stops, fricatives, nasals, and liquids, grounded in extensive experimental data from Peterson & Barney and Klatt's work. For speech synthesis, Stevens provides the critical acoustic parameters and timing rules needed to generate natural-sounding speech by specifying formant targets, bandwidth adjustments, noise characteristics, and coarticulation windows for each phoneme class.

## Stevens_House_1956_FormantTransitionsVocalTract  (formants, coarticulation, vocal-tract, stops)
This paper uses a 35-section electrical vocal tract analog to empirically measure formant frequencies during consonant-vowel transitions, testing the "locus hypothesis" that each stop consonant class has a fixed F2 frequency origin. The key findings show that while post-dental (alveolar) stops have a relatively invariant F2 locus near 1800-2000 Hz, bilabial F2 loci vary from 700-1500 Hz and velar F2 loci vary from 600-2500 Hz depending on the adjacent vowel, with F1 loci at zero for all stops. These formant locus values are directly used in Qlatt's coarticulation rules for formant transitions during consonant-vowel sequences, particularly the alveolar F2 locus of 1800 Hz which this paper validates as articulatorily grounded.

## Stevens_House_1961_AcousticalTheoryVowelProduction  (vowels, formants, acoustics, source-filter, formant-bandwidths, vocal-tract, spectrum-estimation)
Stevens & House present a rigorous exposition of the acoustical theory of vowel production, deriving the vowel spectrum as the product of glottal source, vocal tract transfer function, and radiation characteristic. They compute isoamplitude contour plots showing how the levels of the first three formant peaks depend on formant frequencies, and demonstrate that these predictions match measured vowel intensities. The paper also proposes defining "formant" as a normal mode (pole) of the vocal tract, independent of excitation.

## Stevens_House_1963_PerturbationVowelConsonant  (coarticulation, formants, vowels, phoneme-acoustics, acoustics)
Stevens and House (1963) measured F1, F2, F3, and bandwidths for eight American English vowels produced by three male talkers in 14 symmetric CVC consonantal contexts, comparing them to null-context (/h-d/ and isolated) values. They demonstrate that consonantal context systematically shifts vowel formant frequencies -- F2 shifts toward a neutral value (~1500 Hz) with magnitude depending on consonant place, manner, and voicing -- and propose the articulatory undershoot hypothesis: the inertia of articulatory structures prevents them from reaching ideal vowel targets within the available time, with greater undershoot for shorter vowels and larger articulatory distances. The paper provides comprehensive formant and bandwidth tables that serve as empirical foundations for coarticulation rules in speech synthesis.

## Stevens_Klatt_1974_FormantTransitionsVoicing  (stops, voice-onset-time, formant-transitions, perception, speech-synthesis)
Stevens and Klatt demonstrate through synthesis experiments that the voiced-voiceless distinction for English prestressed stops depends not only on VOT but also on the presence or absence of formant transitions at voicing onset, with these two cues trading perceptually. The phoneme boundary shifts by about 13 ms (averaged across listeners) when transition duration changes by 30 ms, and the most natural voiceless stops are those where both VOT exceeds ~20 ms and formant transitions are completed before voicing begins. The paper also explains why VOT differs by place of articulation (velars > dentals > labials) as a consequence of different articulatory transition speeds.

## Story_1995_BodyCoverVocalFoldModel  (glottal-source, laryngeal-source, voice-quality, vocal-tract)
Presents a three-mass lumped-element model of the vocal folds that extends the classic IF72 two-mass model by adding a body mass to represent the deep muscle layer, enabling independent control of body and cover vibration. The model couples to a wave-reflection vocal tract and uses Bernoulli/jet flow equations to compute intraglottal pressure from mass displacements. Simulations reproduce Hirano's four laryngeal configurations (soft/loud/normal/falsetto) with physiologically grounded parameter values for mass, stiffness, and damping derived from tissue measurements.

## Story_1996_VocalTractAreaFunctionsMRI  (vocal-tract, vowels, nasals, stops, formants, acoustics)
MRI-derived vocal tract area functions for 18 phoneme configurations (12 vowels, 3 nasals, 3 plosives) from one male subject, providing the most comprehensive speaker-specific area function dataset for speech simulation. The paper includes complete numerical area function tables at equal intervals (0.396825 cm), vocal tract lengths, nasal coupling areas, and formant comparisons between natural and simulated speech. This is a foundational reference for vocal tract modeling, providing empirical area functions that can be used directly as input to acoustic tube models or formant synthesizers.

## Strik_Boves_1992_ControlF0IntensityVoiceQuality  (glottal-source, fundamental-frequency, amplitude, aerodynamics, lf-model, voice-quality, intonation, utterance-boundary)
Strik & Boves (1992) investigate the physiological control of F0, intensity, and voice quality in normal conversational speech using simultaneous recordings of acoustic, aerodynamic, and EMG signals. They demonstrate that transglottal pressure (P_tr) is a better predictor of both F0 and intensity than subglottal pressure (P_sb) in connected speech, with IL proportional to P_tr^3.03. The paper also documents voice quality changes at utterance boundaries, showing increased spectral tilt and aspiration noise in utterance-final vowels, and argues that the LF glottal source model lacks the physiological parameters needed to explain the different control regimes observed.

## Strom_2002_TextToProsodyWithoutToBI  (prosody, tts, duration, fundamental-frequency)
Strom (2002) presents a data-driven method for predicting prosodic parameters (phone durations and F0 targets) from preprocessed text, bypassing the need for manual ToBI annotation. The system uses four CARTs (Classification and Regression Trees) trained on a large speech database: two for binary accent/boundary prediction and two for F0 targets and phone duration z-scores. Prosodic labels are created via a bootstrapping/EM-like iterative process that alternates between prosody prediction from text and prosody recognition from text plus acoustics. Evaluated on German TTS voices, the data-driven approach (DataPro) was significantly preferred over hand-crafted rules (ManPro) by a 2:1 ratio in listening tests. Published at ICSLP 2002, AT&T Labs Research.

## Strong_1967_MachineAidedFormantDetermination  (formants, speech-synthesis, spectrum-estimation)
This paper describes a semi-automatic "manual formant vocoder" system where a human operator marks formant positions on processed spectral displays to extract control parameters (F1-F4 frequencies and amplitudes, F0) for a four-pole parallel synthesizer. The key contribution is the detailed intelligibility evaluation showing 4.2% vowel error and 16.9% consonant error rates, with analysis revealing that manner errors (fricatives heard as stops) dominate over place and voicing errors. This early parallel synthesizer work provides rationale for the parallel formant branch architecture and introduces the phase-reversal technique for even-numbered formants to cancel inter-formant zeros, though the fixed bandwidths and binary voicing decision are simpler than modern Klatt-style synthesizers.

## Sun_2006_VocalTractGlottalSource  (glottal-source, vocal-tract, inverse-filtering, voice-quality)
This paper proposes a codebook-based glottal source model that links LSF (vocal tract) parameters to normalized glottal waveforms, demonstrating that glottal wave shape is predictable from phonetic class within a speaker. The key finding is that clustering vocal tract parameters into 9 classes and selecting representative glottal derivatives achieves 27-30% higher correlation with original glottal waveforms and 50% lower spectral distance compared to LF and Rosenberg parametric models. For Klatt synthesis, this validates the principle that phoneme class strongly predicts glottal characteristics, suggesting that voice source parameters could be automatically varied by phoneme category rather than held constant across an utterance.

## Sundberg_1972_SingingFormant  (vocal-tract, formants, acoustics, voice-quality)
This paper explains the acoustic and articulatory mechanisms behind the "singing formant," a characteristic spectral peak near 3 kHz that allows professional opera singers to project their voice over an orchestra. Sundberg demonstrates through X-ray tomography and acoustic modeling that the singing formant arises from the lowered larynx acting as a Helmholtz resonator, combined with a spectral zero created by the sinus piriformes, resulting in approximately 20 dB of spectral gain in the 3 kHz region. Understanding the singing formant provides insights into vocal tract coupling and formant compression that could inform voice quality modifications and higher formant behavior in speech synthesis systems.

## Sundberg_1993_PhonatoryControlSinging  (glottal-source, aerodynamics, voice-quality, amplitude, inverse-filtering)
Experimental study of ten male singers (including two professional opera tenors) measuring how subglottal pressure, fundamental frequency, and phonation mode (flow, neutral, pressed) affect the voice source, analyzed via inverse-filtered flow glottograms. Key findings include that Ps approximately doubles per octave of F0, that SPL increases 8-9 dB per doubling of excess Ps over threshold in neutral phonation, and that peak glottal permittance (peak flow / Ps) effectively distinguishes phonation modes. The paper identifies three mechanisms by which increased Ps raises the maximum flow declination rate (MFDR): increased pulse amplitude, pulse skewing, and earlier closed-phase onset, with skewing behavior dependent on the F1/F0 ratio.

## Sundberg_2005_GlottalSourceLoudness  (glottal-source, voice-quality, gender-differences, inverse-filtering)
This paper quantifies how subglottal pressure variation affects glottal voice source parameters (MFDR, closed quotient, pulse amplitude, H1-H2, compliance) in 15 female and 14 male untrained speakers across three fundamental frequencies, using 10 pressure levels per condition. The key contribution is a set of analytic equations — linear regressions for MFDR and amplitude, a power function for closed quotient — with sex- and F0-differentiated constants that capture the nonlinear relationship between vocal effort and source waveform shape. These equations are directly applicable to effort-dependent voice quality control in formant synthesis, providing the mapping from a loudness/effort parameter to LF source model characteristics needed for natural-sounding speech.

## Szameitat_2011_LaughterEmotionAcoustics  (emotion, arousal, acoustics, perception)
This paper examines the acoustic correlates of four emotional dimensions (arousal, dominance, sender's valence, and receiver-directed valence) in 123 acted laughter sequences from 8 professional actors, correlating 43 acoustic parameters extracted via Praat with listener ratings on each dimension. The key findings show that arousal is most robustly encoded through F0, inter-bout duration, jitter, and shimmer (up to 44% shared variance), while dominance is marked by F1, peak frequency, and intensity variability, with all four dimensions predictable from 13 parameters (r = .75–.91). For speech synthesis, this work reinforces the arousal–acoustic mapping established in speech emotion studies (F0 ↑, jitter ↓, HNR ↑, high-frequency energy ↑) and extends it to non-verbal vocalizations, supporting a unified emotional dimension framework applicable to both speech prosody and non-speech vocalization synthesis.

## Taal_2011_STOI  (speech-intelligibility, acoustics, signal-processing)
This paper presents STOI (Short-Time Objective Intelligibility), a simple two-parameter measure that predicts speech intelligibility by computing correlation coefficients between normalized and clipped temporal envelopes of clean and degraded speech in 15 one-third octave bands over short 384 ms segments. STOI achieves higher correlation with subjective intelligibility scores than five competing objective measures across three diverse listening experiments including ITFS-processed speech, single-channel noise reduction, and speech with artificially introduced errors. For the Qlatt synthesizer, STOI provides an automated, standardized metric for evaluating how rule changes, frontend configurations, and parameter modifications affect the intelligibility of synthesized speech output, enabling objective regression testing and comparative evaluation without requiring listening tests.

## Taylor_2000_TiltModelIntonation  (intonation, prosody, fundamental-frequency, speech-synthesis)
The Tilt model represents F0 intonation contours using three independent parameters—amplitude, duration, and tilt—to describe pitch accents and boundary tones in a continuous rather than categorical framework. The model achieves automatic event detection via HMMs and provides equations for both analyzing F0 contours from speech and synthesizing new contours, with synthesis accuracy nearly identical to the more complex RFC model despite having fewer parameters. For speech synthesis, Tilt enables systematic generation of natural prosody by mapping accent-bearing syllables to events with predictable acoustic properties, supporting flexible F0 contour generation from prosodic markup.

## Titze_1979_LaryngealConfigurationsPhonation  (glottal-source, laryngeal-source, fundamental-frequency, voice-quality)
Titze and Talkin present a three-dimensional finite-difference model of vocal fold vibration with layered tissue structure (mucosa, ligament, muscle), simulating how variations in geometrical shape factors, tissue elasticities, viscosities, and subglottal pressure affect fundamental frequency, volume velocity, acoustic power, and vocal efficiency. The paper provides the first quantitative verification of Hirano's body-cover theory, showing that F0 is primarily controlled by ligament tension with secondary contributions from vocalis muscle tension, while subglottal pressure has minimal effect on frequency under linear conditions. The sensitivity analysis of 120 configurations maps physiological control parameters to acoustic outputs, offering a foundation for understanding how laryngeal configuration governs voice quality.

## Titze_1989_MaleFemaleVoices  (gender-differences, glottal-source, fundamental-frequency, vocal-tract)
This paper derives two physiologic scale factors -- alpha (1.2, general larynx size) and beta (1.6, membranous vocal fold length) -- from cadaveric data and uses them to predict male-female differences in fundamental frequency, mean airflow, sound power, glottal efficiency, and glottographic waveform shape through a vibrating string model and computer simulation of vocal fold contact area. Key findings include that F0 differences are almost entirely explained by membranous length (F0 = 1700/L_m), that vocal fold thickness scales with the smaller alpha factor rather than beta, that male vocal folds exhibit medial surface bulging producing distinct contact area waveform features, and that females are predicted to be ~25% more glottally efficient than males due to higher F0. For the Qlatt synthesizer's speaker personality system, this work provides the physiological foundation for parameterizing male-female voice differences using orthogonal scale factors for size and length, principled scaling laws for airflow and power, glottal shape quotients (Q_a, Q_s, Q_b, Q_p) that could drive the LF source model, and stress-strain relationships that connect F0 range to tissue mechanics.

## Titze_1991_NeurologicAperiodicity  (jitter, fundamental-frequency, laryngeal-source, voice-quality)
This paper develops a quantitative model of how stochastic motor neuron firing in thyroarytenoid muscle creates fundamental frequency perturbation (jitter) through twitch summation, predicting F0 perturbation magnitudes of 0.2-1.2% as a function of four parameters: number of motor units, mean firing rate, coefficient of variation of twitch amplitude, and coefficient of variation of interspike intervals. For the Qlatt synthesizer's speaker personality system, this provides a physiologically grounded jitter model whose four parameters map directly to speaker characteristics (age, pathology, effort level), enabling more realistic F0 micro-perturbation than simple random noise, with quasi-periodic spectral components at motor unit firing frequencies (0-50 Hz) that contribute to natural voice quality.

## Titze_1992_VocalIntensity  (glottal-source, amplitude, source-filter, aerodynamics)
Titze and Sundberg derive analytical expressions for radiated acoustic power from the mouth as a function of glottal source waveform parameters, lung pressure, F0, and vocal tract transfer characteristics, combining radiation impedance theory with empirical glottal flow measurements from professional tenors and nonsingers. The key finding is that SPL increases at 8-9 dB per doubling of *excess pressure over threshold* (lung pressure minus phonation threshold pressure), with professional singers achieving 10-12 dB greater intensity than nonsingers at equivalent lung pressures due to 3-4x greater peak airflow derivatives. This paper is directly relevant to Qlatt's intensity control system, providing physics-based relationships between lung pressure, F0, spectral slope, and SPL that can drive loudness modeling, speaker personality differentiation (singer vs nonsinger profiles), and voicing onset thresholds via the phonation threshold pressure concept.

## Titze_2014_BistableVocalFoldAdduction  (laryngeal-source, voice-quality, aerodynamics)
# Titze 2014 - Bi-stable Vocal Fold Adduction

This paper demonstrates that modal-falsetto register transitions arise from a bistability mechanism in the glottal adductory geometry: smooth changes in the convergence angle of the vocal fold medial surfaces produce abrupt changes in intraglottal pressure, creating two stable states (convergent/modal and divergent/falsetto) separated by a narrow, less stable rectangular configuration that corresponds to mixed registration. Using a two-spring model with empirically-derived intraglottal pressures from Scherer et al. (2010), Titze shows that the transition between registers is governed by the pressure ratio pe/ps (supraglottal/subglottal), the vertical stiffness gradient of the vocal folds (controlled by CT and TA muscle balance), and transglottal pressure magnitude, with the critical finding that large transglottal pressure exacerbates bistability rather than facilitating smooth registration, and that achieving stable mixed voice requires balancing upper and lower vocal fold stiffness to maintain a near-rectangular glottal shape.

## Titze_2015_OddHarmonicSensitivity  (glottal-source, speech-synthesis, acoustics)
This letter investigates how the harmonic spectrum of glottal airflow depends on the symmetry of the flow pulse, parameterized by open quotient (Qo) and skewing quotient (Qs). The key finding is that a perfectly symmetric pulse (Qo=0.5, Qs=1.0) eliminates all odd harmonics through phase cancellation, but mild asymmetry (Qo outside 0.45-0.55 or Qs >= 2.0) restores a balanced spectrum with ~-10 dB/octave slope. For Qlatt's LF glottal source, this provides critical parameter constraints to avoid artifacts like pitch doubling and vowel intelligibility loss from odd-harmonic suppression.

## Trott_2022_ProsodyIndirectRequests  (prosody, pragmatics, perception, fundamental-frequency)
This paper investigates whether prosodic features (pitch, duration, intensity) can disambiguate indirect requests from literal statements or questions in English, using both human perception experiments and machine learning classification. The key finding is that humans achieve 55% accuracy and a classifier achieves 65% accuracy using seven acoustic features, with F0 slope and number of voiced frames emerging as significant predictors that interact with grammatical form. While primarily a perception study rather than a synthesis guide, the identified prosodic correlates of speech act intent (rising F0 for questions, longer duration for marked interpretations) could inform prosody generation rules for TTS systems aiming to convey pragmatic distinctions.

## Turk_2000_WordBoundaryDurationPatterns  (duration, word-boundary, prosody)
Systematic evaluation of five proposed word-level duration adjustment mechanisms (word-final lengthening, word-initial lengthening, polysyllabic shortening, accentual lengthening, syllable ratio equalization) using segmentally matched English triads where word boundary location varies while phonemic content stays constant. ## Umeda_1975_VowelDurationAmericanEnglish  (duration, vowels, english, speech-synthesis, prosody)

Large-corpus empirical study deriving multiplicative duration rules for American English vowels from continuous speech readings by three speakers. ## Umeda_1977_ConsonantDurationAmericanEnglish  (duration, phoneme-acoustics, english, stops, fricatives, nasals, prosody, connected-speech)

## vanDinther_2001_PerceptualGlottalPulse  (lf-model, glottal-source, perception, voice-quality)
This paper investigates the perceptual relevance of the three parameters in the LF glottal pulse model by measuring discrimination thresholds for parameter variations and comparing them against excitation pattern distances computed from an auditory model. The key findings show that the LF model effectively operates as a 1-2 parameter system perceptually, with the return phase parameter (Ra) being most audible, followed by asymmetry (Rk), while open phase duration (Ro) has negligible perceptual impact. These results are important for speech synthesis because they demonstrate that voice quality control can be simplified by focusing computational effort on the most perceptually relevant parameter (return phase spectral tilt) rather than trying to independently vary all three LF parameters.

## vanDinther_2004_PerceptualGlottalPulse  (glottal-source, lf-model, perception, voice-quality)
This paper presents a method for quantifying the perceptual relevance of variations in glottal-pulse parameters (specifically LF model R-parameters: return phase ratio, symmetry quotient, and open quotient) using excitation pattern distance (EPD) derived from cochlear modeling. The study establishes that 4.3 dB EPD corresponds to one just-noticeable difference (JND) across diverse listening conditions and provides a quadratic approximation (Q) that enables analytical computation of which parameter directions produce maximum/minimum perceptual sensitivity. These findings are critical for speech synthesis because they reveal that return phase (Ra) has the highest perceptual impact on voice quality, allowing synthesizers to prioritize parameter control and use a universal perceptual threshold to determine whether voice quality variations are audible to listeners.

## vanSanten_1993_SegmentalDuration  (duration, speech-synthesis, tts)
This paper presents a speaker-dependent segmental duration prediction system that uses sums-of-products models to capture complex interactions between linguistic factors (stress, consonant class, position) affecting how long sounds are held in speech. Van Santen develops 42 separate models organized by phonetic category (vowels, intervocalic consonants, non-intervocalic consonants) that achieve 0.93 correlation with observed durations and significantly outperform Klatt-style multiplicative rules in listener preference testing (73% preference, 60% of sentences rated significantly better). This approach directly addresses the limitations of simpler duration rules in speech synthesis by modeling how stress, place of articulation, and phrasal position interact to modulate segment timing in natural speech.

## vanSanten_1994_SegmentalDurationTTS  (duration, speech-synthesis, tts)
This paper presents the definitive sums-of-products framework for predicting segmental duration in text-to-speech synthesis, using a category tree that groups segments into vowels, intervocalic consonants, and consonants in clusters, with separate models for each category that capture factor interactions (pitch accent x syllabic stress, postvocalic consonant class x phrasal position) through log-domain products of factor scales. The system achieves 0.93 correlation with observed durations from 41,558 segments and is perceptually preferred over the previous MITalk-style Klatt duration rules 73% of the time in forced-choice listening tests, while requiring only 32 parameters for vowels and 196 for intervocalic consonants. This work is essential for Qlatt's duration assignment because it provides the mathematical framework, category structure, and factor hierarchy needed to replace simple multiplicative duration rules with interaction-aware models that properly handle stress amplification, phrase-final lengthening, and speaking rate variation.

## vanSanten_1997_ProsodicModeling  (prosody, duration, speech-synthesis, intonation)
This paper identifies three fundamental obstacles to improving prosodic quality in text-to-speech synthesis: component-specific limitations in how synthesis architectures alter prosody, the combinatorial constraints that prevent general statistical models from generalizing to new utterances, and critical under-researched issues in timing and intonation control. Van Santen demonstrates that sums-of-products duration models with directional invariance generalize far better than CART-based approaches, and that the "concatenative assumption" underestimates spectral coarticulation effects across syllable and word boundaries. For rule-based synthesizers like Klatt, the paper establishes that comprehensive prosodic modeling is essential rather than optional, since such systems cannot rely on large speech corpora to cover combinatorial acoustic contexts.

## vanSon_1997_ConsonantReduction  (acoustics, coarticulation, duration, speaking-style)
This paper presents a comprehensive acoustic analysis of how consonants in Dutch are reduced during spontaneous speech compared to formal read speech, examining duration, formant movement, spectral properties, and energy relationships. These findings are directly applicable to speech synthesis for modeling speaking style variation, enabling synthesizers to reduce both vowel and consonant duration proportionally, modulate spectral effort based on stress level, and adjust formant transitions and energy relationships to create more natural casual or fast-speech output.

## VanZant_Berger_2019_VoicePersuades  (perception, prosody, amplitude, speaker-characteristics, speaking-style)

## VaughanJohnston_2024_VocalIntonationPersuasion  (intonation, perception, fundamental-frequency, prosody)

## Veldhuis_1998_EfficientLFAlternative  (glottal-source, voice-quality, speech-synthesis, lf-model)
Presents the Rosenberg++ (R++) glottal pulse model as a computationally efficient alternative to the Liljencrants-Fant (LF) model. The R++ model uses the same T/R specification parameters as LF but replaces the costly iterative nonlinear solve for the alpha generation parameter with a direct closed-form computation of a polynomial shape parameter t_x. A perceptual experiment with isolated vowels confirms the two models produce speech that is essentially indistinguishable, making R++ a practical drop-in replacement for real-time speech synthesizers.

## Vogel_2010_FatigueSpeechAcoustics  (acoustics, fundamental-frequency, duration, speaking-style)
This paper systematically documents acoustic changes in speech during 24 hours of sustained wakefulness in 18 healthy adults, measuring timing, frequency (F0, formants), and spectral energy (alpha ratio) every 2-4 hours using PRAAT analysis. Key findings show that f0 variation increases, F4 variation decreases, speech rate slows on reading tasks (but speeds up on automated tasks as a compensation strategy), and spectral tilt steepens (increased alpha ratio), with peak effects occurring around 22 hours (just before dawn) and partial recovery at sunrise. ## Walton_1994_SpeakerRaceVocalAcoustics  (voice-quality, jitter, shimmer, harmonics-to-noise-ratio, speaker-identification)

## Wendahl_1963_LaryngealAnalogSynthesisHarshness  (voice-quality, jitter, perception, speech-synthesis, fundamental-frequency)
Wendahl uses an electrical laryngeal analog (LADIC) to synthesize sawtooth stimuli with controlled cycle-to-cycle frequency perturbation (jitter) at two median F0 values (100 Hz and 200 Hz), with deviations ranging from 0 to +/-10 Hz. 536 listeners judged roughness via paired comparisons, establishing that even 1 Hz jitter around 100 Hz F0 is perceptible as rough, and that roughness scales monotonically with jitter magnitude. ## White_2014_ProsodicTimingFunction  (duration, prosody, phrasing)
This paper presents a functional framework for prosodic timing in English, arguing that durational variation serves communicative purposes only through localized lengthening effects at domain heads and edges rather than through compensatory shortening or diffuse rhythmic processes. White identifies four primary lengthening effects (word-initial, phrase-final, lexical stress, and phrasal accent), each with a distinct structural locus and perceptual function, and reinterprets apparent polysyllabic shortening as the distributed attenuation of lengthening when multiple segments share prosodic emphasis. For speech synthesis, this framework provides a parsimonious model for duration control that replaces complex compensatory rules with localized lengthening effects positioned to disambiguate prosodic structure for listeners.

## Wiethoff_2008_CerebralEmotionalProsody  (emotional-prosody, prosody, fundamental-frequency, voice-quality, perception)

## Wightman_1992_SegmentalDurationsProsodic  (duration, prosody, phrase-boundaries, boundary-lengthening, tobi)
Wightman et al. (1992) examine segmental lengthening near prosodic phrase boundaries using a large corpus of radio news speech labeled with break indices. ## Xu_2013_VocalAttractivenessBodySizeProjection  (vocal-attractiveness, voice-quality, breathiness, perception, emotion)
This paper tests the body size projection hypothesis for vocal attractiveness, showing through five perception experiments (using both acoustically altered natural speech and VocalTractLab synthetic speech) that female vocal attractiveness increases with higher pitch, wider formant dispersion, and breathiness (all signaling small body size), while male vocal attractiveness increases with lower pitch and narrower formant dispersion (signaling large body size) but also with breathiness, which softens the aggressiveness associated with large body size projection.
