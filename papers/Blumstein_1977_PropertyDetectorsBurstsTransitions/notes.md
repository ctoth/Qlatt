# Property Detectors for Bursts and Transitions in Speech Perception

Blumstein, S. E., Stevens, K. N., & Nigro, G. N. (1977). *The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 61(5), 1301--1313. DOI: 10.1121/1.381433

## Key Concepts

### Integrated vs. Simple Property Detectors

The paper proposes a two-level perceptual model for stop consonant place of articulation:

1. **Integrated property detectors**: Respond to a combination of acoustic attributes (burst + formant transitions) that together characterize the short-term spectrum at consonantal release. These operate on the gross spectral shape at the abrupt amplitude discontinuity.
2. **Simple property detectors**: Respond to individual acoustic attributes such as burst frequency alone or transition direction alone.

The integrated property is the primary cue; simple properties serve as secondary/backup cues when the integrated property is absent or ambiguous.

### Selective Adaptation Paradigm

Uses repeated presentation of an adapting stimulus to fatigue neural property detectors, then measures shifts in phonetic category boundaries on a test continuum.

## Stimulus Parameters (Implementation-Relevant)

### Test Continuum (14 stimuli along [b d g] dimension)

- Duration: 250 ms total (consonant-vowel)
- Five-formant cascade synthesizer, sampling rate 10 kHz, low-pass filtered at 4800 Hz
- Piecewise-linear transitions on first three formants, followed by fixed vowel portion
- Vowel target formants (F1-F5): 720 Hz (BW 50), 1170 Hz (BW 70), 2600 Hz (BW 90)
- F1 transition: 200 Hz starting frequency, 30 ms duration for all stimuli
- F2 transition: 40 ms duration, starting frequencies vary across continuum (see Fig. 2)
- F3 transition: 40 ms duration, starting frequencies vary across continuum (see Fig. 2)
- F0 contour: starts ~120 Hz, falls to ~90 Hz over 300 ms (Fig. 1)

### Burst Parameters

- Brief noise burst, 10 ms, preceding onset of voicing by 15 ms
- Generated by gated 10-ms burst of white noise from random number generator at 10 kHz clock rate
- Filtered through simple-tuned circuit:
  - **[d] burst**: center frequency 3500 Hz (4th formant of following vowel), BW 150 Hz
  - **[g] burst**: center frequency 1640 Hz (2nd formant at vowel onset for GTr stimulus), at onset of voicing
- Burst amplitude: 0-5 dB less than adjacent formant in following vowel (matching real speech measurements)

### Hierarchy of Adaptation Effects

For both [d]- and [g]-transition stimuli:
- **Full-cue** (burst + transitions) produces greatest adaptation
- **Partial-cue** (transitions only) produces next greatest
- **Conflicting-cue** (wrong burst + transitions) produces least/no significant adaptation

This ranking supports the integrated property detector model: when burst and transitions agree, the integrated property is stronger, producing more fatigue.

### Conflicting-Cue Stimuli Results

- DTr conf (14 stimuli) produced no significant boundary shifts
- For the conflicting-cue stimuli, adaptation effects are based on acoustic information in the signal rather than the phonetic label assigned by the subject
- Subjects who labeled DTr conf as [g] (attending to burst) and those who labeled it [d] (attending to transitions) showed different adaptation patterns consistent with their dominant cue

## Theoretical Model

### Two-Stage Auditory Processing

1. **Initial stage**: Auditory system produces amplitude-frequency-time display (analogous to spectrogram)
2. **Second stage**: Selective filters operate on this display; a filter responds maximally when input matches its characteristic property or configuration

### Integrated Properties at Consonantal Release

The short-term acoustic spectrum sampled at the abrupt amplitude discontinuity at consonantal release constitutes an **integrated acoustic property** for place of articulation. This spectrum is shaped by:
- Formant frequencies at release (determined by vocal tract shape)
- Formant transitions (onset directions)
- Burst spectrum (frequency and distribution of burst energy)

These attributes contribute to a single integrated spectral pattern, not processed independently.

### Role of Burst vs. Transitions (Fig. 13)

Schematized spectrograms show how burst and transition interact:

- **Near [b]-[d] boundary**: F2 onset is at a low frequency; [d] burst at 3500 Hz causes effective higher-frequency onset, biasing toward [d]. Without burst, F2 onset alone may be ambiguous.
- **Near [d]-[g] boundary**: F2 onset at higher frequency; the falling F3 transition creates the "velar pinch." Burst energy at 1640 Hz (near F2 onset) reinforces the [g] percept.

The burst modifies the effective onset frequencies of formant transitions by adding spectral energy that shifts the apparent starting point of the formant trajectories.

## Relevance to Synthesis

### For Klatt Synthesizer Implementation

1. **Burst frequency selection**: The paper confirms that burst frequency is place-dependent and interacts with formant transitions. For [d], burst energy near F4 (~3500 Hz); for [g], burst near F2 onset (~1640 Hz for the vowel [a]).
2. **Burst amplitude**: Should be 0-5 dB below the amplitude of the adjacent formant in the following vowel.
3. **Burst duration**: 10 ms, with 15 ms onset before voicing (VOT = 15 ms for these stimuli).
4. **Integrated cue importance**: Getting burst AND transitions correct together matters more than either alone. Conflicting cues weaken the percept substantially.
5. **Vowel-dependent burst placement**: The [g] burst frequency tracks the frequency region of the second formant at onset of voicing, confirming the context-sensitivity of velar burst cues.
6. **Transition parameters**: F2 and F3 starting frequencies and their trajectories toward vowel targets are the primary transition cues. The paper uses 40 ms transition durations for F2 and F3, and 30 ms for F1.

### Connection to Stevens 1978 and Blumstein & Stevens 1979

This paper is a precursor to the acoustic invariance work. The "integrated property" concept here evolves into the "invariant acoustic property" of Blumstein & Stevens (1979), where the gross spectral shape at release is shown to be invariant across vowel contexts:
- Diffuse-falling spectrum for labials
- Diffuse-rising spectrum for alveolars
- Compact (mid-frequency peak) spectrum for velars
