# Sweet Voice: The Role of Voice Quality in a Japanese Feminine Style

**Authors:** Rebecca L. Starr
**Year:** 2015
**Venue:** Language in Society 44, 1–34
**DOI:** 10.1017/S0047404514000724

## One-Sentence Summary
This paper acoustically characterizes "sweet voice" — a Japanese feminine vocal style used by professional voice actresses (seiyuu) — showing it is produced via head register phonation with elevated H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3, and HNR, combined with lower 2k-4k energy, rather than simply high pitch.

## Problem Addressed
The common perception that Japanese feminine voices are simply "high-pitched" conflates voice quality with pitch. Starr argues that the distinctive quality of sweet voice comes from specific phonation type (head register) rather than elevated F0, and that voice quality plays an independent role in constructing gendered vocal styles.

## Key Contributions
- First rigorous acoustic analysis of sweet voice as a distinct vocal style using six measures: H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3, CPP, HNR, and 2k-4k (Actor's Formant proxy)
- Demonstrates that sweet voice F0 (~263 Hz mean) is actually *lower* than anime heroine voices (320–400 Hz), contradicting the "high pitch" stereotype
- Proposes head register phonation as the mechanism: vocal ligaments elongated and tensed (as in singing head voice), producing a light, resonant timbre distinct from both falsetto and modal
- Shows that sweet voice and nonsweet voice from the same speaker differ significantly on H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3, HNR, and 2k-4k, but not CPP
- Links sweet voice to the sociolinguistic concept of "Yamato Nadeshiko" — the ideal traditional Japanese woman

## Methodology
- 10 professional seiyuu performing sweet voice characters in anime, plus 4 of those performing nonsweet characters for intra-speaker comparison
- Acoustic analysis of /a/ vowel tokens using Praat (H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3) and VoiceSauce (CPP, HNR)
- Tokens manually classified into creaky, modal, and breathy phonation types
- Mixed-effects linear regression (Rbrul) controlling for speaker, F0, phonation type, and time point
- Supplementary sociolinguistic analysis of character types and fan discourse

## Key Acoustic Findings

### Six Measures Used
- **H1-H2**: First harmonic minus second harmonic amplitude — correlates with open quotient
- **H1-A1**: First harmonic minus first formant amplitude — correlates with spectral tilt
- **H1-A3**: First harmonic minus third formant amplitude — correlates with spectral tilt
- **CPP**: Cepstral Peak Prominence — periodicity/breathiness measure
- **HNR35**: Harmonics-to-noise ratio (0–3500 Hz range)
- **2k-4k**: Highest spectral peak ~4 kHz minus highest peak ~2 kHz — Actor's Formant proxy (lower = more energy in upper harmonics)

### Overall Trends (Sweet Voice)
- H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3 **higher** for breathy tokens (consistent with breathiness)
- CPP and HNR **lower** for breathy tokens (less periodic)
- Mean F0 across all sweet voice: ~262.7 Hz (excluding exceptionally high TY and SA)

### Intra-Speaker Analysis (Table 3, 4 speakers with sweet + nonsweet)

Sweet voice vs. nonsweet, controlling for speaker:
- **H1-H2**: Higher for sweet (coeff +3.39, p < .0001)
- **H1-A1**: Higher for sweet (coeff +2.84, p < .0001)
- **H1-A3**: Higher for sweet (coeff +1.52, p = .003)
- **HNR35**: Higher for sweet (coeff +1.97, p < .0001)
- **2k-4k**: Lower for sweet (coeff −1.09, p < .0001)
- **CPP**: No significant difference
- **F0**: Small positive correlation with sweet (coeff +0.014 on log F0)

### Key Interpretation: Head Register
- Sweet voice is NOT falsetto (which would show lower 2k-4k or higher HNR)
- Sweet voice is NOT simply breathy (which would show lower HNR)
- The combination of higher H1-based measures + higher HNR + lower 2k-4k points to **head register**: vocal folds elongated and tensed with vocalis muscle relaxed
- Head register produces a resonant, "ringing" quality with enhanced periodicity
- Higher open quotient (H1-H2) combined with enhanced periodicity (HNR) and upper harmonic energy (low 2k-4k) is inconsistent with simple breathiness

### F0 Context
- Mean sweet voice F0: ~263 Hz
- Japanese mothers speaking to adults: ~177 Hz (Masataka 1992)
- Japanese mothers addressing children: 175–252 Hz
- Female university students (Ohara 1999): ~262 Hz (to professors), ~253 Hz (to friends)
- Anime heroines (Teshigawara 2003): 320–400 Hz
- Sweet voice is at the *higher end of normal female range*, not anomalously high
- The perception of "high" pitch may result from spectral tilt making F0 perceived as higher in the speaker's range

## Results Summary
Sweet voice is acoustically characterized by:
1. Higher spectral tilt (H1-H2, H1-A1, H1-A3 all elevated)
2. Enhanced periodicity (higher HNR)
3. Greater upper harmonic energy (lower 2k-4k, i.e., stronger Actor's Formant region)
4. F0 not reliably different from nonsweet voice
5. CPP not reliably different

This pattern is consistent with head register phonation rather than breathiness, falsetto, or simply high pitch.

## Limitations
- Small sample of professional voice actresses only — not generalizable to ordinary Japanese women
- Acoustic data from recordings of varying quality (anime audio tracks)
- Manual perceptual classification of phonation types (creaky/modal/breathy)
- No articulatory data (EGG, ultrasound) to confirm head register hypothesis
- Only /a/ vowels analyzed
- Cultural analysis relies on limited fan discourse data

## Testable Properties
- Sweet voice H1-H2 should be higher than same-speaker nonsweet voice
- Sweet voice HNR should be higher than same-speaker nonsweet voice
- Sweet voice 2k-4k should be lower than same-speaker nonsweet voice
- Sweet voice F0 should NOT be reliably higher than nonsweet from same speaker
- CPP should NOT differ significantly between sweet and nonsweet
- The combination of high H1-based measures + high HNR is distinctive (rules out pure breathiness)

## Relevance to Project
This paper provides acoustic correlates of a specific feminine voice quality style that could inform voice preset design in Klatt synthesis. The finding that voice quality (phonation type, spectral tilt, HNR) is more important than F0 for gendered vocal style aligns with our collection's findings on voice quality parameters (Hanson 1995/1997, Gobl 2003, Burkhardt 2009). The specific parameter shifts (H1-H2 +3.4 dB, H1-A1 +2.8 dB, HNR +2 dB, 2k-4k −1.1 dB) provide quantitative targets if a "sweet voice" preset were ever implemented. More broadly, the paper demonstrates that perceptually salient vocal styles arise from specific phonation configurations rather than simple pitch manipulation.
