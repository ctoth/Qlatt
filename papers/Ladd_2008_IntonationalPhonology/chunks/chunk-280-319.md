## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Designated Terminal Element (DTE)**: The most prominent terminal element at the end of an utterance or relevant subconstituent; in broad focus, the DTE of the largest phrase bears the last pitch accent (p. 263).
- **Primary accent vs. secondary accent**: Nuclear accents are primary; prenuclear accents are secondary. Only primary/nuclear accents directly signal focus (pp. 265-266).
- **Accent without focus**: Prenuclear accents that occur on words in broad focus contexts without those words being separately focused. These are secondary accents distributed by metrical structure, not by focus (p. 265).
- **Focus without accent**: Cases where focus distinctions are conveyed by segmental cues (duration, vowel quality) without any pitch accent, as in postnuclear deaccented prepositional phrases like "for him" (pp. 266-268).
- **Second Occurrence Focus (SOF)**: When a focused word is mentioned a second time in discourse and is completely deaccented, yet subtle phonetic prominence cues may still be present (pp. 267-268).
- **Culminativeness**: The idea that stress is a "culminative" feature -- it singles out one unit as most prominent within a stretch of speech. The metrical view formalizes this as the DTE and "primary accent" (pp. 272-273).
- **Deaccenting as prominence reversal**: Deaccenting can be treated as a reversal of relative strength in a metrical tree, not simply as removal of pitch accent. Both leftward and rightward accent shifts under deaccenting follow from this (pp. 270-271).
- **Prosodic Phrase (ProsP)**: An informal term for chunks of speech delimited by prosodic boundaries, whose identification involves multiple cues (pause, F0 fall/rise, local slowing, pitch change) (p. 288).
- **Strict Layer Hypothesis (SLH)**: The principle that prosodic structure is of fixed depth, with a hierarchy of domain types where no node dominates another of the same type and no levels are skipped (Selkirk 1984). Results in flat prosodic trees (pp. 291-293).
- **Compound Prosodic Domain (CPD)**: Ladd's proposed construct that allows recursive prosodic structure (a domain of type X whose immediate constituents are also type X), analogous to morphological compounding. Preserves the "flatness" intuition while accounting for variable boundary strength (pp. 297-298).
- **Tune-text association**: The principle describing how abstract tonal elements of a contour are aligned with the segmental/syllabic features of an utterance (pp. 281-282).

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Metrical Theory of Sentence Stress (Section 7.2)
Ladd develops a metrical alternative to the "accent-first" Focus-to-Accent (FTA) view:
- Sentence stress is fundamentally a matter of **relative prominence within a metrical structure**, not directly the distribution of pitch accents.
- Focus is signalled by the location of DTEs (not by accents per se), and accents arrange themselves according to metrical demands.
- Pitch accents are one manifestation of metrical structure but not the definition of it.
- The approach accommodates: accent without focus, focus without accent, deaccenting as metrical reversal, and ambiguity between broad/narrow focus in two-word phrases.

### Metrical Structure for Tunes (Section 8.1.2)
Ladd proposes an X-bar-like constituent structure for intonational tunes:
- A tune has a **nucleus** (head), **postnuclear** elements (complement), and optional **prenuclear** elements (specifier/adjunct).
- The nucleus is the central, obligatory element. Postnuclear tones are more closely bound to the nucleus than prenuclear tones.
- Prenuclear accents can appear zero, one, or more times depending on metrical structure of the text. Their number is a predictable surface detail.
- Postnuclear elements are obligatory but may surface as either edge tones or postnuclear accents depending on context.
- The abstract tune is thus "more abstract than a string of tones" -- tonal elements are not intrinsically accent tones or edge tones; their surface realization depends on the metrical structure of the text.

### Compound Prosodic Domains (Section 8.2.4)
- A CPD of type X has immediate constituents that are themselves of type X (recursive structure).
- This allows prosodic structure to have indeterminate depth while preserving the key insight of the SLH that prosodic trees are "flatter" than syntactic trees.
- The categories of the prosodic hierarchy remain strictly ranked even with CPDs.
- CPDs account for graded boundary strength differences without positing new domain types for each observed level.

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

- **Downstepping contour in broad focus**: In utterances like "A friend of mine works for NASA," the F0 contour smoothly declines from the initial peak with no local pitch movements corresponding to perceived medial accents. Transcribers' judgments about medial accent presence are uncertain because prominence is based on duration and other cues, not obvious pitch excursions (pp. 261-262).
- **Tag questions inherit tune from main sentence**: If the main sentence has a high-rising nucleus, the tag continues high; if falling, the tag continues low; if fall-rise, one possible pattern splits the fall and rise across main sentence and tag (p. 287).
- **Boundary strength correlates with F0**: In three-clause sentences (A and B but C / A but B and C), initial peaks of clauses B and C were higher after a "but" boundary than after an "and" boundary, and pauses before "but" were longer -- both F0 and duration cues signal that "but" boundaries are phonetically "stronger" (p. 295).
- **Declination within and across clauses**: Each clause in a three-clause coordinate sentence shows declination, and each clause ends with a clear boundary tone (p. 295).

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

- **Accent types used in examples**: H*, !H*, L+H*, L*+H, H+!H*, L*, and the sequences H*_L_L%, L+H*_L_L%, etc. (pp. 261, 282-283, 300)
- **Emphatic statement contour**: H* L+upstepH* L_L% (or equivalently, raised H* near end) -- "Do it now" / "Her mother's a lawyer" (p. 282)
- **Neutral declarative**: H* ... H* L_L% with downstep on successive H* accents (p. 283)
- **Finality/continuation**: H*_!H*_L_L% vs. H*_!H*_!H*_L_L% -- the number of prenuclear accents varies but the nuclear H*_L_L% sequence is invariant (pp. 283-284)
- **'Surprise-redundancy' / contradiction contour**: L*_H*_L_L% (p. 283)
- **B accent (Bolinger) / secondary H* accent**: A prenuclear accent type that in Pierrehumbert's terms is simply H* without following L phrase accent, used to distinguish epithet from literal readings of words like "butcher" (p. 300)

## Alignment and Timing

- **Tune-text association problem**: The same abstract contour (e.g., H*...L+upstepH*_L_L%) must map to texts with different numbers of syllables. Pierrehumbert's analysis solves this by treating contours as sequences of discrete tonal events at particular points, not holistic shapes (p. 282).
- **Prenuclear accents associate with stressed syllables**: The H* accents in "Do it now" associate with "do" and "now" based on their relative metrical prominence (p. 282).
- **Variable number of prenuclear accents**: When the same tune is applied to "I read it to Julia" vs. "I wanted to read it to Julia," the number of prenuclear accents (H*, H*, or L*) increases, but these count as the same linguistic choice. The categorical identity of the accents (H*, !H*, or L*) represents a single linguistic choice regardless of how many syllables are available (pp. 283-284).

## Cross-linguistic Data

### Korean dephrasing (pp. 278-279)
- In Korean, focus is signalled by prosodic phrasing rather than pitch accent. Given information is "dephrased" (merged into the preceding phrase) rather than deaccented.
- Korean question-answer pairs show that contextually given words lose their separate phrase status in answers, analogous to English deaccenting.
- Object-verb sequences in Korean normally form a single prosodic phrase; splitting them into two phrases gives the verb undue emphasis -- showing that structural phrasing, not accent placement, is the vehicle for focus marking.

### Cross-linguistic comparison of focus expression (pp. 278-280)
- In English and Dutch, focus differences are most saliently signalled by the presence/absence of pitch accents ("deaccenting").
- In Japanese, Korean, and Chichewa (Bantu), focus differences are signalled by the presence/absence of phrase boundaries ("dephrasing").
- Ladd proposes that deaccenting and dephrasing are "different surface symptoms of the same deeper structural effects" -- both involve prosodic restructuring to signal information structure.

### Spanish and Egyptian Arabic vs. Portuguese and English (p. 280)
- Spanish and Egyptian Arabic prefer flat prosodic structures with short intermediate phrases (almost every content word in its own phrase).
- Portuguese (like English) tolerates deep prosodic structure with content words subordinated within larger phrases.
- Portuguese also more readily accepts contextual deaccenting (West Germanic type) than Spanish or Arabic.
- There may be a typological connection between depth tolerance and deaccenting tolerance.

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation Rules
- **Broad focus default**: In broad focus, the last accent in a phrase is the nuclear accent (DTE). F0 implementation should always make the last accent the most prominent in terms of structural position, even if it is not the highest in absolute F0 (due to declination).
- **Downstepping sequences**: In broad focus declaratives, successive prenuclear H* accents are downstepped (!H*). The number of prenuclear accents depends on how many metrically strong syllables precede the nucleus. The contour may appear as a smooth decline without obvious local pitch peaks for medial accents (Figure 7.1).
- **Deaccenting implementation**: When a word is deaccented (given information), the metrical tree reverses -- the previously weak node becomes strong. In F0 terms, the accent shifts to the other word in the pair. This is not just accent removal but accent reassignment.

### Phrasing Rules
- **Intermediate phrase boundary insertion**: Sentence length affects phrasing. Short sentences (subject + predicate) form a single intermediate phrase with one nuclear accent (typically on the predicate in English). Long sentences split subject and predicate into separate intermediate phrases, each with its own nuclear accent.
- **Boundary strength is gradient**: F0 reset height and pause duration both correlate with perceived boundary strength. Stronger boundaries show higher F0 reset and longer pauses. This suggests that boundary strength should be a continuous parameter in TTS, not just binary present/absent.
- **Compound Prosodic Domains**: For TTS, the CPD framework suggests that phrase boundaries of the same structural type (e.g., IP) can vary in phonetic strength. A weaker IP boundary (within a CPD) would have less F0 reset and shorter pause than a stronger one.

### Accent Placement Strategies
- **Nuclear accent = DTE of the phrase**: The nuclear accent falls on the DTE, determined by metrical structure. In English, broad focus places the DTE at the rightmost content word of the phrase.
- **Prenuclear accents are optional/variable**: Their number depends on the number of available metrically strong syllables between the phrase onset and the nuclear accent. The same tune identity is preserved regardless of how many prenuclear accents surface.
- **Focus-driven phrasing**: Narrow focus on a prenuclear word can promote it to nuclear status by giving it its own intermediate phrase. Double focus creates two intermediate phrases with two nuclear accents.
- **Arguments vs. predicates**: In English intransitive sentences, arguments (subjects) tend to be more "accentable" than predicates (verbs), so short intransitives may have the nuclear accent on the subject. In longer sentences, the predicate gets its own phrase and thereby its own nuclear accent.

### Tag Question Intonation
- Tags inherit their tonal contour from the main sentence's nuclear tone. Implementation should carry the "tail" of the main sentence's tune onto the tag, with the tag's prominent syllable bearing whatever postnuclear tonal material follows the nuclear accent of the main sentence (p. 287).
