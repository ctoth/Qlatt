# Horizontal Directivity of Low- and High-Frequency Energy in Speech and Singing

## One-Sentence Summary
Simultaneous multi-channel horizontal-plane recordings of 15 speakers reveal that high-frequency energy (HFE) in speech and singing is highly directional, with voiceless fricatives /s/ and /sh/ showing the greatest directivity differences (up to 9 dB at angles beyond 90 degrees in the 8 kHz octave band), while gender, production level, and production mode (speech vs singing) have only minor effects.

## Problem Addressed
Previous speech directivity studies relied on rotation/repetition methods that introduced error due to subject variation between repetitions, used low spatial resolution, seated subjects, and generally did not examine high-frequency energy (above 4-8 kHz) with adequate accuracy.

## Key Contributions
- First simultaneous multi-channel full-bandwidth directivity measurements of speech and singing in the horizontal plane with high spatial resolution (15 degree intervals, 13 microphones)
- Demonstrates that HFE (8 and 16 kHz octave bands) is highly directional compared to lower frequencies
- Shows voiceless fricatives have large, phoneme-specific directivity differences: /s, sh/ far more directional than /f, h/
- Establishes that gender, production level, and speech vs singing mode have minimal effect on directivity patterns (differences generally < 3 dB)
- Provides complete Table I with octave-band SPL data at all angles for all conditions

## Methodology
- 15 subjects (8 female, 7 male) standing in an anechoic chamber
- 13 microphones in horizontal plane at 15-degree intervals (0 to 180 degrees, exploiting left-right symmetry)
- Microphones at 60 cm from mouth, values extrapolated to 1 m using inverse square law
- Simultaneous recording at 44.1 kHz, 24 bits
- Conditions: normal speech (read passage + isolated fricatives), soft speech, loud speech, normal singing
- Analysis: octave-band SPL (125 Hz to 16 kHz), directivity index (DI), repeated-measures ANOVA

## Key Results

### Overall Speech Directivity
- Normal speech at 0 degrees: 62.0 dB SPL overall (at 1 m)
- Nearly omnidirectional below 500 Hz (< 3 dB variation across angles)
- Increasing directionality with frequency: ~7 dB front-to-back difference at 1 kHz, ~13 dB at 2 kHz, ~19 dB at 4 kHz, ~26 dB at 8 kHz, ~27 dB at 16 kHz
- Mean directivity index (DI) for normal speech: 2.1 dB

### Gender Effects
- No significant gender differences below 8 kHz octave
- Male speech slightly more directional than female in 8 and 16 kHz octaves (< 3 dB difference)
- Male DI: 1.9 dB; Female DI: 2.3 dB (not significantly different)

### Production Level Effects
- Louder speech slightly more directional, most pronounced at 16 kHz
- Mean DI: soft 1.9 dB, normal 2.1 dB, loud 2.7 dB (highly significant, F(2,28)=23.629, p<0.001)
- Differences mostly < 3 dB except at extreme rear angles in 16 kHz octave

### Speech vs Singing
- Virtually no difference in directivity (DI = 2.1 dB for both)
- All differences within 1 dB across frequency bands

### Fricative Directivity (Major Finding)
- /s/: DI = 5.3 dB — energy concentrated in 8 kHz octave, highly directional
- /sh/: DI = 4.6 dB — energy concentrated in 4 kHz octave
- /f/: DI = 3.2 dB — broadband, less directional
- /h/: DI = 2.5 dB — most broadband, least directional
- Differences highly significant: F(2,28)=71.301, p<0.001
- Largest differences in 8 kHz octave: /s/ vs /h/ approaching 9 dB at angles > 90 degrees
- /f/ and /h/ not significantly different from each other

## Implementation Details

### Implications for Klatt Synthesizer
1. **Radiation filter**: The frequency-dependent directivity data constrains the high-frequency radiation characteristic. For on-axis (0 degree) listening, the full spectral content is present. Off-axis listening would require attenuation of HFE by the amounts in Table I.

2. **Fricative amplitude scaling by phoneme**: The large directivity differences between fricatives confirm that /s/ and /sh/ energy is concentrated in narrow high-frequency bands (8 kHz and 4 kHz respectively), while /f/ and /h/ are more broadband. This supports different AF amplitude contours for different fricative places of articulation.

3. **Production-level scaling**: Louder speech becomes slightly more directional at high frequencies. If Qlatt models vocal effort, HFE should increase slightly more than proportionally with effort level.

4. **Recording angle recommendation**: HFE decreases by at least 3 dB when recording angle exceeds 30 degrees. Reference measurements for synthesis targets should be from recordings at 0-30 degrees.

## Limitations
- Horizontal plane only (no vertical directivity data)
- 15-degree spatial resolution may miss fine directivity features
- Left-right symmetry assumed (13 mics covering 0-180 degrees)
- Standing subjects only
- Only 4 voiceless fricatives examined (no voiced fricatives, no vowel-specific directivity)
- Limited to English speakers

## Testable Properties
- At 0 degrees, normal speech overall level should be approximately 62 dB SPL at 1 m (for calibrated output)
- Front-to-back (0 vs 180 degrees) attenuation should increase monotonically with frequency
- Fricative /s/ should show greater front-to-back attenuation than /f/ at 4 kHz and above
- Below 500 Hz, directivity should be < 3 dB across all angles
- Production level should not significantly affect directivity below 1 kHz

## Relevance to Project
This paper provides empirical directivity data relevant to understanding how speech energy is distributed spatially, particularly for high-frequency components. For Qlatt, the primary value is:
- Confirming that fricative spectral differences (/s/ energy at 8 kHz vs /sh/ at 4 kHz vs /f/ broadband) are robust across measurement angles, supporting the current AF/AH amplitude differentiation in the fricative rules
- Providing quantitative attenuation data if directivity-aware synthesis is ever implemented (e.g., for spatial audio applications)
- The finding that speech and singing have identical directivity validates using speech-derived acoustic targets for singing synthesis
