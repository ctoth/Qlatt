# The American Four-Level Analysis of Intonation Contours: Historical Postscript

**Authors:** D. Robert Ladd
**Year:** 2014
**Venue:** *Historiographia Linguistica* 42.1
**DOI/URL:** Preprint; published in HL 42.1 (2014)

## One-Sentence Summary
This paper establishes the historical priority of Kenneth Lee Pike (not Rulon Wells) for the American four-level pitch analysis of English intonation, based on publication records and 1989 correspondence with Pike himself.

## Problem Addressed
The four-level analysis of English intonation (Low, Mid, High, Overhigh pitch phonemes) was widely used in American structuralist linguistics from the 1940s-1960s, but credit was inconsistently assigned between Kenneth Lee Pike (1912-2000) and Rulon S. Wells (1919-2008). The standard account, following Trager & Smith (1957), credits Wells or treats Pike's contribution as derivative. This paper corrects the historical record.

## Key Contributions
- Demonstrates through publication chronology that Pike's intonation analysis was developed as early as 1942 in English-language teaching materials at the University of Michigan, predating Wells's 1945 paper in *Language*
- Publishes previously unpublished 1989 correspondence between Ladd and Pike, in which Pike describes his development of the four-level system and its relationship to his earlier work on tone languages
- Documents how Bloomfieldian orthodoxy (particularly Bernard Bloch and George L. Trager) marginalized Pike's contributions due to intellectual gatekeeping and Pike's applied/missionary orientation
- Shows that Trager (1964) unambiguously credits Wells and treats Pike's work as derivative, while Bolinger (1972) clearly credits Pike — the discrepancy traces to Trager & Smith's ambiguous 1957 phrasing
- Reveals that Pike anticipated key aspects of Pierrehumbert's (1980) autosegmental approach, including the distinction between local contrastive contours and global pitch trends

## Methodology
Historical/archival analysis combining:
1. Bibliographic investigation of publication dates (Pike 1942 teaching materials, Pike 1945 monograph, Wells 1945 paper)
2. Analysis of secondary literature attribution patterns (1947-1972)
3. Previously unpublished primary source: Ladd-Pike correspondence from February-March 1989
4. Contextual analysis of Bloomfieldian institutional politics and their effect on credit assignment

## Implementation Details

### Pike's Four-Level Pitch System (from his 1942/1945 work)
- **Four contrastive pitch levels**: extra-high, high, mid, low (numbered 1-4 with 1=Low in Pike's convention; reversed to 1=Low, 4=Overhigh in Wells/Trager & Smith convention)
- **Contrastive contours**: sets of substitutable intonation patterns at specific positions in utterances
- Contours are "locally replaceable parts" — substitutable at particular positions in longer sequences, analogous to tone frames in tone languages
- Pike explicitly distinguished between local contrastive contours and global sentence pitch declination (p. 34 of his 1942 materials, as described in the letter)
- Pike's system included pre-peg (pre-nucleus) material: stressed syllable followed by part of a syllable or several unstressed syllables
- Complex Intonation Patterns built from simple contours (pp. 72-73 of Pike 1945)

### Relationship to Later Frameworks
- Pike's concept of "intonation frames" (places where a word maintains constant pitch/meaning while contours vary) directly anticipates the autosegmental-metrical concept of pitch accents
- Pike's insistence on the distinction between local contrastive contour sets and global pitch trends anticipates the separation of phrasal (H-, L-) and boundary (H%, L%) tones in Pierrehumbert 1980
- Ladd notes (footnote 3, p. 11) that Pike's discussion of how contours can occur at various positions in a sentence while the general sentence pitch drops "clearly anticipated Pierrehumbert 1980"

### The Levels-vs-Configurations Controversy
- The four-level system was challenged by Bolinger (1951) who argued for "configurations" (pitch movements/contours) rather than discrete levels
- This debate dominated intonation theory from 1951-1980
- Resolved by Pierrehumbert (1980) who posited a two-level system (H, L) with pitch accents as combinations

## Results Summary
- Pike's teaching materials from 1942 already contained the four-level analysis
- Pike's monograph *The Intonation of American English* (copyright 1945, possibly printed 1946) contains the full theoretical treatment
- Wells's paper "The Pitch Phonemes of English" appeared in *Language* March 1945
- Trager & Smith (1957) adopted Wells's numbering convention (1=Low to 4=Overhigh), opposite to Pike's
- Wells's 1947a review of Pike and 1947b "Immediate Constituents" paper use Pike's convention, suggesting Wells was following Pike
- Pike felt underappreciated due to the "intellectual climate" of Bloomfieldian linguistics

## Limitations
- Based partly on Pike's recollections from 1989, nearly 50 years after the events
- Wells did not respond to the inquiry; his perspective is absent
- The paper is primarily historiographic rather than theoretical — it corrects the record but does not evaluate which analysis was better
- The question of whether Pike and Wells arrived at the idea independently remains unresolved

## Relevance to Project
This paper provides essential historical context for the intonation frameworks that underlie Qlatt's prosody system. Specifically:
- It documents the intellectual lineage from Pike (1945) through Trager & Smith (1957) to Pierrehumbert (1980) to ToBI — the same lineage that Qlatt's F0 rules implement
- Pike's concept of "locally replaceable contour sets" maps directly onto how Qlatt's prosody rules define pitch accent patterns as substitutable F0 target sequences
- The paper clarifies that the idea of separating local pitch events from global declination trends (which Qlatt implements via separate rule phases) was present from the very beginning of the American tradition, not just from Pierrehumbert 1980
- Understanding this history helps explain design choices in Ladd (2008) *Intonational Phonology*, which is a primary reference for Qlatt's prosody model
