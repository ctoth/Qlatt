# The Perceptual Significance of High-Frequency Energy in the Human Voice

## One-Sentence Summary
This review establishes that high-frequency energy (HFE, defined as the 8- and 16-kHz octave bands, i.e., 5.7-22 kHz) in speech and singing is perceptually significant for quality, naturalness, localization, intelligibility, and talker recognition, contrary to longstanding assumptions that frequencies above ~5 kHz are unimportant.

## Problem Addressed
Speech research has historically neglected frequencies above ~5 kHz due to telephone bandwidth limitations, early articulation index findings (95% accuracy at 4 kHz low-pass), and technological constraints. This review synthesizes the growing body of evidence that HFE carries perceptually meaningful information.

## Key Contributions
- Comprehensive review of acoustical analysis, generation mechanisms, and perceptual significance of HFE
- Establishes that HFE affects quality, naturalness, localization, intelligibility, and talker identification
- Documents spectral peak locations for fricatives in the HFE range
- Shows that HFE is 15-51 dB below maximum low-frequency energy levels
- Demonstrates children use HFE for word learning

## HFE Definition
- HFE = energy in the **8-kHz and 16-kHz octave bands** (5.7-22 kHz)
- Some studies use broader definitions (everything above 2-5 kHz)
- This paper uses center frequencies 5.6-22 kHz for LTAS band analysis

### Overall HFE Levels (Table 1 - scaled to 65 dB SPL overall)

| Study | HFE Band | Male | Female |
|-------|----------|------|--------|
| Sivian (1929) | 5.6-12 kHz | ~13 dB | ~21 dB |
| Dunn and White (1940) | 5.6-12 kHz | ~24 dB | ~30 dB |
| Byrne et al. (1994) | 5.7-18 kHz | 43.7 dB | 46.3 dB |
| Moore et al. (2008) | 5.7-18 kHz | | 45.2 dB |
| Monson et al. (2012a) | 5.7-22 kHz | 48.2 dB | 51 dB |

**Key finding:** HFE bands are **30-45 dB down** from bands with maximum energy. HFE levels are generally **higher for females** than males (2-6 dB difference).

### HFE Level vs. Production Level
- HFE level increases significantly for both speech and singing as production level increases (soft to normal to loud)
- Loud speech exhibits the highest HFE levels
- Mean HFE level (combined 8- and 16-kHz octaves) in normal speech: **47 dB SPL** (15 dB down from overall 62 dB SPL mean)
- Moore et al. (2008) data: HFE about **45 dB SPL** (scaled to 65 dB overall)

### Gender Differences in HFE
- Significant gender differences found only for the **16-kHz octave** (higher for females)
- The **8-kHz octave** shows no significant gender difference
- Gender differences in HFE level are 2-3 dB across languages (Byrne et al., 1994)

## Fricative Spectral Peaks in HFE Range

### Key Data from Hughes and Halle (1956)
- Three classes of fricatives (labial, dental, palatal) are "quite consistent" within subject
- **/s/ and /z/**: spectral peaks between **3 and 8 kHz** -- consistently higher in frequency than peaks for /sh/ and /zh/
- **/sh/ and /zh/**: spectral peaks between **1 and 3 kHz**
- Peaks for /f/ and /v/: around **8-9 kHz**

### Key Data from Jongman et al. (2000)
- Spectral peak locations for voiceless fricatives (from the paper's review):
  - **/f/**: spectral peaks around **8 kHz** (higher than voiced counterpart)
  - **/theta/ (/th/)**: spectral peaks around **7.5 kHz** (voiceless) and **7.2 kHz** (voiced, from males)
  - **/s/**: spectral peaks around **7 kHz** (context-dependent, can shift to 5.5-7.5 kHz with lip rounding)
  - **/sh/**: spectral peak around **4 kHz**
  - Voiced counterparts: /v/ at **7.5 kHz**, /dh/ at **7.2 kHz**

### Key Data from Shadle and Scully (1995)
- Fricative /s/ spectral peak is **context-dependent** -- lip rounding shifts the peak
  - /s/ in /pasa/: peak around **5.5 kHz**
  - /s/ in /pusu/: peak shifts to **7.5 kHz** (lip rounding effect)
- Major spectral differences in differing contexts were **above 5 kHz**

### Key Data from Monson et al. (2012a)
- Voiceless fricatives exhibit HFE octave and third-octave level differences that distinguish phonemes
- Only significant differences between /f/ and /theta/ found in the **8- and 10-kHz 1/3 octave bands**

### Maniwa et al. (2009)
- Confirmed that voiceless fricatives exhibit HFE octave and third-octave level differences for classification
- Number of acoustic parameters used to classify fricatives extended beyond earlier work

### Summary of Fricative HFE Spectral Peaks

## Voice/Singing Voice HFE

### Shoji et al. (1991) - Isolated Voice HFE
- Spectral peaks found above 5 kHz for all subjects
- Spectral dip around **5 kHz** (caused by antiresonance of piriform fossa -- Dang and Honda, 1997)
- Higher relative levels of HFE spectral peaks for vowels **/a/, /e/, /i/** compared to **/o/ and /u/**
- Gender had significant effect on both frequency and level of HFE spectral peaks (higher for females)

### Singing Voice
- Fry and Manen (1957): "aggressive," "joyful," "fearful" moods affect HFE (4-8 kHz range)
- Titze and Sung (2003): consistent peak near **10 kHz** in trained tenors (second "singer's formant" from epilaryngeal tube resonance)
- Ternstrom (2008): harmonic energy up to **20 kHz** in singing; nasal /n/ has strong fundamental but very little higher energy

### Generation Mechanism
- Isshiki et al. (1978): spectrogram data showing turbulent flow noise up to **8 kHz**
- Zhang et al. (2004): broadband flow noise through physical glottis model, spectral data out to **13 kHz** for normal, convergent, and divergent orifices
- Narayanan and Alwan (2000): computational modeling of flow noise for fricatives out to **10 kHz** using MRI data

## Perceptual Significance

### Quality
- Listeners use sound quality terms to describe HFE changes above 5 kHz (Olson 1947, Monson et al. 2011, 2014)
- Spectral level of 6-8 kHz band correlates with perceived "breathiness" (Hammarberg et al., 1980)
- Breathy voices: steep decrease in spectral level from 0-2 kHz to 2-5 kHz bands; 5-8 kHz band nearly same level as 2-5 kHz
- "Vocal fry/creaky" voices: correlated with steep decrease from 2-5 kHz to 5-8 kHz band

### Naturalness
- **Largest naturalness decrease** occurred when upper cutoff frequency ($f_u$) dropped from **10.9 to 7 kHz** (Moore and Tan, 2003)
  - Mean naturalness score dropped from ~8 to ~5.5 (on 1-10 scale)
  - Further large decrease at step to **5.6 kHz** (score ~3.5)
- Naturalness was also affected (but less dramatically) by lower cutoff ($f_l$) changes
- Low-pass filtering at 7.5 kHz vs 10 kHz: listeners significantly preferred 10 kHz (Fullgrabe et al., 2010)
- Both male and female speech preferred with HFE included

### Localization
- Front-back errors increase significantly when speech is low-pass filtered at **8 kHz** (Best et al., 2005)
- Decreasing HFE level in 20-dB steps: systematic decreases in elevation localization accuracy (Bronkhorst, 1995)

### Intelligibility
- Lippmann (1996): consonant identification maintained from 92% (at 3.15 kHz) to **75% at 8 kHz** to 53% at 10 kHz
  - Sharp accuracy decrease from 8 to 10 kHz suggests the **2-kHz band (8-10 kHz)** carries useful consonant information
  - At 10 kHz low-pass only: 53% correct; at 800 Hz low-pass only: 44% correct
  - Female speech more important for HFE intelligibility
- Apoux and Bacon (2004): listeners (n=6) gave **highest weighting** to HFE band (3.5-10 kHz) for consonant identification in spectrally degraded CV syllables
- 8-kHz octave amplitude modulation most predictive of overall intelligibility (LeGendre et al., 2009)
- Moore et al. (2010): small but significant intelligibility benefit from extending cutoff from 5 to 7.5 kHz when target and background noise are spatially separated

### Talker Recognition
- Schwartz (1968): gender discrimination from fricatives -- 93% for /s/, 90% for /sh/, 74% for /f/, 69% for /theta/
  - /s/ and /sh/ show gender spectral peak differences in HFE region
  - /f/ and /theta/ broadband nature makes gender discrimination harder
- 5 kHz spectral peak distinguishes young boys from young girls in singing (White, 2001)
- Speaker recognition rates improve from 88% at 6 kHz to 96% at 16 kHz bandwidth (Hayakawa and Itakura, 1994)

## Implementation Details

### Implications for Klatt A7-A10 Parallel Formant Amplitudes

The paper does not provide direct amplitude measurements suitable for setting Klatt A7-A10 targets. However, it provides strong constraints:

1. **Overall HFE level**: HFE is 15-17 dB below overall speech level, and 30-45 dB below the band with maximum energy. For a Klatt synthesizer with typical output levels, this constrains the aggregate energy contributed by F7-F10.

2. **Fricative-specific energy**: Fricatives (especially /f/, /theta/, /s/) have their primary spectral peaks in the 5-9 kHz range, which is exactly the F7-F8 region (assuming F7~7 kHz, F8~8 kHz). These peaks need to be relatively prominent during frication.

3. **Vowel HFE**: Vowels /a/, /e/, /i/ have more HFE than /o/, /u/. This suggests A7-A10 should not be zero during vowels, but should vary by vowel quality.

4. **5 kHz dip**: The piriform fossa creates a spectral dip around 5 kHz (Dang and Honda, 1997). This corresponds to the region between F5/F6 and F7. An antiresonance or amplitude reduction near 5 kHz would be acoustically realistic.

5. **Naturalness threshold**: The critical perceptual range for naturalness is 7-10.9 kHz. This means F7 (~7 kHz) and F8 (~8 kHz) are most important for perceived quality; F9 and F10 contribute less.

6. **Gender differences**: Female HFE is 2-6 dB higher than male. A7-A10 should be correspondingly higher for female voice presets.

### Suggested A7-A10 Strategy
- **A7** (at ~7 kHz): Most perceptually important; set to significant level during fricatives, moderate during vowels
- **A8** (at ~8 kHz): Important for /f/, /theta/ peaks and naturalness; set similarly to A7
- **A9** (at ~9 kHz): Less perceptually critical; begin rolloff
- **A10** (at ~10 kHz): Marginal perceptual importance; low amplitude
- All should be **higher for female voices** by 2-4 dB
- During voiceless fricatives /f/ and /theta/: A7 and A8 should be relatively prominent (these are the primary spectral peaks)
- During /s/: A7 dominant, A8 secondary
- During /sh/: A7-A10 all low (peak is around 4 kHz, below F7)

## Results Summary
HFE (5.7-22 kHz) plays a more significant role in speech perception than historically believed. It affects quality judgments, naturalness, front/back localization, consonant intelligibility (especially for fricatives), and talker/gender recognition. The 7-10.9 kHz range is the most critical perceptual region. Fricatives /f/, /theta/, and /s/ have their primary spectral energy in the 5.5-9 kHz range, making them the phonemes most dependent on accurate HFE representation.

## Limitations
- This is a review paper, not original experimental research
- Does not provide direct amplitude measurements suitable for synthesizer parameter setting
- Most cited studies used LTAS (long-term average spectra) rather than phoneme-specific measurements
- Recording methodology varies significantly across studies (distance, angle, microphone placement), affecting absolute HFE levels
- Definition of "HFE" varies across studies (some start at 2 kHz, others at 5 or 8 kHz)

## Testable Properties
- HFE energy should be 15-17 dB below overall speech level in synthesized output
- Fricatives /f/ and /theta/ should have peak spectral energy near 7-9 kHz
- /s/ peak should be higher in frequency than /sh/ peak
- Low-pass filtering synthesized speech at 8 kHz should noticeably degrade naturalness
- Female voice presets should have 2-4 dB more HFE than male presets
- Vowels /a/, /e/, /i/ should have more HFE than /o/, /u/

## Relevance to Project
Directly relevant to Qlatt's recently added F7-F10 cascade formants. The paper establishes the perceptual importance of the 7-10.9 kHz range for naturalness and provides fricative spectral peak data that constrains A7-A10 amplitude targets in the parallel branch. The findings that /f/ and /theta/ peak near 7-9 kHz and that HFE is 15-17 dB below overall level provide concrete design constraints.
