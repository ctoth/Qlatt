# Paraverbal Indicators of Deception: A Meta-Analytic Synthesis

**Authors:** Siegfried Ludwig Sporer, Barbara Schwandt
**Year:** 2006
**Venue:** Applied Cognitive Psychology, 20: 421-446
**DOI:** 10.1002/acp.1190

## One-Sentence Summary
This meta-analysis quantifies the effect sizes of nine paraverbal speech behaviors (pitch, speech rate, message duration, pauses, response latency, speech errors, repetitions, number of words) associated with deception across 41 studies, finding that only pitch and response latency are reliably associated with lying in weighted analyses.

## Problem Addressed
Previous meta-analyses (Zuckerman & Driver 1985, DePaulo et al. 2003) produced inconsistent or contradictory findings about which paraverbal cues reliably indicate deception. This paper attempts to resolve these inconsistencies by analyzing moderator variables (content, preparation, motivation, sanctioning, experimental design) that may explain the heterogeneity.

## Key Contributions
- Comprehensive meta-analysis of 41 studies with 121 effect sizes across 9 paraverbal variables
- Demonstrates that most effect sizes are very small (Cohen's d < 0.2), questioning practical utility
- Identifies moderator variables that explain heterogeneous results: preparation, motivation, sanctioning, lie content
- Proposes a working memory model of lie production as the best theoretical framework
- Shows that pitch increase with deception is reliable only when measured instrumentally, not by human raters

## Methodology
- Literature search of SSCI (1981-2000) yielding 41 studies with sufficient statistical data
- Effect sizes calculated as Pearson's r using Rosenthal (1991) formulas
- Both unweighted and weighted (Hedges & Olkin 1985; Schmidt-Hunter) analyses conducted
- Moderator analyses via blocking/classification (Mullen, 1989)
- Funnel plots inspected for publication bias

## Key Equations

Effect size conversion:
$$r = \frac{t}{\sqrt{t^2 + df}}$$

Cohen's d from r:
$$d = \frac{2r}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}$$

## Implementation Details

This paper provides no algorithms or synthesis procedures. The data is purely statistical meta-analytic results about speech behavior during deception.

### Theoretical Predictions (Table 1)

Four theoretical approaches predict different paraverbal changes when lying:

1. **Arousal approach**: Pitch increases (>), speech errors increase (>), filled pauses increase (>)
2. **Emotion/Affect**: Fear → pitch increase; Guilt → pitch decrease (conflicting)
3. **Attempted control**: Message duration decreases (<), speech rate decreases (<), unfilled pauses decrease (<)
4. **Cognitive load/Working memory**: Message duration decreases (<), number of words decreases (<), response latency increases (>), speech errors increase (>)

## Results Summary
- Of 9 paraverbal behaviors, only **pitch** (r=.101) and **response latency** (r=.106) reliably associated with deception in weighted analyses
- All effect sizes are very small (Cohen's d < 0.3), questioning practical detectability
- Moderators dramatically change results:
  - Pitch increases only with high motivation (r=.529) and short preparation (r=.194)
  - Response latency increases only with short preparation (r=.190)
  - Unsanctioned lies produce stronger effects across multiple variables
  - Lying about feelings+facts shows pitch and latency effects; facts-only does not
- The working memory model best predicts observed patterns when preparation is limited

## Limitations
- Small number of studies for some variables (pitch: k=7, repetitions: k=4)
- Heterogeneity significant for most variables, making overall effect sizes unreliable
- Moderator categories often had to be collapsed due to small cell sizes
- No studies with very long preparation times
- Operationalization of variables differs across studies (duration vs. frequency measurement)
- Publication bias possible though funnel plots suggest minimal impact

## Testable Properties
- Pitch should increase more during high-motivation deception than low-motivation (r=.529 vs .012)
- Response latency should increase with deception only under short preparation (r=.190 vs -.044)
- Message duration should decrease under high motivation deception (r=-.102)
- Effect sizes for paraverbal deception cues should be small (|d| < 0.3)
- Unsanctioned lies should produce larger paraverbal effects than sanctioned lies

## Relevance to Project
**Marginal.** This paper quantifies how speech parameters (pitch, rate, pauses, duration) shift during deception. For Qlatt's voice quality synthesis work, the key takeaways are:
1. **Pitch increase under stress/deception is real but tiny** (r=.101 overall, larger r=.529 under high motivation)
2. **Response latency increases** — relevant if modeling hesitation/disfluency
3. **Speech rate and pause effects are unreliable** — context-dependent
4. If implementing "deceptive speech style" synthesis, the dominant acoustic changes would be pitch elevation and longer response latency, not speech rate or error changes
