# Abstract

Linguists often divide stop consonants into "voiced" and "voiceless" categories, with some languages additionally using aspiration to yield two, three, or four stop categories. This study examines the acoustic feature of voice onset time (VOT) — the time interval between the release burst and the onset of glottal periodicity — as a unifying measure for distinguishing stop categories across eleven languages: American English, Dutch, Puerto Rican Spanish, Hungarian, Tamil, Cantonese, Eastern Armenian, Thai, Korean, Hindi, and Marathi. Spectrographic measurements of stops in both isolated words and sentences show that VOT effectively separates stop categories in most languages, with values clustering into three cross-linguistic modes centered at approximately -100, +10, and +75 milliseconds. The paper discusses the physiological basis for these patterns in terms of glottal mechanisms and proposes perception experiments to test whether the category boundaries along the VOT dimension are perceptually relevant.

## Interpretation

This is the paper that established VOT as the standard acoustic measure for stop consonant voicing contrasts. For a Klatt synthesizer, the key implementation insight is that the voicing distinction maps directly to the timing relationship between the burst (AF/AH onset) and voicing onset (AV onset). The three-mode structure (lead, short lag, long lag) provides the target values for any language's stop system. The English data is particularly relevant: /p t k/ have VOT of approximately 58/70/80 ms respectively (place-dependent aspiration lag), while /b d g/ cluster near 0-10 ms short lag in most speakers. The place-of-articulation effect on VOT (velar > alveolar > labial) is consistent and should be encoded in duration/timing rules.
