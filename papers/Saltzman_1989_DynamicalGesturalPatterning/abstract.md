# Abstract

## Original Abstract

The focus of this article is on a computational modeling approach to speech production that highlights the dynamical nature of articulatory movement patterning. It is argued that the basic phonological units are gestures defined as dynamically specified units of articulatory action. Articulatory gestures are modeled using a mass-spring point-attractor dynamical system (a "gestural dynamical system") that generates movement of a given vocal tract variable toward a target position. The relative timing of articulatory gestures is described in terms of gestural activation and the "gestural score," a specification of the durations and mutual phasing of the set of gestures that an utterance comprises. Utterance kinematics are determined by the time-varying values of the gestural score, via a set of dynamical interactions among the various tract-variable and model-articulator components of the vocal tract. It is shown that the model is able to account for a wide range of contextual articulatory variability (coproduction), and an approach is outlined for extending the account to serial dynamics (the sequencing of gestures in connected speech). This approach to serial dynamics involves the embedding of the gestural dynamical system within a larger-scale dynamical framework in which the gestural score is modulated and "played" by a task-dependent neural network architecture. Suggestions are also made concerning the dynamical implementation of the phonological functions of intergestural cohesion, organizational dominance, and the control of speaking rate.

## Interpretation

The paper establishes that speech is not a sequence of static phoneme targets but a continuously evolving dynamical system where overlapping gestural activations produce the smooth, coarticulated movements observed in natural speech. The core computational model uses critically damped second-order dynamics (mass-spring systems) for individual gestures, parameter blending for simultaneously active gestures on the same tract variable, and a gated Jacobian pseudoinverse for the many-to-many mapping between abstract tract variables and physical articulators. The approach is significant because it derives coarticulatory patterns from first principles (gesture overlap + blending) rather than requiring enumerated context-dependent rules, providing a compact generative model that scales to arbitrary utterances. For Qlatt, the key takeaways are: (1) the gestural score maps conceptually to the parameter track, (2) coarticulation = temporal overlap of rectangular gesture activations + parameter blending, and (3) speaking rate variation is primarily overlap modulation rather than uniform time compression.
