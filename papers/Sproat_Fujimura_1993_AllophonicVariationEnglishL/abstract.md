# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

English /l/ has traditionally been classified into at least two allophones, namely light, which typically occurs syllable initially, and dark, which typically occurs syllable finally. Arguments have been advanced claiming that the light and dark allophones, rather than being ordinary /l/s that provide phonological boundaries may be phonologically distinct by two categories; most researchers have tended to assume that the allophonic variation is actually due to different phonetic environments. This paper presents articulatory and acoustic data from four speakers of American English in various pre-boundary positions in the context V-L The primary articulatory measure is the locus (the cursor) and chosen to be: (1) amplitude of retraction of the tongue body as measured by various articulatory (and acoustic) factors; (2) in darker variants of /l/, an earlier occurrence of the dorsal retraction and lowering position relative to the apical advancement; (3) where the dorsal extremum is temporally later than the tongue tip in the data by "lip delay." Since there is a high correlation between the articulatory measures above -- as well as by relevant frequency measures -- variation strongly with the contextually measured duration of the pre-boundary time, the following general conclusions are presented. We interpret our results as evidence that there is no reason to treat the light and dark allophones as categorically distinct phonological (or phonetic) entities in English, and rather, the single phonological entity /l/ is phonetically implemented as a lighter or darker variant depending upon such factors as the /l/'s position within the syllable, and the phonetic duration of the prosodic context containing the /l/.

---

## Our Interpretation

Sproat and Fujimura investigate whether English light and dark /l/ are truly separate phonological categories or simply gradient variants of a single phoneme. Using X-ray microbeam articulatory tracking and acoustic formant measurements, they demonstrate that the darkness of /l/ varies continuously with syllable position and prosodic boundary duration, driven by the relative timing of two independent articulatory gestures (tongue tip raising and tongue body retraction). For speech synthesis, this means /l/ allophony should be modeled as a gradient continuum with formant targets (especially F2) shifting based on syllable position, rather than as a simple categorical switch between two fixed allophone targets.
