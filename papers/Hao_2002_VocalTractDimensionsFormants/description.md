This dissertation compares vocal tract dimensions (oral/pharyngeal length and volume measured via acoustic reflection) and the first three formant frequencies of nine English vowels across 120 speakers from three racial groups (White American, African American, Chinese), balanced by gender. Key findings include significant racial differences in oral volume and total vocal tract volume (Chinese speakers largest), significant gender differences in tract length and formant frequencies (~20% higher for females), and a regression model showing that oral length, pharyngeal length, and oral volume predict average F1 ($R^2_{adj} = .18$) while F2 and F3 have no significant linear relationship to gross tract dimensions. For speech synthesis, this provides comprehensive normative formant data across demographic groups and establishes the physical basis for speaker-dependent formant scaling, with the important caveat that articulatory configuration rather than overall tract size drives higher formant variation.
