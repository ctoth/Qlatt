# Fowler (1980) — Coarticulation and Theories of Extrinsic Timing

## Overview

This paper is a theoretical critique of extrinsic timing theories of speech production and a proposal for an alternative intrinsic timing framework. It does not provide acoustic parameter tables or synthesis algorithms directly, but its theoretical framework has deep implications for how coarticulation should be modeled in a formant synthesizer.

## Key Concepts

### Extrinsic Timing Theories (the target of critique)

Three defining assumptions of extrinsic timing theories:

1. **Segments are timeless** — the essential properties of a phonological segment exclude the dimension of time
2. **Segment boundaries are abutting straight lines perpendicular to the time axis** — segments occupy non-overlapping time slots
3. **The plan is distinct from its executor** — spatial coordinates are specified independently of temporal coordinates; the articulatory mechanism actualizes timing

In these theories, coarticulation is explained by "feature spreading" — adjusting feature specifications in adjacent segments to smooth articulatory transitions. Canonical forms (ideal, uncoarticulated targets) are assumed to exist but never appear in actual speech.

### Criticisms of Extrinsic Timing

1. **Some segments are inherently temporal** — prenasalized stops, affricates, and diphthongs cannot be characterized as the convergence of features at a single point in time (Lisker 1972)
2. **VOT and rate of release** distinguish phonemes like /t/ vs /c/, /b/ vs /w/ — these require temporal specification
3. **Feature bundles predict synchronous gesture triggering** — but evidence shows gestures are initiated based on when they need to complete (goal-directed), not synchronously (Bell-Berti 1973: levator palatini onset varies with following vowel identity)
4. **Canonical forms never appear in speech** — creates an unexplained gap between representation and realization
5. **Vowel and consonant rate strategies differ fundamentally** — fast speech reduces vowel muscle force (undershoot) but increases consonant muscle force (Gay & Hirose 1973; Gay & Ushijima 1974). An extrinsic executor alternating between these strategies lacks rationalization.
6. **Stress-timing** — controlled intervals between stressed vowel centers (not edges) cannot be generated by sequential feature-bundle execution

### Intrinsic Timing Theory (the proposal)

Key claims:

1. **Segments are four-dimensional** — time is an inherent dimension of phonological segments
2. **Segments are coproduced, not sequenced** — consonants and vowels overlap in production
3. **Perceived discreteness arises from qualitative difference** — we perceive segments as discrete because they are qualitatively different kinds of events (like hearing a singer's voice over accompaniment), not because they occupy separate time slots
4. **The plan IS the coordinative structures** — no separate executor needed; plans are self-executing (supported by Sternberg et al. 1978 reaction time data)

### Coordinative Structures

Functional organizations of muscles (Easton 1972; Turvey 1977) with three key properties:

1. **Equivalence classes of movements** — the structure generates a family of acts described by a parameterized mapping (e.g., F = ae^(k(l-l₀)), Feldman 1966)
2. **Nested organization** — small coordinative structures are organized within larger ones (like intralimb stepping within whole-body locomotion)
3. **Cyclic operation** — many structures reinitiate after completing their repertoire

#### Three Subsystems in Speech

1. **Respiratory system** — maintains nearly constant subglottal pressure through coordinated inspiratory/expiratory muscle activity; expiratory phase occupies ~87% of cycle during speech vs ~60% in vegetative breathing (Draper et al. 1958; Lenneberg 1967)

2. **Laryngeal system** — three servo-systems (Wyke 1967):
   - Mucosal pressure receptors → reflex adjustment of adductor/abductor tone
   - Mechanoreceptors in laryngeal joints → adjustment of intrinsic laryngeal muscles
   - Muscle spindles in intrinsic muscles → stretch reflex
   - Macroscopic modes: normal phonation, whisper, falsetto, creaky voice (Abercrombie 1967)

3. **Supralaryngeal system** — evidence for coordinative structures:
   - Jaw closure velocity varies with distance to travel → nearly constant gesture duration (Ohala et al. 1968; MacNeilage 1970)
   - Ohman (1967) data: nearly identical formant transition durations in /idi/ and /ada/ despite different tongue-tip travel distances
   - Compensatory articulation: lip perturbation during bilabial closure → jaw compensates within ~20-30 ms (Folkins & Abbs 1975)

### Coarticulation as Coproduction

The central model for coarticulation:

1. **Vowels and consonants are produced by distinct coordinative structures** (following Ohman 1966 and Perkell 1969)
   - Vowels: slow, global vocal tract shape changes; primarily extrinsic tongue musculature
   - Consonants: fast, local obstructions; primarily intrinsic tongue musculature + tongue tip

2. **Vowel production may be continuous throughout an utterance** — the vowel-producing coordinative structure is invoked once and runs continuously, with consonantal gestures superimposed

3. **Vowel system modeled as spring-like dynamics:**
   - F = k(l - l₀) where l₀ is the resting/target position (tunable parameter)
   - Different vowels = different l₀ values
   - Same vowel in different contexts: same l₀, different gestures (lowering after /i/, raising after /a/)
   - The vowel is NOT the target position — it is the functioning of the system with that parameter value (like a curve vs. its asymptote)

4. **Coarticulatory effects explained:**
   - Anticipatory lip rounding: vowel /u/ is coproduced with preceding consonants, not feature-spread
   - Anticipatory velar opening for nasals: parallel to lip rounding, with fixed temporal lead relative to primary gesture onset
   - Vowel-to-vowel effects: unstressed vowel production superimposed on trajectory from one stressed vowel to another
   - Formant transitions into/out of medial stops vary with flanking vowels (Ohman 1966): vowel gesture continues through consonant closure

## Relevance for Klatt Synthesizer

### Direct Implications

1. **Coarticulation rules should model coproduction, not feature spreading** — formant transitions between vowels should reflect continuous vowel-to-vowel trajectories with consonantal perturbations superimposed, rather than target-to-target interpolation with context adjustments

2. **Vowel undershoot at fast rates** — reduce formant excursions (toward a neutral position) rather than simply shortening durations; consonants at fast rates should maintain or increase articulatory precision

3. **Formant transition durations may be relatively invariant** regardless of the distance between formant targets (following Ohman 1967 data showing similar transition durations in /idi/ and /ada/)

4. **Timing of anticipatory gestures** — lip rounding, velar opening etc. should have fixed temporal leads relative to the segment that requires them, independent of preceding consonant context (Bell-Berti & Harris 1979)

5. **Stress-timing** — intervals between stressed vowels are the controlled unit, not segment durations; this aligns with duration rules that operate on inter-stress intervals

### Modeling Considerations

The paper advocates for a view where:
- The basic unit of temporal organization is larger than the segment (coordinative structure lifespans)
- Consonants are perturbations on a continuous vowel substrate
- This maps naturally onto Ohman's (1966) locus model, which Qlatt already references

The spring-dynamics model (F = k(l - l₀)) for vowel articulation provides a principled basis for:
- Vowel undershoot (spring doesn't reach target before next parameter change)
- Context-dependent formant trajectories (same target, different starting positions → different gestures)
- Rate effects (faster parameter changes → more undershoot)
