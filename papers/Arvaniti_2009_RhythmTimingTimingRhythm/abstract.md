# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

This article reviews the evidence for rhythmic categorization that has emerged on the basis of rhythm metrics, and argues that the metrics are unreliable predictors of rhythm which provide no more than a crude measure of timing. It is further argued that timing is distinct from rhythm and that equating them has led to circularity and a psychologically questionable conceptualization of rhythm in speech. It is thus proposed that research on rhythm be based on the same principles for all languages, something that does not apply to the widely accepted division of languages into stress- and syllable-timed. The hypothesis is advanced that these universal principles are grouping and prominence and evidence to support it is provided.

*(p.46; Phonetica 2009;66:46-63; received September 15, 2008; accepted January 8, 2009)*

---

## Our Interpretation

The problem is that duration-based rhythm metrics were taken as validating the stress-timed/syllable-timed dichotomy, when they are unstable across metric family, corpus, elicitation method, speaker, and even sentence design. Arvaniti shows the metric families contradict each other (Thai, Luxembourgish, Japanese, Polish flip classes), that %V and ΔC barely correlate (r = -0.26) while nPVI and rPVI do not correlate at all (r = 0.019), that within-class score gaps can exceed between-class gaps, and that in her own six-language study speaker variability swamps language and material design alone moves a language across the notional continuum. She then argues that rhythm is a perceptual construct of grouping and prominence, universal across languages, with interstress intervals clustering near the natural tempo of 0.5-0.6 s regardless of rhythmic class. For a source-filter synthesizer this matters twice: it disqualifies rhythm metrics as synthesis targets or evaluation criteria, since identical scores arise from opposite mechanisms, and it supplies a concrete inventory of duration and prominence effects to model instead, including prosodically gated vowel reduction and spectral tilt as a prominence cue.
