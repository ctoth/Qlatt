## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Covariance view vs. Configuration view** (p.22): Two approaches to intonation and emotion. The covariance view claims direct continuous acoustic cues covary with emotional messages. The configuration view claims intonation has categorical linguistic structure, and emotion is conveyed partly by configurations of category variables.
- **Overlay models of F0** (p.23): Models that treat the pitch contour as a complex function decomposable into simpler components -- local "bumps" for accent overlaid on global shapes/slopes. Originates with Ohman (1967), best known as Fujisaki's F0 model.
- **Fujisaki model** (p.24-25): Posits two components of F0: a *phrase component* (impulse response that decays exponentially) and an *accent component* (step function smoothed by time constant). The two are added to produce the F0 contour. See Figure 1.3 (p.24/43).
- **Declination** (p.25-26): The general tendency for F0 to fall across a stretch of speech. The rate of declination is inversely related to domain length -- the longer the domain, the less steep the slope; the total F0 drop is relatively constant regardless of domain length.
- **Lookahead problem** (p.26-28): Overlay models require advance knowledge of the utterance domain length to set the correct declination slope, which is psycholinguistically implausible. Fujisaki's model avoids this with its decaying impulse phrase component.
- **Pitch accent** (p.48-49): A local feature of a pitch contour -- usually a pitch *change* involving a local maximum or minimum -- that signals prominence of the syllable with which it is associated. Term first proposed by Bolinger (1958), resurrected by Pierrehumbert (1980).
- **Intonational pitch accent vs. lexical pitch accent** (p.49): Intonational pitch accent = sentence-level prominence feature in languages like English. Lexical pitch accent = lexically specified pitch features in languages like Japanese, Swedish, Norwegian.
- **Edge tones** (p.47): Tones associated with the periphery of prosodic domains. In English, this covers Pierrehumbert's "phrase accent" and "boundary tone." Edge tones can occur at both beginnings and ends of prosodic domains.
- **Events vs. transitions** (p.47): The AM theory distinguishes localised linguistically important pitch features ("events") from the stretches of pitch contour between events ("transitions"), which are phonologically unspecified.
- **Stress vs. accent** (p.49-54): Ladd carefully distinguishes: *Metrical strength* = abstract prominence relations in prosodic structure; *Dynamic stress* = concrete acoustic prominence on a syllable; *Pitch accent* = intonational event associated with prominent syllable. These have often been conflated but are conceptually independent.
- **Designated Terminal Element (DTE)** (p.56): The single strongest point of prominence in a metrical tree structure (Liberman and Prince 1977). In compounds, only the DTE receives a pitch accent.
- **Tune-text association** (p.55-57): The idea that pitch accents are associated with metrically strong positions (stressed syllables), analogous to how song lyrics align with musical beats. Originated by Liberman (1975).
- **Paralanguage** (p.34-38): Aspects of vocal communication that are meaningful but not organized along linguistic lines. Uses the same acoustic dimensions as intonation (pitch range, loudness, duration) but differs in the *structure of the sound-meaning relation*: paralinguistic signaling is *gradient/scalar*, while linguistic signaling is *quantal/categorical*.
- **Linguist's Theory of Intonational Meaning** (p.41): Ladd's term for the widely shared view that intonational elements have morpheme-like meaning -- general meanings that give rise to specific pragmatic nuances in specific contexts. Contrasts with the instrumental approach that assumes specific meanings like "interrogation" are directly conveyed by acoustic parameters.

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Fujisaki's F0 Model (p.23-29)
- Two-component superposition model: phrase component + accent component
- Phrase component: impulse response (rises rapidly, decays exponentially to asymptote)
- Accent component: step function creating rises and falls at accented syllables, smoothed by time constant
- The two are summed to produce ln(F0(t))
- **Critique**: (1) lookahead problem for declination slope; (2) negative accent commands don't work for low-rising contours; (3) a single phrase-level function is inadequate for all utterance types; (4) doesn't generalize well beyond Japanese

### Groennum's Model of Danish Intonation (p.29-30)
- Sentence intonation modelled as a straight line of variable slope
- Nearly level for interrogatives (line 1 in Figure 1.5)
- Steeply declining for complete final declaratives (line 3)
- Intermediate slopes for incomplete declaratives and other question types (line 2)
- Local low-rise-fall accentual configurations fitted to the sentence intonation line
- Argues global slope is a distinctive element of intonation

### Kochanski/Shih/Xu Quantitative F0 Models (p.29-31)
- Parametric models where individual accent realisation parameters can change syllable-to-syllable
- Pitch range can become wider or narrower to reflect prominence or focus
- Broadly compatible with sequential intonational phonology
- Originally motivated by Chinese lexical-postlexical F0 interactions

### Autosegmental-Metrical (AM) Theory -- Four Basic Tenets (p.44)
1. **Sequential tonal structure**: Tonal structure = string of local events at defined points in the segmental string. Between events, pitch is phonologically unspecified (transitions). Main event types: *pitch accents* (associated with prominent syllables) and *edge tones* (at prosodic boundaries).
2. **Distinction between pitch accent and stress**: Pitch accents are *intonational* features associated with certain syllables per prosodic principles. Perceived prominence of accented syllables is a matter of *metrical strength* and/or *dynamic stress*, distinguishable from pitch accent.
3. **Analysis in terms of level tones**: Pitch accents and edge tones can be analysed as composed of primitive *level tones* or pitch targets: High (H) and Low (L).
4. **Local sources for global trends**: The scaling of any H or L tone depends on factors (emphasis, position, etc.) orthogonal to its identity as H or L. Global trends like declination reflect *localised but iterated* scaling changes rather than a single global function.

### Metrical Phonology (p.55-56)
- Linguistic prominence involves *relations* between nodes in binary-branching tree structures
- Each branch is labelled s (strong) or w (weak)
- No absolute degree of prominence implied by labels -- only whole structures can be interpreted
- The DTE (Designated Terminal Element) is the single peak of prominence in a tree

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

- **Declination domain-length relationship** (p.26): The longer the declination domain, the less steep the slope. Total F0 drop across a domain is relatively constant, irrespective of domain length. (Groennum's work, Thorsen 1986)
- **Global slope varies with sentence type** (p.29, Figure 1.5): In Danish: nearly level slope for echo questions; steep decline for complete final declaratives; intermediate for incomplete declaratives.
- **Pitch range modulation for focus** (p.29-30): Pitch range can become narrower following the focused word of an utterance.
- **Pitch peak alignment distinguishes Swedish accent types** (p.32-33): Accent 1 (acute) has the word-accent peak preceding the stressed vowel; Accent 2 (grave) has the peak and subsequent drop coinciding with the stressed vowel. The genuinely distinctive feature is the *alignment* of the pitch peak relative to the stressed vowel.
- **Stress signalled by pitch in citation forms but not always in context** (p.49-50): In citation forms, "permit" (noun vs. verb) shows clear pitch contour differences. In questions or post-focal positions, pitch distinctions can disappear while stress is still perceived through duration and vowel quality.
- **Acoustic correlates of stress** (p.54): In English, not only F0 but also duration, intensity, and vowel quality (spectral balance) serve as reliable indicators of stress. Beckman (1986) argues melodic accent and dynamic accent are valid distinct categories. Spectral balance can identify stressed syllables even without pitch accent.

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

- **Rise-fall-rise contour** (p.45-46): Used for echo questions/contradiction in English. AM analysis: an accentual rise-plus-fall (pitch accent) + an independently selected boundary rise (edge tone). Not a holistic "rise-fall-rise nuclear tone" but a sequence of two discrete events.
- **Swedish word accents** (p.32-33, Figure 1.6):
  - Accent 1 (acute): single-peaked contour in citation form; word-accent peak precedes stressed vowel
  - Accent 2 (grave): double-peaked contour; word-accent peak coincides with stressed vowel; additional intonational peak follows
- **French intonation** (p.59-60): Tonal events anchored to the last full-vowel syllable of the phonological word, not to the edge per se. Dell's (1984) analysis shows accent placement in French is accentual (metrical) not edge-based, despite the "stress deafness" of French speakers.
  - Example contour notation: L M H L for emphatic declarative statements
  - Final L spreads to tag/vocative constituents

## Alignment and Timing

- **Peak alignment as distinctive feature** (p.32-33): In Swedish, the alignment of the pitch peak relative to the stressed vowel is what distinguishes the two accent types -- not the overall contour shape.
- **Tune-text association as beat alignment** (p.56-57): Stressed syllables align with musically strong positions ("on the beat"). Liberman's children's chant examples (9-11) show that note durations must be adjusted so stressed syllables fall on beats, analogous to how pitch accents associate with metrically strong syllables.
- **Edge tone placement** (p.47): Edge tones need not be at the very edge of a domain -- they are associated with the periphery but can be aligned with specific syllables near boundaries. Example: Japanese initial H edge tone can align with first or second mora.
- **French accent anchoring to last full-vowel syllable** (p.60): When final schwa (*e muet*) is present, tonal events remain anchored to the preceding full-vowel syllable, not the schwa -- showing the anchor is structural (metrically defined) rather than purely edge-based.

## Cross-linguistic Data

- **Swedish** (p.32-33, 50-51): Word accent system with Accent 1 (acute) vs. Accent 2 (grave), distinguished by pitch peak alignment. Classic minimal pair: *nummer* (Accent 1) vs. *nunnor* (Accent 2).
- **Danish** (p.29-30): Groennum's model -- sentence intonation as variable-slope straight line with local accent configurations fitted to it.
- **Chinese** (p.29-31, 50): Lexical tone and postlexical F0 interactions central to motivating Kochanski/Shih and Xu models. Some Chinese varieties distinguish tone phonemes partly by voice quality differences, not just F0.
- **Japanese** (p.47): H edge tone at beginning of prosodic words, aligned with first or second mora (Pierrehumbert and Beckman 1988).
- **French** (p.59-60): Debate over whether French has stress at all (Beckman 1986 suggests only demarcative cues). Dell (1984) argues last full-vowel syllable has special structural prominence (DTE status). Speakers show "stress deafness" (Dupoux et al. 1997, 2001).
- **Indonesian** (p.60): Difficulties applying IPO framework to Indonesian intonation suggest that different theoretical assumptions may be needed (Ode 1994).
- **Dinka** (p.37): Nilotic language of Southern Sudan with systematic breathy/non-breathy voice quality distinction running through the lexicon as a phonemic contrast.
- **Yoruba** (p.36): Tone language with H, M, L levels where paralinguistic modifications of pitch range do not obscure lexical identity.

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation Architecture
- The AM theory's sequential tonal structure (tenet 1, p.44) directly maps to TTS F0 generation: generate a string of tonal events (pitch accents + edge tones) and interpolate between them. Transitions between events are phonologically unspecified -- TTS systems can use simple interpolation.
- The event/transition distinction (p.47) suggests F0 generation should focus computational precision on *accent locations* and *boundary locations*, with smooth interpolation elsewhere.

### Pitch Accent Placement
- Pitch accents are associated with metrically strong (stressed) syllables (p.54). The TTS system needs a stress/prominence pattern first, then distributes pitch accents to prominent positions.
- Not all stressed syllables receive pitch accents (p.53-54). The system must determine which stressed syllables are *accented* (information structure, focus, given/new status).
- Pitch accent is "prominence-cueing" not "prominence-lending" (p.54) -- it signals where prominence is, rather than creating it.

### Stress as Multi-Component Phenomenon
- In English, stress is cued by duration, intensity, vowel quality (spectral balance), AND pitch accent (p.50, 54). A TTS system should implement multiple acoustic correlates for stressed syllables, not just F0 changes.
- Spectral balance (relative energy in different frequency bands) can reliably identify stressed syllables even without pitch movement (p.54-55). This suggests Klatt parameters like spectral tilt and formant amplitudes should vary with stress.

### Declination Implementation
- Declination slope should be inversely related to utterance/phrase length (p.26). A fixed declination rate will sound wrong for short vs. long utterances.
- Alternative to continuous declination function: model declination as local, iterated scaling changes on successive tonal events (AM tenet 4, p.44). Each successive H or L target is slightly lower than the previous one.

### Boundary/Edge Tone Implementation
- Edge tones mark prosodic boundaries and are independent of pitch accents (p.45-47). The TTS system needs separate edge tone generation at phrase boundaries.
- Edge tones can be aligned with specific syllables near (not necessarily at) the boundary edge (p.47).

### Phrase-Level Slope Variation
- Different sentence types may require different global slopes (p.29): steep decline for declarative completion, nearly level for questions, intermediate for continuation. This can be implemented as different baseline declination parameters per sentence type.

### Paralinguistic Modulation Layer
- Paralinguistic effects (emotion, emphasis) modify the *realisation* of phonological categories rather than replacing them (p.35-36). Implementation: apply paralinguistic scaling (pitch range expansion/compression, rate changes) as a post-phonological transformation layer on top of the categorical intonation pattern.
- Pitch range is the most obvious shared dimension between linguistic intonation and paralinguistic signaling (p.37). A TTS system should support pitch range modulation that is independent of the tonal sequence.
