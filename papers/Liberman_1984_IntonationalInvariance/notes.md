# Intonational Invariance under Changes in Pitch Range and Length

> **Ingestion scope:** Chapter 10 of *Language Sound Structure* (eds. Aronoff & Oehrle, MIT Press 1984), book pages 157-233. The PDF holds only this chapter (40 scanned images, two book pages per image, plus a ResearchGate cover sheet at page-000). The whole chapter was read.

## One-Sentence Summary
Liberman and Pierrehumbert give a quantitative, locally computed model of English F0 realization in which tonal targets are scaled by a multiplicative pitch-range transform above a speaker-specific asymptotic baseline, downstep is a constant-ratio operation on that transformed scale, and phrase-final lowering is a separate multiplicative constant, so that "declination" falls out of local rules rather than a global phrase-planned trend.

## Problem Addressed
Four factors interact in English intonation: tune (contour type), prominence (local emphasis), declination (downward trend across a phrase), and overall pitch range (global scaling). Past descriptions treated only tune as linguistic and set the rest aside as phonetic or paralinguistic. But without a model of the quantitative factors, one cannot decide the linguistic questions: how many tone levels there are, whether features are static (H/L) or dynamic (rise/fall), and how tonal structure maps onto syntax and semantics. A four-tone-level description has too many degrees of freedom: an F0 rise-fall could be 4-1-4 in one range, 4-2-4 in a larger range, or 4-3-4 in a still larger one, and nothing distinguishes them *(p.164)*.

## Key Contributions
- A pitch-range scaling model in which F0 values are transformed relative to a speaker-specific asymptotic **baseline** and a **reference line**, so that ratios between accent targets are invariant under changes of overall pitch range *(pp.161, 168-169)*.
- Evidence that **final lowering** is a separate, non-expressive multiplicative effect applied to the last pitch accent of a phrase, distinct from prominence and pitch range *(pp.166-167, 169)*.
- Evidence that **downstep** in English stepping contours is a constant-ratio decay on the transformed scale, matching Anderson's (1978) treatment of African tone downstep *(pp.170-171)*.
- The claim that no phrase-level preplanning of F0 is needed: all computations are on pairs of adjacent elements, with parameters independent of phrase length *(p.166)*.
- Support for static tonal features (H, L target levels) over dynamic ones (rise, fall) *(pp.161, 165)*.

## Terminology and Framework

### The four factors *(p.157)*
| Term | Definition as used in this chapter |
|------|------------------------------------|
| Tune | Intonation contour type (qualitative, categorical) |
| Prominence | Local degree of stress or emphasis (quantitative, continuously variable) |
| Declination | Downward trend in pitch across a phrase |
| Pitch range | Global, or at least phrase-sized, choice of pitch-scaling parameters |

- **Tune** is decomposed into sequences of elements aligned with the text: **pitch accents** marking some (not necessarily all) stressed syllables, plus additional tonal features associated with the end of the intonation phrase *(p.159)*.
- **Peak accent**: realized as a peak on the stressed syllable *(p.159, fig. 5)*.
- **Step accent**: relatively lower level on its stressed syllable, relatively higher level just before. The lower level of one step accent becomes the higher level of a succeeding step accent, so a sequence of step accents forms a descending staircase *(p.159, fig. 6)*.
- **A configuration**: the intonational complex on the main "answer" phrase - peak followed by a fall to the bottom of the speaker's range *(p.169)*.
- **B configuration**: the complex on the "background" phrase - peak, fall stopping short of the bottom, then a rise at the very end of the phrase *(p.169)*.

### Empirical observations motivating the model
- Prominence is quantitative and continuously variable; more emphasis gives a higher peak *(p.161)*.
- Under varied overall emphasis on the monosyllable *Anne*, **peak F0 values vary widely while terminal low values remain essentially constant** (fig. 4, p.159). This is the core asymmetry the baseline/reference-line model explains.
- Declination: the range of F0 values used is narrower and lower at the end of a phrase than at the beginning *(p.161)*.
- Listeners normalize for declination when judging relative prominence: for two accents to sound equally prominent, the second must generally have a lower F0 value (Pierrehumbert 1979) *(p.163)*.
- In the AB pattern the peak on the answer is much higher than the peak on the background phrase; in the BA pattern the peak on the answer is only slightly higher. Hypothesis: the underlying prominence relation is the same in both, and the difference arises because final lowering **adds to** the prominence difference in the first case but **partly offsets** it in the second. Under the right transform, the ratio of answer to background peak F0 is constant regardless of order *(p.169)*.
- In a long stepping contour each step is smaller than the one before, giving the impression of an exponential curve - just what a constant-fraction-of-preceding-level step rule predicts *(p.169, fig. 13)*.
- Final lowering is **not** varied for expressive purposes, so its separation from tune and prominence is fairly simple. Local prominence and overall pitch range *are* independently variable, and their separation is problematic; in these experiments the overall pitch range and the prominence of the initial pitch accent co-vary in a way that still permits unique recovery of the full description from the measurements *(p.167)*.

## Study Design

### Experiment 1: AB / BA two-phrase patterns *(pp.167-173)*
- **Type:** Repeated-measures speech production experiment.
- **Subjects:** Four - two were the authors, two were Bell Labs summer employees; two women and two men. Subject codes appearing in figures: KXG, JPB, DWS, MYL *(pp.172-176)*.
- **Materials:** The single sentence *Anna came with Manny*, produced in two intonation patterns:
  - **AB pattern** (fig. 9): response to *What about Manny? Who came with him?* - *Anna* carries the A accent (answer), *Manny* the B accent (background).
  - **BA pattern** (fig. 10): response to *What about Anna? Who did she come with?* - *Anna* carries the B accent, *Manny* the A accent.
  - Both patterns have **two intonational phrases**: each pitch accent is the nuclear pitch accent of its own phrase. The single-phrase alternatives (figs. 11, 12) were not studied *(p.169)*.
  - The cited patterns and priming questions may not be interchanged: fig. 10's pattern is not an appropriate answer to question (1), nor is fig. 9's pattern possible as an answer to question (2) *(p.167)*.
- **Manipulation:** "Degree of overall emphasis or excitement" varied in **10 steps** (a number 1 to 10 on each card). "1" was demonstrated as a phlegmatic mumble, "10" as a forceful shout, using a one-word utterance *(pp.172-173)*.
- **Repetitions:** Six to eight repetitions of each pattern in each pitch range. Cards randomized in blocks of 20 covering all combinations of pitch range and response type; six blocks for the first subject, eight for subsequent subjects *(pp.169, 172-173)*.
- **Card contents:** (a) the priming question, (b) the response *Anna came with Manny*, (c) a number 1-10 for overall emphasis. Subjects read both question and response *(p.172)*.
- **Measurements:** F0 peaks of the A and B accents (primary); also starting F0 in each phrase, the low point at the end of the A configuration, the valley between the peak and the terminal rise in the B configuration, and the maximum height of the terminal rise in the B configuration *(p.177)*.

### Experiment 2: berry-name stepping contours *(pp.169-177)*
- **Type:** Repeated-measures speech production experiment on downstepping staircases.
- **Subjects:** Three of the four Experiment 1 subjects also participated *(p.172)*.
- **Materials:** Lists of berry names drawn from *bilberries, raspberries, bayberries, blueberries, mulberries*. List length varied from **two to five**; **20 distinct lists of each length** *(pp.171, 173)*.
  - For length two, all possible ordered pairs of unlike berries were used. For each longer length, all berries were equally represented in all serial positions and all transitional probabilities were equal *(p.171)*.
  - Sequences apt to produce deaccenting were rearranged *(p.177)*.
- **Rationale for permuted berry lists:** (1) all words in such a list have equal importance, minimizing semantic/pragmatic effects on relative prominence; (2) averaging peak F0 in a given list position across lists of a given length removes segmental effects on measured F0. The five berry names were chosen because they are **voiced throughout**, and because **two syllables separate the accented syllables from each other and from the end of the phrase**, which facilitates measurement and analysis *(p.171)*.
- **Manipulation:** Pitch range varied in **three steps** (a number 1 to 3 on each card) *(pp.171, 173)*.
- **Design size:** 20 lists x 4 lengths x 3 pitch ranges = **240 cards**, shuffled in blocks of 12. Each block contained all four lengths and three pitch ranges, with no repetitions of the same berry-name list in any block *(p.173)*.
- **Measurements:** Peak F0 on each primary stressed syllable; only values at least two frames from a consonantal release or closure were used, to reduce transient effects from [b] articulation. Also the lowest stable F0 in the terminal fall and the earliest stable F0 at the start of the utterance. Timepoints of all F0 measurements were stored *(p.177)*.
- **Why this design:** up to five list items samples serial-position effects in more than two places; differing list lengths allow a test for preplanning; and unlike Experiment 1 it shows effects **inside a single intonational phrase** *(pp.171-172)*.

### Common procedures *(pp.172-177)*
- Recordings made in a sound-treated booth; materials on note cards.
- For subjects other than the authors, the desired intonation patterns were demonstrated by example beforehand, and the subject's ability to produce them naturally was checked.
- Varying intonation pattern and overall emphasis orthogonally "did not seem very difficult"; a wide variety of pitch ranges was elicited. A corresponding range of amplitudes was produced, and **the most emphatic renditions were up to twice as long as the least emphatic** *(p.173)*.
- F0 contours computed from digitized recordings using an **LPC-based method due to Bishnu Atal**; measurements made with an interactive parameter display program *(p.177)*.
- Instruction intent was **not** pure variation on a single psychological scale, but natural covarying variation in pitch range, amplitude, and rate *(p.169)*.

### Note on figure 8's declination evidence *(p.161-163)*
Figure 8 averages the first 43 phrases produced by a radio announcer in an extemporized monologue, time-normalized. A phrase boundary was posited wherever there was a nonhesitation pause or a clear sentence boundary. The authors caution that **the apparent downtrend in figure 8 could be due in part to the time-normalization or to the speaker's use of step accents**, not to declination per se *(p.163)*.

## Section 3: General Characteristics of Intonational Variation

Stated goal: a simple, coherent model of F0 realization that (a) interprets the categories of intonational description, (b) incorporates the important qualitative characteristics of F0 measurements, and (c) shows good quantitative agreement. The model's outline is determined by exploratory data analysis, including fitting of partial or alternative models *(p.177)*.

### 3.1 The bottom of the range is constant *(pp.178-182)*
- Increasing pitch range has different effects on different points of the contour: **peak values increase dramatically, low values remain much more nearly constant**. The lowest F0 values are quite constant over the full spread of pitch ranges. These are the low value at the end of the A configuration and the low value at the very end of the phrase in the stepping patterns *(p.178)*.
- Statistics were computed on **60 to 80 measurements per set** in table 1. The three reported numbers per set are the mean (Hz), the standard deviation (Hz), and the ratio SD/mean *(p.179)*.
- Reexpressing SD as a proportion of the mean removes the effect of simple multiplicative scaling. The ratios are consistently smaller for low-point sets than for peak sets; **the only exception is the A accent in AB order for subject DWS** (.16 vs .16) *(p.179)*.
- **Table 2 regression** predicts the A-configuration low from the associated peak as `low = intercept + slope * peak`; R squared gives the proportion of variance in the predicted quantity accounted for by the straight line *(pp.180-181)*.
- In utterance-final position (BA pattern), A-accent lows show **no consistent direction of dependence** on their peaks: half the subjects positive, half negative, and R squared is very small. Utterance-final A accent lows are essentially **unaffected by pitch range changes** *(p.181)*.
- Striking cross-experiment result: for a given speaker, the utterance-final low-point values in Experiment 1 are **essentially the same** as in Experiment 2, even though the two data sets were collected **more than six months apart** with quite different materials. This final low value is "a relatively invariant characteristic of a speaker's voice" *(p.181)*.
- A-accent lows in utterance-**medial** position (AB pattern) are less invariant: all four subjects show a positive slope, so lows tend to rise as peaks rise, and R squared values are considerably larger than in final position *(p.181)*.
- B-configuration lows (tables 3, 4) also vary less than their peaks in proportion to their means, but they have a considerably **greater tendency to rise** with the pitch-range manipulation than A lows do. In both medial and final position, table 4's slopes and R squared values are consistently larger than table 2's *(p.181)*.
- Summary: **lower F0 values are less affected by pitch range changes than higher ones, and the lowest F0 values (utterance-final falling accents) are nearly constant for a given speaker.** Similar observations by Maeda (1976) and Boyce & Menn (1979) in corpus studies; the present results strengthen theirs because pitch range varied much more widely *(pp.181-182)*.

### 3.2 Final peaks are lower *(pp.182-189)*
- Figures 14-17 are not symmetric about the diagonal: BA points are hardly above the diagonal at all, while AB points are substantially below it. **The second peaks are lower, relative to the first peaks, than time-order invariance would predict** *(p.182)*.
- With only two peaks per phrase, one cannot tell whether the effect is a lowering of the last peak, a raising of the first, or any revaluation leaving the last peak lower relative to the first - hence Experiment 2 *(p.182)*.
- In figures 18-20 (downstep data averaged by pitch range instruction and list length), all lines trend downward. There is some tendency for **shorter lists in a given pitch range to begin lower** *(p.186)*.
- **The most striking phrasal-position effect is in list-final position: the list-final measurements are generally lower than would be expected** *(p.186)*.
- Nonfinal points do not form straight lines; they fall on **curves concave upward**, consistent with exponentially decaying series *(p.186)*.
- Two independent demonstrations of final lowering: (i) extrapolating an exponential fit to the nonfinal points and observing the final point's shortfall; (ii) comparing final measurements against nonfinal measurements in the same serial position in longer lists (no assumption about the downstep function needed) *(pp.186-189)*.
- Fitting procedure for the crude downstep model: for each subject, each pitch range was allowed its **own asymptote (reference level)** but the **same downstep constant was required for all pitch ranges**. Numerical methods found the downstep constant plus three reference levels best fitting **12 numbers** - the four nonfinal average F0 measurements in the three pitch ranges. This test cannot be run on three-item lists because the two nonfinal elements do not provide enough information to estimate the parameters *(pp.188-189)*.
- Table 5 errors (predicted minus actual) in items 1-4 are small (mostly under 2 Hz), while item-5 errors are large and always positive (4.3 to 27.3 Hz), showing the final measurement falls reliably below the extrapolated trend *(pp.188-189)*.
- Table 6 (five-item minus shorter-list averages, position by position) shows small increasing values in the first position (3, 6, 10) reflecting the shorter-lists-start-lower tendency, small hints of downdrift in nonfinal positions (3, 4, 5 and 6, 8), which "could easily be due to chance variation," and a **much larger last-column value in each row (18, 27, 31)** - the final-lowering signature *(p.189)*.

### 3.3 "Speaking up" raises the reference level *(pp.189-191)*
Three obvious ways to model "speaking up" given decay-to-an-asymptote downstep: (a) increase the reference level, (b) increase the starting value, or (c) change the decay constant *(p.189)*. Methodological preference: keep the **downstep constant independent of pitch range**, since that parameter is peculiar to downstep; the reference level, by contrast, is naturally treated as an aspect of F0 scaling in general and therefore a reasonable parameter for pitch range to modify *(p.190)*. The optimization in table 5 chose asymptotes freely with **no assumption that reference levels rise with pitch range instruction**, yet the fitted asymptotes (table 7) rise consistently with pitch range, which the authors take as quantitative support for making the reference level pitch-range dependent *(pp.190-191)*.

### 3.4 "Speaking up" raises the initial pitch with respect to the reference level *(p.191)*
Once reference levels are subtracted out, initial peak values still increase with pitch range (table 8).

## Key Equations

### Exponential decay (3) *(p.186)*

$$
X_{i+1} = s \cdot X_i
$$

Where: $X_i$ is the value at step $i$; $s$ is a constant less than 1 (the decay/downstep constant). Such sequences asymptote to zero unless a nonzero asymptote is supplied.

### Exponential decay to a nonzero asymptote (4) *(p.186)*

$$
X_{i+1} - r = s \cdot (X_i - r)
$$

Where: $r$ is the asymptote ("reference level"); $s$ is the decay constant, $s < 1$.

### Model 1 (5) *(p.192)*

**(5a) General F0 transform**

$$
T(P) = P - r
$$

Where: $P$ is a measured F0 target in Hz; $r$ is the reference level for the phrase, in Hz; $T(P)$ is the transformed value, i.e. the target's distance above the reference line.

**(5b) Downstep**

$$
T(P_i) = s \cdot T(P_{i-1})
$$

Where: $P_i$ is the F0 target in Hz of a step accent in position $i$, downstepped with respect to the previous accent target $P_{i-1}$; $s$ is the downstep constant. *(The scanned printing of the subscript on the right-hand side is ambiguous; the accompanying prose says explicitly that $P_i$ is downstepped with respect to the previous accent target $P_{i-1}$, so the intended relation is successor-from-predecessor as written here.)*

**(5c) Answer-background relation**

$$
T(P_A) = k \cdot T(P_B)
$$

Where: $P_A$ is the F0 target in Hz of the A accent; $P_B$ is the target of the B accent; $k$ is the answer-background constant.

**(5d) Relation of $r$ to the initial accent target**

$$
r = f \cdot (P_0 - b)^{e} + d + b
$$

Where: $P_0$ is the target in Hz of the first pitch accent; $b$ is the invariant bottom of the speaker's system in Hz (the utterance-final low value); $d$ is the minimum distance in Hz that $r$ must remain above $b$; and $e$, $f$ are constants. The exponent $e$ is present because the preliminary modeling in tables 7 and 8 suggests the relation is nonlinear *(p.193)*.

**(5e) Final lowering (pseudo-phonological rule)**

$$
P \rightarrow r + l \cdot (P - r)
$$

Where: $l < 1$ is the final-lowering constant; the rule lowers F0 targets in phrase-final position, and the lowering effect is a constant fraction of the distance of $P$ above $r$ *(p.192)*.

### Modified transform for model 1 (6) - the equivalent single-transform formulation *(p.193)*

$$
T(P) = (1/l) \cdot (P - r)
$$

Where: $l < 1$ in final position and $l = 1$ otherwise. The identical model results from deleting rule (5e) and using this transform instead. The two-part form (5a plus 5e) was chosen to **separate the claim of final lowering from the claim that F0 relations are constant ratios once $r$ has been subtracted** *(p.193)*.

### Design notes on the model *(pp.192-193)*
- The effect of phrasal position is assumed to be **limited to lowering in final position** *(p.192)*.
- $r$ is constrained to remain at least $d$ Hz above $b$, the invariant low value, since utterance-final low points do not increase with pitch range; $b$ is treated as the bottom of the entire system *(pp.191-192)*.
- Without an equation like (5d), the model would need **two** numbers specific to each utterance ($r$ and one accent target). Equation (5d) makes them co-vary predictably, so that **only one parameter need be fixed per utterance** *(p.193)*.
- Model 1 has **seven constants per speaker** ($k$, $s$, $l$, $b$, $d$, $e$, $f$) plus **one per-phrase pitch-range parameter** ($r$, or equivalently $P_0$) *(pp.191, 193)*.

## Model Fitting Procedure *(pp.193-194)*
1. Fix values for the seven constants $k, s, l, b, d, e, f$.
2. For each type and length of utterance, each value of the pitch-range parameter $r$ (equivalently $P_0$) predicts a set of F0 targets. For an $N$-accent utterance this traces a **curve in $N$-space**.
3. The measured F0 values for a particular utterance are a point in $N$-space; the predicted values are taken at the point on the model curve nearest to it.
4. Error measure: the **average absolute difference** between model values and data values, chosen over Euclidean distance in $N$-space because it is easier to interpret and does not emphasize outliers the way squared error does.
5. Parameters are found by **hill climbing** to minimize that error.
6. Each subject is fit **separately, but on both experiments at once**. $k$ is relevant only to the Answer-Background data and $s$ only to the Downstep data; the other five constants help predict both.
7. For the Answer-Background experiment the models are fit to **raw peak F0 measurements**; for the Downstep experiment each subject's peak F0 measurements are **first averaged by length and pitch range instruction**, since the variation of berry names across positions lets averaging remove segmental effects.
8. Data volume per subject: **42 data points in the Downstep experiment** (each the average of 20 observations) and **160 data points in the Answer-Background experiment**.
9. To combine error measures across the two data sets, the average absolute error was computed for each data set independently and **the two averages were summed**.

## Section 4-5: Model Assessment and Alternatives

### What the authors conclude from the model-1 fit *(pp.202-205)*
- The error measures in table 9 are "encouragingly small" given the scatter in the data; the initial observations that gave rise to the model describe the data fairly well, and no large surprises emerge from the interaction of the posited effects *(p.202)*.
- Explicit caveat: a good fit does **not** mean the model is correct. There may be small uncovered effects; **many rather different models make nearly equivalent predictions**; and new phenomena or different measurement approaches may require a different approach *(p.202)*.
- **There is no obvious residual pattern consistent across subjects** *(p.204)*.
- Specific residual patterns: in the Downstep data, JBP and MYL show a **less steep fall than predicted for the highest pitch range** in four- and five-item lists, while DWS does not; for the middle pitch range JBP consistently shows a **steeper fall** than predicted, while MYL and DWS do not *(p.204)*.
- Diagnosis: these Downstep deviations are caused **entirely** by the function relating $r$ and $P_0$. For JBP, table 7's length-five data indicate asymptotes at (158, 163, 218) Hz, while the model-1 fit puts them at **(157, 178, 197) Hz** - the $r$-to-$P_0$ function does not become sufficiently nonlinear, presumably because the consequences for the Answer-Background fit would be undesirable *(p.204)*.
- **Curvature diagnostic:** if $r$ as a function of $P_0$ were linear, the AB-experiment peak-to-peak functions would also be linear. The nonlinearity of (5d) curves them. The exponent $e$ is the main determinant of the direction of curvature: **$e > 1$ predicts a "wishbone" configuration in plots like figures 14-17; $e < 1$ predicts a "trumpet" shape.** Data from both experiments agree in setting $e$ greater than 1 *(p.204)*.
- But setting $e$ as high as JBP's Downstep data alone would suggest makes the AB/BA model have **excessive curvature in the higher pitch range area** *(p.204)*.
- Remaining AB misfit: there is a tendency for the **low-pitch-range BA peak-2 predictions to be a little too high** *(p.204)*.
- Possible explanations for the residuals: (i) the two experiments differ in many ways and may genuinely have different $r$-to-$P_0$ relations or different final-lowering functions; per theory, $r$ and $P_0$ **should be freely variable in general** - they co-varied here only because subjects unconsciously adopted a strategy for interpreting the pitch-range instruction, and the two experiments' different circumstances could have led to different strategies; (ii) a genuine flaw - it may be that the **Answer-Background relation should be modeled as a change in the reference line $r$** rather than as a change in the height of the F0 target above it *(pp.204-205)*.

### Acknowledged over-parameterization *(p.205)*
- "Without any doubt, model 1 has too many parameters, in the sense that the available data underdetermine their values." Four parameters ($f$, $e$, $d$, $b$) are devoted to the $P_0$-to-$r$ function, which the data constrain relatively little, and the two data sets do not entirely agree on its shape *(p.205)*.
- Form (5d) was adopted on the beliefs that $r$ must stay somewhat above $b$, that its relation to $P_0$ might be nonlinear, and that perhaps only the portion of $P_0$ above $b$ is relevant *(p.205)*.
- **Except for $b$** - identified with the speaker's invariant final low F0 value - **none of the parameters in (5d) have any clear interpretation.** $d$ is "the translation of *somewhat*," while $e$ and $f$ are "just a way of getting a curved function with a minimum number of additional parameters" *(p.205)*.
- These four parameters trade off against each other to give very similar functions. In the Downstep experiment, although 12 asymptotes are in fact required to fit the data, they group into three classes determined by pitch range instruction. In the Answer-Background experiment, changes in $k$ can somewhat compensate for the effects of the $P_0$-to-$r$ relation *(p.205)*.
- **$b$ is consistently too low** relative to the measured utterance-final lows in table 1, but the **sum $b + d$ is much closer** *(p.205)*.
- $k$, $s$, and $l$ are interpretable in that the model asserts certain F0 relations to be constant ratios in the transformed space; but the authors have **no a priori idea what these ratios should be**, so the fitted values cannot be checked against independent evidence *(p.205)*.

### How to evaluate a model *(pp.203-204)*
1. The crudest evaluation is the error measure, which is meaningless without a standard of comparison: how does the error compare with the error implied by the apparent variability of the data, and how well do other models fit?
2. A more sensitive evaluation is looking for **patterns in the residuals**; systematic errors suggest problems in the model.
3. The number and nature of parameters matters: with enough arbitrary parameters any data can be fit arbitrarily well, so **the smallest reasonable number should be used and each one's true contribution honestly assessed**.
4. "The last, best question about a model is how well it copes with new material, in domains far from those that spawned it."

### Which model choices were data-driven vs. arbitrary *(p.203)*
- **Dictated by the data:** downstep as decay to a nonzero asymptote $r$ rather than linear stepping in Hz or decay to zero; $r$ increasing with pitch range; an extra lowering in last position in downstepping contours.
- **Motivated by desire for generalization:** using the same $P - r$ transform for both data sets, even though the Answer-Background experiment gives no direct evidence for a reference level that increases with pitch range; equating the time-order asymmetry in the Answer-Background experiment with the final lowering visible in the Downstep data.
- **Essentially arbitrary:** the form of the equation relating $r$ and $P_0$, designed just to make the rest of the model work, since the Downstep patterns in tables 7 and 8 specify the function at only three points and the Answer-Background data offer little direct guidance.

## Alternative Models

### Model 1A (7) - three-parameter $r$ function *(p.206)*

$$
r = f \cdot (P_0)^{e} + d
$$

Substituted for equation (5d) in model 1. Drops the $b$ offset inside and outside the power term.

### Model 1B (8) - two-parameter (linear) $r$ function *(p.206)*

$$
r = f \cdot P_0 + d
$$

Substituted for equation (5d) in model 1.

**Result:** these changes have relatively little effect on the Answer-Background fit, but the fit to the Downstep data becomes **consistently worse**, due to an exaggeration of the previously noted error pattern *(p.206)*.

### Model 1C (9) - alternative final lowering *(p.206)*

$$
P \rightarrow l \cdot P
$$

Substituted for rule (5e) in model 1. That is, final lowering scales the raw F0 value rather than the distance above $r$. Error measures (table 12) are similar to model 1's, and **examination of the residuals shows no crucial advantage for one version of Final Lowering over the other** *(p.206)*.

### Model 2 (10) - the log transform, extending the model to L tones *(pp.206-208)*

$$
T(P) = \log\!\left(\frac{P - b}{r - b}\right)
$$

Substituted for equation (5a) in model 1.

Where: $P$ is the F0 value in Hz; $b$ is the speaker's baseline (absolute floor); $r$ is the reference line for the phrase.

**Motivation and properties:**
- In the contours modeled so far, increases in emphasis raise F0 targets, but **there are cases in which increased emphasis lowers F0** - motivating two tonal categories H and L (as in Pierrehumbert 1980) *(pp.206-207)*.
- Under transform (5a), the zero value for H tones is at $r$, with increasing values going up from there. L tones are constrained to remain above the baseline $b$, are usually lower than H tones in comparable circumstances, and scale **downward** under emphasis. This suggests a transform in which **H tones scale upward from $r$ and L tones scale downward from $r$, with $b$ forming a floor below which L tones cannot be pushed** *(p.207)*.
- Under (10), transformed H tones range from 0 to $+\infty$ as their realizations range from $r$ to $+\infty$; transformed L tones range from 0 to $-\infty$ as their realizations range from $r$ down to $b$ *(p.208)*.
- The **strong nonlinearity** of the transform is what lets L and H tones be treated symmetrically in the transformed domain while behaving quite unsymmetrically in the F0 domain. Many other nonlinear transforms share this property *(p.208)*.
- **Generalization payoff:** if such a transform fits both L and H tone data, **prominence values could be implemented as ratios of absolute values in the transformed domain, regardless of tone type, with the sign of the transformed value determined by the tone type.** This would generalize equation (5c) to cover strings of free L accents and mixed configurations of H and free L accents *(p.208)*.
- **Limitation:** the experiments did not address tones taking negative values under transform (10); only the positive side could be tested *(p.208)*.

### 5.3 Models that don't fit *(p.208)*
The authors verified the **failure** of:
- models that leave out final lowering;
- models with no parameter like $r$ to represent the increasing asymptote of downstepping patterns with increasing pitch range;
- models that do not provide a **prepausal low value invariant under changes in pitch range and length**;
- models that predict a **rate of declination that varies directly with phrase length**.

Such models show poorer fits and, more importantly, **a residual pattern that reflects the omitted or added assumption** *(p.208)*.

### Explicit self-criticism of the authors' own earlier model *(p.210)*
The model of Liberman & Pierrehumbert (1979) and Pierrehumbert (1980) is named as a case in point of a failing model. It was built to account for the AB/BA data and for informal observations of downtrending F0 in normal speech (figs. 7, 8). It includes:
- **a baseline that declines linearly over the duration of a phrase, with starting and ending values fixed**, and
- a scaling function for F0 peaks of

$$
\frac{P_i - b_i}{b_i}
$$

Where: $P_i$ is the peak value in Hz and $b_i$ is the value of the (declining) baseline at the point in the phrase where $P_i$ occurs *(p.210)*.

It **lacks lowering of final peaks** and **lacks any parameter like $r$ to increase the asymptote of downstepping contours with increasing pitch range**. As a result it **fits the downstep data very badly indeed**, and the nature of its failures, seen in the residual pattern, pointed the authors toward the models presented in this chapter *(p.210)*.

### 5.3.1 "Down with Rises and Falls" - the case against configurational/dynamic features *(pp.210-211)*
- Framing: the target-level approach follows the American structuralists, who described intonation as a sequence of pitch-level targets ("contour points" in Pike's 1945 phrase) connected by transitions. The opposing tradition holds that **configurational features such as "scoop-like rise" are crucial**; a first-order version would model measured F0 **rises and falls** rather than F0 levels (Bolinger 1958, Bailey 1970, 1971, Clark 1978, Ladd 1978) *(p.210)*.
- Methodological complaint: **linguists who claim rises and falls to be primitive have given no instructions for measuring them**, so the authors had to improvise. For the Answer-Background data they measured F0 at the beginning, peak, and lowest point of the first and second pitch accents. For the Downstep data they assume a "stairstep" idealization, using the original peak measurements on accented syllables as estimates of step levels, so the configurational model concerns the movement from one step to the next; the authors say they are **"puzzled about how to give the rise-fall theory a fair test"** *(p.210)*.
- **Result for the AB data: the configurational approach fails badly.** Rise-against-rise and fall-against-fall scatter plots (figs. 30-33) are "quite unruly" compared with the peak-against-peak plots of figures 14-17. Table 14 quantifies this with R squared from linear regression, treating rises and falls both as differences and as ratios in Hz *(p.211)*.
- **The lower correlations for rises and falls are not due to nonlinear relations but simply to unsystematic ones. Rises and falls are not being controlled as carefully as relative peak levels are** *(p.211)*.
- **For the Downstep data:** the configurational theory in its simplest form makes differences or ratios between successive step levels primitive. The **ratio version is equivalent to model 1 with no reference line $r$, and thus fails badly**; the **difference version is no better, since successive differences are systematically unequal**. One could argue the primitives should be step-level ratios after $r$ has been subtracted, with $r$ rising with pitch range - but **that model is identical to model 1, and nothing about it seems especially attuned to the spirit of configurational theories** *(p.211)*.

## Section 6: Discussion

### 6.1 English tonology (Pierrehumbert 1980 framework) *(pp.216-219)*
- Fundamental assumption: **tunes decompose into a sequence of elements aligned with the text** - pitch accents on some but not necessarily all stressed syllables, plus additional tonal elements at the edges of the phrase. This decomposition is what makes realization rules like (5b), which compute a tone's value by looking back to a preceding one, possible *(p.216)*.
- The phonetic data support **tone levels rather than tone changes** as the primitive elements of the tune *(p.217)*.
- Degrees-of-freedom argument accepted from Bolinger: four phonologically distinct tone levels is excessive given that local prominence and overall pitch range also introduce variation. **Reducing the tonal inventory to two is the most direct way of alleviating the problem. Reduction to one tone (only upward excursions from a phrasal baseline or reference line, of whatever shape) would leave the system unable to represent the full range of English intonation patterns** *(p.217)*.
- In Pierrehumbert's (1980) grammar all tunes are strings of Low and High tones. **Pitch accents consist of a single tone, or a pair of tones one of which is marked with `*` for alignment with the accented syllable.** Additional tones at the phrase margins: **boundary tones** determining F0 at onset and offset, and a **phrase accent** (proposed for Swedish in Bruce 1977), a single tone occurring right after the nuclear pitch accent *(p.217)*.
- Under this account: the **peak accents of Experiment 1 are H accents**. The **B configuration** = L phrase accent followed by an H boundary tone. The **A configuration** ends with an L phrase accent and an L boundary tone *(p.217)*.
- The **step accents of Experiment 2 are the two-tone accent H + L**. When one step accent follows another, its H tone is lowered by equation (5b). **This downstep rule puts the H on the same level as the L of the preceding pitch accent, producing the staircase.** The fall at the end of the phrase is due to an L phrase accent and L boundary tone - which is why the terminal values in the Downstep experiment are comparable to the terminal values for A configurations in BA order *(p.217)*.
- Relation to African tone: many descriptions (surveys in Welmers 1973, Anderson 1978) use a downstep rule to generate an unbounded number of phonetic levels from an underlying two-tone system. The classic rule lowers H after a preceding HL sequence. **Total downstep** = the downstepped H lands on the same level as a preceding L; **partial downstep** leaves the H somewhat higher than a preceding L *(p.217)*.
- The English downstep rule resembles the African cases in lowering H after L, but **differs in being triggered specifically by the L + H and H + L pitch accents**. In the African cases tones are not organized into pitch accents and the rule applies across the board to given tonal sequences. **Restricting the English rule to H + L and L + H accent triggers is necessary to avoid overapplication** *(pp.217-218)*.
- Specifically, **downstep is assumed not to apply to the second peak in the AB and BA sequences**, despite the intervening L tone(s). If the second peak were both downstepped and subjected to final lowering it would have been lower than observed, and an attempt to fit the data on that hypothesis **would not have succeeded as well as model 1**. Downstep is presumed blocked because the tones are not organized into the requisite pitch accents and because of the intervening **intonational phrase boundary** *(p.218)*.

### H vs. L asymmetry (free tones) *(pp.218-219)*
- **H\* accents scale upward as local prominence increases. L\* accents do the opposite: increasing local prominence on an L\* accent causes it to scale downward**, other things being equal (illustrated in fig. 34) *(p.218)*.
- **The downward scaling is bounded in a way that upward scaling is not.** L\* accents, plus the L phrase accent and L boundary tone from Experiment 1, occupy a rather restricted region in the lower part of the F0 range. As prominence increases the L\* accents approach the baseline, and there is **clear evidence of saturation**. **No evidence of saturation was seen as H accents are raised**, although in principle there must be a ceiling on achievable F0 *(pp.218-219)*.
- The authors' analogy: like the floors and ceilings of everyday life - jumping high can raise the issue of hitting the ceiling, but normal operations are carried out in the lower part of the room with feet on or near the floor, so **the floor has more of a role in the planning of motor activities than the ceiling does** *(p.219)*.
- These H/L contrasts follow from a transform like $\log((P - b)/(r - b))$ **if one assumes that the transformed value of a free L (an L not in a two-tone accent) is the negative of the transformed value of an equally prominent H** *(p.219)*.
- **Explicitly flagged as speculative:** no systematic evidence on the scaling of L tones was collected, so the symmetry suggestion is speculative. **How two-tone accents behave under changes in prominence is left unanswered** *(p.219)*.

### 6.2 Physiological issues *(pp.219-220)*
- The posited reference level and final lowering "call out for physiological explication." Some aspects could in principle be rooted in F0 perception, but the authors' view is that **the physiology of production is a more likely source** *(p.219)*.
- Final lowering and reference-level variation might come about through **variations in pulmonary control and/or the rest state of the larynx** *(p.219)*.
- It is tempting to attribute phrase-level effects to **subglottal pressure**, as Collier (1975) proposes. **However, the authors' perusal of subglottal-pressure/F0 data in the literature (Collier 1974, Atkinson 1973) suggests that subglottal pressure does not vary with a time course that would permit it to control final lowering** *(p.219)*.
- **Proposed experimental test of phonological control of final lowering:** if final lowering applies to the last (nuclear) accent regardless of how many postnuclear syllables intervene before the phrase boundary, it is under phonological control. If instead it occurs in a **fixed timing relation to the end of the utterance**, it is more likely a phonologically irrelevant consequence of how utterances are ended *(p.219)*.
- The same question applies to the reference level. Under one kind of phonological control, each reference-level setting would be associated with a phonological domain, perhaps the intonational phrase, and there might be **rules relating reference levels of different phrases, just as downstep relates the values of different tones**. Alternatively, the reference level might be **a parameter only loosely coupled to linguistic structure, on the model of eyebrow height or even heart rate** *(p.220)*.

### 6.3 Preplanning *(pp.220-223)*
- **Hard preplanning:** processing that is an essential part of intending to say something and that normally must be accomplished before executing that intention, on pain of dysfluency. **Soft preplanning:** preparation a speaker may freely choose to make, out of rational calculation, ritual observance, or any other cause, and that might well be omitted for a linguistically equivalent utterance in other circumstances. The extreme example given is preparing to duck before uttering an insult. The distinction names the ends of a continuum *(p.220)*.
- Soft preplanning of some F0 aspects must surely occur: speakers can choose a pitch range at will and sometimes know in advance how long a phrase will be. **The desire to have plenty of space for stepping down might urge higher pitch ranges for longer phrases; the desire to save breath for the long pull might urge the opposite.** In the Downstep experiment the subjects used neither strategy to any great extent, but little significance is attached to that *(pp.220-221)*.
- Any observed association of pitch range choice with phrase length is a natural candidate for explanation as soft preplanning; such effects would be expected to be **erratic and generally small compared to other influences**, and speech lacking such correlations does not sound abnormal *(p.221)*.
- **Central negative claim:** "we see no evidence that the F0 implementation for an entire phrase is necessarily laid out before speaking begins, even when the phrase is known in advance and fluently produced. **All of our measurements can be modeled quite well on a left-to-right, plan-as-you-go basis.**" The authors concede this negative evidence is logically weak *(p.221)*.

### Arguments against Cooper & Sorensen (1981) *(p.221)*
Cooper & Sorensen propose two consequences of preplanning: (i) an increase in first peak height with phrase length, and (ii) a phrasally based computation of medial peak values by the **Topline rule**, which computes medial peaks as a function of first and last peak values - implying a noninitial peak cannot be computed without knowing the final peak. Three reasons for rejecting that conclusion:
1. **The outputs of the Topline rule resemble downstepped contours, which the authors model without assuming preplanning.**
2. **Cooper and Sorensen do not compare their model to models that do not rely on the final peak**, so the need for the final peak in predicting earlier ones is uncertain.
3. **Serious errors in their statistical analysis leave their claim that their model fits the data unsupported.** (Developed at length in Pierrehumbert & Liberman 1982.)

Cooper & Sorensen also do not handle the first-peak/length effect formally, since it is unclear whether the relevant measure of length is phonological, syntactic, or semantic *(p.221)*.

The last peak in each sentence is lowered below the value for nonfinal peaks at the same serial position; in the longer sentences the lowering appears not to be confined to the last peak.

### Thorsen's Danish data as a challenge to local rules *(pp.221-223)*
- Thorsen studied Danish pitch accents, which the authors would analyze as **L\* + H**, in utterances of one to eight accents, including terminal and nonterminal declaratives and interrogatives *(p.222)*.
- Her result: peaks fall gradually from beginning to end, and **the slope of the fall depends on sentence type - steepest for terminal declaratives, least steep for interrogatives, nonterminal declaratives in between**. Thorsen takes the downslope to arise from declination and asserts that **the slope of declination depends on sentence type** *(p.222)*.
- Liberman & Pierrehumbert's reinterpretation: assume the downslope arises from a **downstep rule** - plausible since Danish has the classic downstep context of alternating Hs and Ls. **The slope differences would then arise from different choices of relation between $r$ and $P_0$: the steepest contours have the lowest $r$; raising $r$ makes the interrogative and nonterminal declarative contours less steep, for the same $P_0$, by raising the asymptote for stepping** *(p.222)*.
- A prediction of that account: $P_0$ should run higher in sentences with higher $r$, since there is no a priori reason for initial prominence to be inversely correlated with $r$. Thorsen's data do show $P_0$ higher in sentences with less overall downslope. **However, $P_0 - r$ evidently decreases as $r$ is raised**, which might represent a **ceiling effect on choice of $P_0$ in a normal speaking voice** *(p.222)*.
- **An alternative description of the slope difference would rely on varying the step factor $s$ with sentence type** *(p.222)*.
- In Thorsen's terminal declaratives (fig. 36, her own best-behaved data), as in the present data, **the last peak is lowered below the value for nonfinal peaks at the same serial position. But in the longer sentences the lowering appears not to be confined to the last peak** *(p.222)*.
- Two interpretations of that: (i) the physiological process responsible for final lowering **appears further from the end** in Thorsen's longest sentences, because her seven- and eight-accent sentences taxed speakers in a way the present five-accent sentences did not; (ii) **limited look-ahead** in the principles determining F0 targets - which would require relaxing the authors' position somewhat, but is still very different from a theory with global realization rules *(p.222)*.

### 6.4.1 The Great Declination Debate *(pp.224-225)*
- Positions in the literature: a gradual F0 downtrend called declination is reported by many; **Lieberman & Tseng (1980) contest its existence**; **Umeda (1982) regards it as "situation dependent"** *(p.224)*.
- **The authors' methodological attack on all sides of the debate:** essentially all participants measure declination on material that is **not analyzed, and thus not controlled, for phrasing, stress pattern, and tune**; and **none distinguishes among the plausible contributions to F0 trends** - global decay, boundary effects such as initial raising or final lowering, local tonal downstep, correlations of stress pattern or semantic/pragmatic prominence with order, and the interaction of all these with pitch range and initial peak height. **"It follows from what we have learned here that such measurements are uninterpretable."** *(p.224)*
- Worked demonstration: the **AB patterns have a surface downtrend through the peaks, but a line fit through the lowest points would rise**, since the A accent ends in an L boundary tone but the B accent does not. In the **BA patterns** the lowest point in the second phrase is lower than in the first, yet the second peak is usually higher than the first. **The phrase-final lowering effect is the same in both cases** - a conclusion not evident from measuring peak or valley trends in either contour, reachable only by separating phrase-level effects from other factors on the basis of a theory about what those factors are *(p.224)*.
- Downstepped patterns, unlike AB/BA, do exhibit **stereotypical declination**, with both local maxima and local minima monotonically decreasing. **But most of the downtrend should be attributed to the choice of pitch accent type, not to a phrase-level effect. Only when the downstepping due to the pitch accents is taken out does the phrase-level effect (final lowering) turn out to be the same as in the AB and BA contours** *(p.224)*.
- Models like this one **yield differing rates of apparent declination (for a given phrasing, stress pattern, and tune) as a function of pitch range and initial peak value.** Thus reports of overall slope differences among intonation types, such as Waibel (1979) and Thorsen (1980b), **may reflect control of parameters that influence observed downtrends only indirectly** *(pp.224-225)*.

### 6.4.2 Log models: 't Hart & Cohen, Lea *(p.225)*
- 't Hart & Cohen (1973) propose a model of Dutch intonation in which "hat" patterns are added onto a **declining baseline in the log domain**. Lea's model for English (1973, 1979) adds utterance, phrase, and stressed-syllable components together in the same way *(p.225)*.
- **Criticism:** "Such models make wrong predictions because they lack any counterpart to our reference level, which changes with overall pitch range while leaving the baseline (seen in the final L tones) invariant." *(p.225)*

### 6.4.3 Fujisaki's model *(pp.225-227)*
Description as the authors present it (following an approach originally laid out by Ohman 1967). F0 contours are modeled by summing, **in the log Hz domain**, three independent contributions:
1. **Fmin** - a constant "indicating the lower limit of fundamental frequency below which vocal fold vibration cannot be sustained."
2. A **baseline component** - a phrase-sized falling pattern.
3. An **accent component** - local excursions due to pitch accents.

Mechanics *(pp.225-226)*:
- Both baseline and accent components are modeled as a **succession of discrete levels, "smoothed separately by the low-pass characteristics of their respective control mechanisms," approximated as critically damped second-order linear systems**. Each baseline unit and each accent unit has an **amplitude, a start time, and an end time**.
- **Accent units:** after smoothing, each rises from zero at its start toward its specified amplitude, approaching asymptotically, then falls asymptotically to zero after its end. **Accent units are assumed not to overlap** - an accent's start time cannot precede a previous accent's end.
- **Baseline units:** all have their ends at the end of the utterance (or perhaps the breath group), so successive units **ride on the tails of their predecessors**. Their smoothing differs, so their contribution rises toward the specified amplitude and then falls gradually toward zero. **At the end time of a baseline unit, a negative copy of its onset is added in.**
- Baseline unit start times are generally placed **about 200 msec before the beginning of the corresponding phrase**, so the baseline peak lines up approximately with the phrase start. The placement of baseline end times relative to the utterance end seems to vary, so a **variable amount of the negative-going region** plays a role in the modeling.
- The baseline control mechanism has a **time constant of about 300 msec** *(p.226)*.

**Two main difficulties, which put Fujisaki's model in qualitative disagreement with the present data** *(pp.226-227)*:
1. **The negative-going region of the baseline component after its end time is an unfortunate feature.** Its amplitude equals that of the positive-going part it arises from, and with more than one phrase the negative tails superpose. If baseline end-time placement is held constant and these regions play any role, the model predicts that **higher-pitch-range sentences will have lower final values** (if long enough for the positive baseline component to damp out), and that **subdividing a single intonational phrase into two, thus adding two baseline components, will lower the low value at the end of the utterance. Both predictions are false, at least for English** *(p.226)*.
   - Evidence of inconsistency in Fujisaki's own fits: for three cited sentences, the negative-going part plays no significant role since baseline end times sit essentially at the utterance ends; but for four isolated words the post-end-time part of the baseline appears to have been used to get F0 low enough at the end. The authors attribute this to the ~300 msec baseline time constant leaving the baseline component quite high for single words (~400 msec long) while allowing decay for multiword phrases (all longer than one second) *(p.226)*.
   - The authors' own experiments with Fujisaki's model suggest the problem is general: **since utterance-final low tones are essentially invariant in real life, making this work in the model requires the baseline component's end time to be earlier in short low-ending utterances than in long ones** *(p.226)*.
2. **The model cannot represent the effects of pitch range change.** With respect to the downstep data, it **lacks any effective mechanism to raise the downstep asymptote with pitch range**. Because the baseline component decays to zero, increasing baseline amplitude will not effectively raise the level two seconds into the phrase. **Raising Fmin will make it nearly impossible to get the final low values to come out invariant**; at best, just the right amount of the negative-going baseline tail must be accessed to bring the final F0 values down in compensation *(p.227)*.

**Verdict:** "We have spent so much time on Fujisaki's model because we think so highly of it. It attempts to model the entire time course of F0 patterns on the basis of a small number of linguistically motivated parameters, which are given plausible physiological interpretations. There can be little doubt that an appropriately modified form of this approach can be made to fit our data; however, they are incompatible with the model as Fujisaki presents it." *(p.227)*

## Section 7: General Issues in Phonology and Phonetics *(pp.227-231)*

This section is a methodological argument, not new intonation data, but it states the paper's general thesis about the phonology/phonetics division of labor.

- **Core claim:** the phonetic implementation of phonological representations is responsible for much of a language's sound pattern. Any treatment of a sound pattern implies a division of labor between the principles determining the distribution of phonological entities and those governing their relation to observations. **Implementation systems are neither trivial nor obvious, so the correct division of labor is not obvious either** *(p.227)*.
- Three English observations used as test cases *(pp.227-228)*:
  1. The indefinite article is realized as /ei/ or schwa before consonant-initial words but as /aen/ (or reduced forms) before vowel-initial words. **Usually considered morphology; an implementational treatment would require lexical conditioning of phonetic implementation, which seems the exception rather than the rule. The observed pattern is exactly what is expected if the phonological representation contains an /n/ in one case and not the other** *(p.228)*.
  2. The aspiration of voiceless stops is longer before high vowels than before low vowels. **Explicable on physical grounds (Ohala 1974): with greater supraglottal impedance, as for high vowels, vocal cord adduction must be further advanced for oscillation to begin. Writing a phonological rule for this would be a category mistake** *(p.228)*.
  3. In American speech a silent closure is often inserted between tautosyllabic nasals and voiceless fricatives, so *tense* becomes rather like *tents*; per Fourakis (1980) this does not occur in South African English. **This is the troublesome case:** the dialect-specificity argues against a purely physiological explanation, yet Fourakis's own measurements show epenthetic stops are systematically shorter (**about 25% shorter in *tense* than the corresponding region of *tents***), so a phonological epenthesis rule would require the realization process to know whether the stop is underlying or epenthetic. **The authors prefer leaving the representations as /tens/ and /tents/ and letting the "epenthesis" be accomplished by dialect-specific patterns of timing control for velum, larynx, and tongue** *(pp.228-229)*.
- **Generalization:** cases of "allophonic variation" often turn out to have properties like observation (3), which leads the authors to suspect that a correct division of labor **will leave the output of the phonology rather more abstract than usually assumed** *(pp.228-229)*.

### 7.1 Favorable consequences of enhanced phonetic implementation *(p.229)*
- Better understanding of phonetic implementation **removes from phonology the burden of representing sound patterns for which its natural descriptive mechanisms are inappropriate**, clarifying and simplifying the theory of phonological features and rules.
- Example: Pierrehumbert (1980) is **broader in coverage and simultaneously simpler in conception** than Liberman (1975). "The foundation stone of the improved edifice is the reduction of the tonal inventory to H and L, from the four-way contrast in Liberman. This reduction depends crucially on a set of nontrivial implementation rules for tonal sequences." **The present paper revises those rules considerably for empirical adequacy but retains Pierrehumbert's phonological advantages unchanged** *(p.229)*.
- Example: Prince (1980) argues overlength should not be in Estonian's phonemic inventory but treated as phonetic realization of monosyllabic feet, reducing an unusual three-way length contrast to the interplay of foot structure with a familiar two-way contrast *(p.229)*.
- Example: Clements & Ford (1979) on Kikuyu downstep - a set of rules moving a downstep marker (a floating L tone) over arbitrarily many adjacent L tones, all changed to H, with deletion of adjacent downstep markers. **Prince & Liberman (1982) show a simple extension of the Clements-Ford system for realizing downstep lets the downstep movement, the block raising, and the downstep deletion all be eliminated** *(p.230)*.

### 7.2 The locality conjecture on phonetic control *(pp.230-231)*
- The danger: lacking constraint, one could construct a "phonetic implementation" that directly interprets logical form, simplifying syntax, morphology, and phonology to vacuity *(p.230)*.
- **The conjecture (offered as a crude example of the type of restriction to aim for, and true of the implementation rules proposed here):**
  - Various phonological objects have phonetic parameters associated with them. In this model, **$r$ is a property of an intonational phrase, while $P$ is a property of a pitch accent or perhaps of one of its constituent tones** *(p.230)*.
  - The set of phonetic parameters presumably has **about the same degree of universality as the set of phonological features** *(p.230)*.
  - When a parameter value has paralinguistic meaning, as with pitch range, this is best modeled by **interpretation of free selection among available alternatives, as in lexical choice**. Other aspects of parameter determination are the expression, in the current context, of the object's intrinsic phonological content *(p.230)*.
  - **"The computation of any parameter of object $Y_i$ can only depend on the 'accessible' properties of $Y_i$ and $Y_{i-1}$, where $Y_{i-1}$ means the immediately previous object of the same type (if any). Thus, pitch accent can look back to previous pitch accent, phrase to previous phrase, etc."** "Accessible" includes a small set of intrinsic properties of the objects and any relations between them; **it excludes any properties of the subconstituents of these objects** *(p.231)*.
- **Functional motivation:** speakers can get on with the task at hand without knowing all the details of what follows, while hearers can in principle complete phonetic processing of what they have heard up to any given point in the stream of speech *(p.231)*.
- **Falsifiable consequence:** many apparent anticipatory effects are known. Unless the conjecture is wrong, all such cases must be explained by **feature spreading at the phonological level, computation of some parameter of a higher-level constituent, interpolation between one target and the next, or temporal overlap in the realization of the segments in question** *(p.231)*.

### 7.3 Lexical phonology and a division of labor *(p.231)*
Quoting Kiparsky (1982, p.131) on lexical phonology - derivational and inflectional processes organized in levels, each with a set of phonological rules for which it defines the domain of application, dividing rules into those assigned to lexical levels and those operating after words are combined into sentences. **The authors' proposal: limit postlexical rules to the minimal set that combines lexical representations into a well-formed phrase-level phonological structure, and assign all other postlexical regularities to phonetic implementation.** They note that permitting "floating" segments and/or empty structural positions to emerge from the lexicon still allows some phonological readjustment of word edges, as in French liaison.

## Section 8: Conclusion *(p.232)*
The authors ask to be judged on three separable counts:
1. **Descriptive particulars:** the phonetic parameters underlying English intonation and their role in relating abstract intonational categories to observed F0 contours. "We have proposed to reinterpret F0 measurements in terms of a fixed 'baseline,' a 'reference line' that increases with pitch range, and a lowering effect specific to the domain of (certain) final pitch accents; **this approach yields F0 implementation rules that do not require whole-phrase preplanning**."
2. **Theoretical proposals:** that nontrivial, partly language-particular phonetic interpretation should take over the function of most postlexical phonological rules. **"This suggestion may stand or fall quite independently of the intonational descriptions that engendered it."**
3. **Style of research:** hybrid strategies exercising the methods of phonology and phonetics simultaneously. "There are distressingly few researchers who give serious thought both to phonological descriptions and to phonetic measurements."

## Results Summary
- Peak F0 varies dramatically with pitch range while utterance-final low F0 is nearly invariant for a given speaker, stable even across two experiments run more than six months apart with different materials *(pp.178-181)*.
- The AB/BA asymmetry (final peaks lower than time-order invariance predicts) and the extra drop on the last item of a downstepping list are the same phenomenon: **final lowering**, a multiplicative constant $l$ in the range .59 to .77 across four speakers *(pp.182-189, 202)*.
- Downstep is a constant-ratio decay above a pitch-range-dependent asymptote, with fitted ratio $s$ of .59 to .68 across three speakers *(pp.186-190, 202)*.
- Model 1 achieves mean absolute errors of 1.5 to 4.8 Hz on downstep data and 4.9 to 7.8 Hz on the Answer-Background data, on F0 values ranging from about 70 Hz to about 600 Hz *(p.194)*.
- Nonfinal peak values in a downstepping list are predicted to within about 2 Hz by a per-subject exponential decay with a shared downstep constant across pitch ranges *(p.188)*.
- Peak-to-peak regressions have R squared .84 to .93; rise-to-rise and fall-to-fall regressions on the same utterances fall as low as .04, so **rises and falls are not controlled as carefully as relative peak levels** *(p.211)*.

## Limitations
- "Without any doubt, model 1 has too many parameters, in the sense that the available data underdetermine their values." Four parameters go to the $P_0$-to-$r$ function, which the data constrain relatively little *(p.205)*.
- **Except for $b$, none of the parameters in equation (5d) have any clear interpretation**; $d$ is "the translation of *somewhat*," and $e$ and $f$ are just a way of getting a curved function cheaply *(p.205)*.
- The fitted $b$ is consistently lower than the measured utterance-final lows; only $b + d$ matches them *(p.205)*.
- $k$, $s$, and $l$ have no independent evidence against which their fitted values can be checked *(p.205)*.
- **Many rather different models make nearly equivalent predictions** about these data sets; a good fit is not proof of correctness *(p.202)*.
- Residual patterns for JBP and MYL in the highest and middle pitch ranges are real but not consistent across subjects, and are traced entirely to the $r$-to-$P_0$ function *(p.204)*.
- The Answer-Background relation might be better modeled as a change in $r$ rather than in target height above $r$; this was not pursued *(pp.205-206)*.
- **No systematic evidence was collected on the scaling of L tones**; the H/L symmetry under transform (10) is explicitly speculative, and the experiments could not test the negative side of the transform *(pp.208, 219)*.
- **How two-tone accents behave under changes in prominence is unanswered** *(p.219)*.
- The negative preplanning evidence is "logically rather weak - perhaps we simply looked at the wrong contours or at the wrong measurements" *(p.221)*.
- Thorsen's longer Danish sentences show lowering not confined to the last peak, which may require admitting **limited look-ahead** *(p.222)*.
- The subject pool is four speakers, two of whom are the authors, and the second experiment used only three of them *(pp.172-173)*.
- Emphasis instructions produced simultaneous variation in pitch range, amplitude and rate rather than pure variation in one dimension; the most emphatic renditions were up to twice as long as the least emphatic *(pp.169, 173)*.

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- **Four-tone-level descriptions (Trager & Smith 1951, Pike 1945, Liberman 1975)** have too many degrees of freedom: an F0 rise-fall could be 4-1-4, 4-2-4, or 4-3-4 in successively larger pitch ranges with nothing to distinguish them. "The burden of proof is on the proponents of four-tone systems to show how the claimed information content can be conveyed." *(p.164)*
- **The declination literature as a whole** (both those asserting and those denying declination) measures uncontrolled material and conflates global decay, boundary effects, local downstep, prominence-order correlations, and pitch-range interactions. **"Such measurements are uninterpretable."** *(p.224)*
- **Reports of slope differences among intonation types (Waibel 1979, Thorsen 1980b)** may reflect control of parameters influencing observed downtrends only indirectly, since apparent declination rate is itself a function of pitch range and initial peak value *(pp.224-225)*.
- **Additive log models ('t Hart & Cohen 1973 for Dutch, Lea 1973/1979 for English)** make wrong predictions because they lack any counterpart to the reference level, which changes with pitch range while the baseline stays invariant *(p.225)*.
- **Fujisaki's model** has a negative-going baseline tail whose consequences are false for English (higher pitch range would give lower final values; subdividing a phrase would lower the final value), and no effective mechanism to raise the downstep asymptote with pitch range *(pp.226-227)*.
- **Liberman & Pierrehumbert (1979) / Pierrehumbert (1980)'s own earlier model** - linearly declining baseline with fixed endpoints and $(P_i - b_i)/b_i$ scaling - lacks final peak lowering and lacks $r$, and **"fits the downstep data very badly indeed"** *(p.210)*.
- **Configurational / rise-fall theories (Bolinger 1958, Bailey 1970/1971, Clark 1978, Ladd 1978)** fail on the AB data (unruly, low-R-squared rise and fall relations) and on the downstep data (ratio version equals model 1 without $r$ and fails; difference version fails because successive differences are systematically unequal). Their proponents also **give no instructions for measuring rises and falls** *(pp.210-211)*.
- **Cooper & Sorensen (1981)'s Topline rule** and their first-peak/length effect: the Topline outputs resemble downstepped contours modelable without preplanning; they never compare against models not relying on the final peak; and serious statistical errors leave their fit claim unsupported *(p.221)*.
- **Collier (1975)'s subglottal-pressure account of phrase-level effects:** the available subglottal pressure/F0 data (Collier 1974, Atkinson 1973) show subglottal pressure does not vary on a time course that would permit it to control final lowering *(p.219)*.
- **Fourakis (1980)'s stop-epenthesis rule** for *tense*/*tents*: his own measurements show epenthetic stops are about 25% shorter than underlying ones in the same environment, so a phonological epenthesis rule would need the realization process to know which is which *(p.228)*.

## Design Rationale
- **Two-part final lowering (5a + 5e) rather than the equivalent single transform (6):** chosen to separate the claim of final lowering from the claim that F0 relations are constant ratios once $r$ is subtracted *(p.193)*.
- **Decay to a nonzero asymptote rather than linear stepping in Hz or decay to zero:** dictated by the concave-upward shape of nonfinal step levels *(pp.186, 203)*.
- **Reference level, not downstep constant, carries the pitch-range effect:** the downstep constant is peculiar to downstep and should be insulated from pitch range, whereas the reference level is naturally an aspect of general F0 scaling. The freely-fitted asymptotes in table 7 rose consistently with pitch range, supporting the choice *(pp.190-191)*.
- **The same $P - r$ transform for both experiments:** motivated by generalization, though the Answer-Background experiment gives no direct evidence for a pitch-range-dependent reference level *(p.203)*.
- **Equation (5d) exists to reduce the per-utterance free parameters from two to one** ($r$ and one accent target would otherwise both be needed) *(p.193)*.
- **Constraining $r \ge b + d$:** because utterance-final lows do not rise with pitch range, $b$ is treated as the bottom of the entire system *(pp.191-192)*.
- **Average absolute error rather than squared error:** easier to interpret than Euclidean distance in N-space and does not emphasize outliers *(p.194)*.
- **Permuted berry lists of voiced-throughout names with two syllables between accents:** equalizes word importance, permits averaging out segmental effects on F0, and eases measurement *(p.171)*.
- **Downstep blocked in the AB/BA contours** despite intervening L tones: because the tones are not organized into the requisite pitch accents and because of the intervening intonational phrase boundary; a model with both downstep and final lowering on the second peak would put it lower than observed and fit worse *(p.218)*.
- **Two tones rather than four or one:** four has excessive degrees of freedom; one (only upward excursions from a baseline) cannot represent the full range of English patterns *(p.217)*.

## Testable Properties
- Utterance-final low F0 of a falling accent is nearly invariant for a given speaker across the full spread of pitch ranges, and stable across months and materials *(pp.178, 181)*.
- Low-point SD/mean ratios are smaller than the corresponding peak SD/mean ratios in every A-configuration set except DWS's A in AB *(pp.178-179)*.
- Utterance-medial lows and all B-configuration lows rise with their peaks (positive regression slope in every subject), unlike utterance-final A lows *(p.181)*.
- Nonfinal step levels lie on a curve concave upward, fit by $X_{i+1} - r = s(X_i - r)$ to within about 2 Hz *(pp.186, 188)*.
- The last item of a list falls below the exponential extrapolation of the preceding items, by 4.3 to 27.3 Hz depending on speaker and pitch range, and the shortfall is always positive *(pp.188-189)*.
- A list-final measurement is lower than a nonfinal measurement in the same serial position in a longer list, by more than the shorter-lists-start-lower offset *(p.189)*.
- The fitted downstep asymptote rises monotonically with pitch range instruction for every subject *(p.190)*.
- The initial peak's height above the reference level rises with pitch range for every subject *(p.190)*.
- Downstep constant $s$ lies in .59-.68 and final lowering constant $l$ in .59-.77 for these four speakers *(p.202)*.
- $e > 1$ (wishbone rather than trumpet configuration in peak-1 vs peak-2 plots) *(p.204)*.
- Peak-against-peak regressions have higher R squared than any rise or fall measure on the same data *(p.211)*.
- Raising overall pitch range **raises** an L tone of given prominence (by raising $r$), while raising local prominence on an L\* **lowers** it - opposite directions *(pp.218, 233 note 10)*.
- L\* accents saturate as they approach the baseline; H accents show no observed saturation *(pp.218-219)*.
- Final lowering applied to the last accent regardless of the number of postnuclear syllables implies phonological control; a fixed timing relation to utterance end implies a mechanical consequence *(p.219)*.
- Every parameter of a given object depends only on that object and the immediately previous object of the same type, never on subconstituents or on later material *(p.231)*.

## Relevance to Project
This chapter is the canonical quantitative source for an F0 scaling and declination model in a formant/source-filter synthesizer. Concretely:

- **It gives a directly implementable F0 target model.** For a phrase, pick a reference line $r$; realize each H target as a value a fixed ratio above $r$; realize downstep as $T(P_i) = s \cdot T(P_{i-1})$ on the $(P - r)$ scale; apply final lowering by scaling the final target's distance above $r$ by $l$; and convert back with $P = r + T(P)$. The synthesizer then needs only two speaker constants with clear meaning ($b$, and the pitch-range mapping) plus $s$ and $l$.
- **It supplies concrete default parameter values** to seed a voice: $s \approx 0.6$, $l \approx 0.7$, $k \approx 1.6$, $b$ in the 64-112 Hz range for these speakers, with $b$ interpreted as the speaker's absolute floor.
- **It tells you what *not* to implement.** A linearly declining baseline over the phrase, a global declination slope parameter, additive log models without a reference line, and any model that plans the whole phrase before onset are all shown to misfit. This is directly relevant if the project's prosody module currently uses a "declination slope in semitones per second" parameter: that parameter is the wrong primitive, and this chapter explains why the apparent slope varies with pitch range and initial peak.
- **It is a left-to-right, plan-as-you-go algorithm.** Every computation touches only adjacent elements, so it can run in a streaming synthesis pipeline without lookahead beyond the current and previous accent, and the parameters do not need to change with phrase length.
- **The log transform (model 2) extends the same machinery to L targets**, which a synthesizer needs for question contours, low nuclear accents, and boundary tones: H scales up from $r$, L scales down from $r$ toward the floor $b$, with saturation near $b$ falling out of the transform's shape.
- **It defines a pitch-range control that behaves correctly.** "Speaking up" should raise $r$ and the initial target's height above $r$, while leaving the terminal low essentially fixed - which is what listeners expect and what naive gain-on-F0 scaling gets wrong.
- **It supplies validation targets.** The parameter tables and the mean-absolute-error figures (1.5-7.8 Hz) give a concrete accuracy bar for a re-implementation, and the testable properties above are directly usable as unit-test assertions on a generated contour.
