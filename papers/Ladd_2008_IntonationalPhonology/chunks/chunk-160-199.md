## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Phrase Accent (pp. 142-147)
- In Pierrehumbert's description, falling nuclear accents in English are analyzed as H* or L+H* pitch accent + L phrase accent + L% boundary tone
- Ladd argues that nuclear accents consist of two distinct phonological elements: an accentual element and a peripheral element (edge tone)
- **Postnuclear accents**: tonal events after the nuclear accent that are related to stress/prominence. In short utterances, the contour appears unitary (e.g., "fall-rise"), but in longer utterances it becomes clear that postnuclear elements seek metrically prominent syllables

### Eastern European Question Tune (EEQT) (pp. 145-146)
- Used in Hungarian, Greek, Romanian, Serbo-Croatian
- Consists of L* (or L*+H) nuclear pitch accent + H_L% edge tone sequence
- The H_L% sequence follows the main accent and is phrase-edge associated
- In some languages (Hungarian), H_L% is invariably at the phrase edge; in others (Greek, Romanian), the H may associate with the stressed syllable of a following word

### Postnuclear Accent Behavior (pp. 143-146)
- The calling contour: H* accent tone followed by downstepped !H; the !H 'prefers' to occur on the most prominent postnuclear syllable
- In falling-rising tunes, the low postnuclear turning point aligns with lexically stressed syllables following the nucleus

### Semantic Compositionality (pp. 147-150)
- Pierrehumbert and Hirschberg (1990) attempt to provide compositional meanings for elements of Pierrehumbert's analysis
- Pitch accents convey information about discourse referents, modifiers, predicates, and relationships
- Phrase accents and boundary tones are similarly determined
- Meaning of L*+H_L_H% must be derivable from L*+H accent + L phrase accent + H% boundary tone
- This approach is based on the 'Linguist's Theory of Intonational Meaning'

### Gradient vs. Categorical Variation (pp. 151-156)
- A fundamental problem: what looks like gradient paralinguistic variation within a single category to one analyst may look like two distinct phonological categories to another
- Example: German contrastive vs. non-contrastive topic accent -- Braun (2006) found no clear dichotomy between patterns; contrastive accents had later, higher peaks and longer durations, but no clear categorical boundary
- High Rising Terminals (HRT): L*_H_H% vs H*_H_H% distinction may be gradient rather than categorical
- Ladd suggests intonational categories may have small inventories but be subject to meaningful gradient modification

### Core Tones vs. Edge Tones (pp. 157)
- **Core tones**: the tonal events that make up the body of the F0 contour; correspond to pitch accents in English, lexical tones in Chinese/Japanese
- **Edge tones**: tonal events at boundaries of prosodic domains; signal phrasing, statement/question distinctions
- In Japanese: pitch accents are lexically specified (always HL pattern), edge tones are postlexical
- In English: both types are postlexical/intonational

### Alignment vs. Association (p. 179)
- **Association**: an abstract phonological property of 'belonging together' -- the phonological linking of a tone to a syllable
- **Alignment**: a phonetic property -- the relative timing of F0 events and segmental landmarks
- Association entails no detailed predictions about alignment; a tone associated with a syllable may peak early, late, or even outside the syllable boundaries

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Gussenhoven's Three-Tone System (p. 148)
- Three basic 'tones' (roughly equivalent to accent types in Pierrehumbert): H*L (fall), L*H (rise), H*LH (fall-rise)
- Subject to 'modifications': stylisation (section 3.2.3.1) and delay (section 2.2.3)
- Each tone and modification assigned a basic meaning:
  - Fall (H*L): introduces entity into 'background' / shared knowledge
  - Rise (L*H): non-committal about whether entity is in background (basic question intonation)
  - Fall-rise (H*LH): 'selects' entity from the background

### Pierrehumbert and Hirschberg (1990) Compositional Semantics (pp. 149-150)
- Assigns meanings to the six Pierrehumbert pitch accents, two phrase accents, and two boundary tones
- Claims intonational meaning is compositional: meaning of whole contour derivable from meanings of individual elements
- Mark Steedman (1991, 2000) provides related account: pitch accent choice conveys information about individual discourse referents

### Table 4.1: Word-Level Prosodic Typology (p. 165)
- Two independent dimensions:
  - **Lexical typology**: lexical pitch vs. postlexical pitch only
  - **Phonetic typology**: stress accent vs. non-stress accent
- Four resulting types:
  - Lexical pitch + stress accent: Swedish
  - Lexical pitch + non-stress accent: Japanese
  - Postlexical pitch only + stress accent: English
  - Postlexical pitch only + non-stress accent: Bengali

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### Pitch Range Modification (pp. 158-159)
- Paralinguistic pitch range modification (expansion/contraction for emotion) is orthogonal to tonal specification
- A tone sequence M...H...H...L...M can be realized in normal or expanded pitch range (Fig. 4.2)
- This effect is identical in tone and non-tone languages
- Pitch range modification does not constitute evidence against sequential tonal phonology

### Downdrift/Downstep in Tone Languages (p. 160)
- In Hausa: H...L...H...L...H -- each H is realized at a progressively lower level (downdrift)
- In questions: downdrift is suspended -- H tones are all realized at approximately the same level
- This is produced locally by operation or non-operation of the downstep rule at successive points

### Focus and Pitch Range in Chinese (pp. 161-162)
- Garding (1987): 'early focus' sentences have a grid (pitch range) that narrows abruptly after the focus
- Normal 'late focus' sentences maintain constant pitch range throughout
- AM interpretation: pitch range modifications occur at specific points in the utterance (Fig. 4.4 shows idealized tonal space for modeling focus effects)

### Chinese Sentence-Level Tunes (pp. 162-163)
- Shen (1990) proposes three intonational tunes:
  - Tune I (declaratives): mid start, rise to mid-high peak, fall to low ending
  - Tune II (yes-no questions): mid-high start, rise to high peak, drop but ending high/mid-high
  - Tune III (alternative questions): mid-high start, rise to high peak, step down, ending low
- Can be interpreted as combinations of pitch range specifications and final lowering at well-defined points

### Alignment Data for English (pp. 172-173)
- Ashby (1978): For high-fall nuclear accents, peak aligned ~35 ms after vowel onset with very little variation
- Low of high-fall on sentence-final monosyllables: ~150 ms after vowel onset
- Low-rise contours: low plateau whose length is a linear function of voicing time after vowel onset, followed by rise with fixed slope of 6.7 octaves/sec

### Alignment Effects of Prosodic Context (pp. 173)
- Silverman and Pierrehumbert (1990): prenuclear accent peak aligned proportionally earlier when closer to following nuclear-accented word (backward time pressure)
- Speech rate effects: prenuclear peaks aligned later in syllable when speech rate increased
- Prieto, van Santen, and Hirschberg (1995): accent peaks in word-final stressed syllables aligned earlier than in initial or medial positions; phrase-final nuclear accents aligned earlier than phrase-medial prenuclear accents

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

### Accent Types and Pragmatic Function
- H* signaling 'Answer' (new/rheme); L+H* signaling 'Background' (given/theme) -- proposed by Steedman (2000)
- L+H* (early alignment) vs L*+H (late alignment): the perceptual boundary may be categorical (Kohler 1987, Pierrehumbert and Steele 1989, Rietveld and Gussenhoven 1995)
- Three-way distinction proposed by Kohler: early aligned 'given', later aligned 'new', emphatic (aligned even later)

### Swedish Word Accents under Emphasis (pp. 166-167)
- Accent 1 (HL fall is early): with increasing emphasis (R=14 to R=20), accentual H, L, and phrase tone H all raised in F0
- Accent 2 (H* is early, L is later): with increasing emphasis, H tones raised but L is lowered
- This asymmetry suggests the L in Accent 2 is "more real" (lexically specified) than in Accent 1

## Alignment and Timing

### Segmental Anchoring (pp. 174-178)
- **Key finding**: F0 turning points are aligned with specific segmental landmarks rather than having fixed slopes or durations
- Arvaniti, Ladd, and Mennen (1998) on Greek prenuclear accents:
  - Beginning of accentual rise: aligned at beginning of accented syllable
  - End of accentual rise (peak): aligned at beginning of following unstressed vowel
  - Rise duration is NOT fixed -- it depends on the interval between these two segmental anchors
  - Slope of rise is adjusted according to available time
  - F0 levels at turning points are roughly constant per speaker (Fig. 5.1)
- This has been replicated across many languages: English, Japanese, Catalan, Chinese
- The term "segmental anchoring" was coined by Ladd et al. (1999)

### Segmental Anchoring and Phonological Conditioning (pp. 176-178)
- In Dutch: prenuclear peak alignment depends on phonological vowel length (tense vs. lax) -- peak for long vowels is late in the vowel, for short vowels it's in the following consonant
- Ladd, Mennen, and Schepman (2000) suggested this is alignment with syllable edges rather than pure vowel duration effect
- But Schepman, Lickley, and Ladd (2006) found the vowel length effect is also present with nuclear accents, complicating syllable-edge interpretation

### Association vs. Alignment Distinction (p. 179)
- Association is phonological (tone "belongs to" a syllable)
- Alignment is phonetic (exact timing of F0 turning point relative to segments)
- Analogy to VOT: phonological voicing category has no absolute VOT value, but VOT varies lawfully by language, place, etc.

### Compression, Truncation, and Alignment Effects (p. 180)
- Cases where alignment is conditioned by phonological factors: accent type in a specific language, phonological vowel length
- Cases where alignment is affected by nearby prosodic events: stress clash, upcoming boundaries
- These effects on alignment parallel phonetic conditioning of segment duration

## Cross-linguistic Data

### Languages Discussed
- **Greek**: EEQT (H_L% edge tone), prenuclear accent peaks show segmental anchoring
- **Hungarian**: EEQT with H_L% invariably at phrase edge
- **Romanian, Serbo-Croatian**: EEQT variants
- **German**: contrastive vs. non-contrastive topic ('hat pattern' / 'bridge accent'); alignment differs between Northern and Southern German
- **Dutch**: IPO Type 1/Type 3 rise distinction based on alignment; calling contour with multiple postnuclear !H; vowel length affects alignment
- **Swedish**: Accent 1 vs. Accent 2 word accent distinction is fundamentally about alignment of the same HL tonal sequence; emphasis affects Accent 1 and 2 differently
- **Serbo-Croatian**: falling vs. rising accent; choice of accent type partly lexical, partly postlexical
- **Japanese**: lexical accent marked only by pitch movement (non-stress accent); pitch accents always HL
- **Bengali**: postlexical pitch only with non-stress accent
- **Chinese**: lexical tones; sentence-level 'tunes' interpretable as pitch range modifications + edge tones; focus signaled by pitch range narrowing
- **Hausa**: downdrift in statements, suspension of downdrift in questions; phrase-final tone modification
- **Kipare**: three sentence-level tunes (declarative, incredulous question, yes-no question)
- **Yoruba**: only edge tones; no core tones (or edge tones are completely predictable from last core tone)
- **Italian (Neapolitan)**: categorical alignment distinction for statement vs. question

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation: Segmental Anchoring Model
For TTS F0 generation, the segmental anchoring findings suggest:
1. **Tonal targets should be aligned to specific segmental landmarks** (syllable onset, vowel onset, following unstressed vowel onset) rather than using fixed durations or slopes for pitch movements
2. **Rise/fall duration is variable** -- it depends on the distance between the segmental anchor points, not on an inherent pitch movement duration
3. **Slope is adjusted** to maintain roughly constant F0 levels at turning points while accommodating variable durations between anchors
4. **Prenuclear peaks align earlier** when under time pressure from nearby accents or boundaries (tonal crowding effect)
5. **Nuclear peaks are generally earlier in the syllable** than prenuclear peaks

### Pitch Range Modification for Focus/Emphasis
- Focus marking can be implemented as a pitch range modification occurring at a specific point (after the focused constituent)
- Early focus: pitch range narrows abruptly after the focus word; subsequent tones compressed
- Late/default focus: pitch range remains relatively constant throughout
- This is independent of the tonal string itself -- same tone sequence, different pitch range envelope

### Postnuclear Accent Placement
- Postnuclear tonal events (phrase accents, downstepped H in calling contour) seek metrically strong syllables
- Implementation should check for lexically stressed postnuclear syllables and align postnuclear tonal targets with them
- The L phrase accent in falling-rising patterns attaches to secondary-stressed postnuclear syllables if available

### Categorical vs. Gradient Distinctions
- For a TTS system, the distinction between categorical accent types (H* vs L+H* vs L*+H) should be maintained in the phonological representation
- Gradient variation (degree of emphasis, attitude) can be layered on top as continuous parameter modification (pitch range, alignment shift)
- This suggests a two-layer F0 model: categorical tonal string + continuous paralinguistic modulation

### Downstep and Declination
- In tone languages, downstep is local (triggered at each H-L-H sequence point)
- Suspension of downstep signals questions
- For English, the same mechanism operates via Pierrehumbert's downstep rule (section 2.4)
- Implementation: track a "current H reference level" that decreases after each triggering sequence, and can be reset (for questions/emphasis)
