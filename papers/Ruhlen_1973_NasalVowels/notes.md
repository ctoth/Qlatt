# Nasal Vowels

## One-Sentence Summary

Cross-linguistic survey of nasal vowel systems in 50+ languages establishing universals about nasal vowel inventories, their diachronic origins from nasal consonant loss, and synchronic constraints on how nasal vowel systems relate to oral vowel inventories.

## Problem Addressed

What universal constraints govern nasal vowel systems across languages? The paper investigates: What defines a nasal vowel? Why do nasal vowels arise? What are their historical sources? How do nasal vowel systems change and disappear? Prior work (Issatschenko 1937, Ferguson 1963, Greenberg 1966) proposed universals but these needed systematic verification across a larger language sample.

## Key Contributions

1. **Three-level NV taxonomy**: Distinguishes phonetic NVs (allophonic nasalization, e.g. English [m&aelig;n]), phonemic NVs (contrastive but derivable from morphophonemic structure, e.g. French [b&otilde;]), and phonological NVs (truly underlying, e.g. Portuguese [v&iacute;])
2. **Verified and refined Ferguson's universals**: Confirmed that NV inventories are always subsets of (or equal to) oral vowel inventories; NV frequency of occurrence is generally lower than oral vowels; NVs almost always arise from loss of primary nasal consonants
3. **Inventory asymmetry patterns**: High/low vowel height distinctions are maintained in NV systems even when mid-vowel distinctions are lost (e.g. many languages contrast /e/ vs /&epsilon;/ orally but have only one front mid NV)
4. **Diachronic pathway**: Established a five-stage model of NV evolution using French as exemplar: (1) no nasalization -> (2) allophonic nasalization before NCs -> (3) NC loss creates contrast -> (4) denasalization before remaining NCs -> (5) stable NV system
5. **Comprehensive appendix**: Vowel inventories (oral and nasal) with nasal consonant inventories for 50+ languages across all major language families

## Methodology

- Cross-linguistic survey of 50+ languages spanning Indo-European, Niger-Congo (Kwa), Algonquian, Athapaskan, Iroquoian, Siouan, Otomanguean, Tupian, Arawakan, Choco, and others
- Data drawn from published phonological descriptions
- Synchronic inventory comparison (oral vs nasal vowel systems)
- Diachronic reconstruction of nasalization pathways

## Key Universals (with evaluation)

| Universal | Source | Status | Notes |
|-----------|--------|--------|-------|
| Every language has >= 1 primary nasal consonant | Ferguson #1 | Near-universal | Exceptions: some NW Coast languages (Thompson & Thompson 1972), some Kwa languages where NCs derive from /b, d/ before NVs |
| No NVs without nasal consonants | Ferguson #10 | Near-universal | Corollary of #1; Kwa languages may be counterexamples if NCs are derived |
| NV count <= oral vowel count | Ferguson #11 | Confirmed | Deducible from diachronic origin (Greenberg 1966) |
| NV frequency < oral vowel frequency | Ferguson #12 | Generally true | Individual NVs can exceed oral counterpart frequency due to merger |
| NV/oral neutralization occurs next to NCs | Ferguson #13 | Confirmed | Synchronic consequence of diachronic denasalization |
| NVs arise from loss of primary NCs | Ferguson #14 | Near-universal | Exceptions exist: spontaneous nasalization, nasalization from laryngeals |

### Inventory asymmetry pattern
Languages that contrast /e/ vs /&epsilon;/ (or /o/ vs /&otilde;/) in oral vowels frequently neutralize this distinction in the nasal system, having only one front mid NV. Examples: French, Breton, Portuguese, Yoruba, Ogoni, Gun, Mazahua.

### Height asymmetry
Languages with three oral height levels (high, mid, low) often reduce to two NV heights (high and low), dropping the mid series. Examples: Akan, Assiniboine, Baule, Ioway-Oto, Nupe, Temoayan Otomi, Scot's Gaelic, Tolowa, Yankton-Teton.

## Diachronic Pathway (French Exemplar)

Five stages in the development of nasal vowels:

| Stage | Word-final | Pre-consonantal | Description |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| 1. /bon/, /bona/ | oral + NC | oral + NC | No nasalization |
| 2. /b&otilde;n/, /b&otilde;na/ | NV + NC | NV + NC | Allophonic nasalization before all NCs |
| 3. /b&otilde;/, /b&otilde;na/ | NV (NC lost) | NV + NC | Word-final NC deletion creates contrast |
| 4. /b&otilde;/, /bona/ | NV | oral + NC | Denasalization before remaining NCs |
| 5. /b&otilde;/, /bon/ | NV | oral + NC | Stable system (modern French) |

## Sources of Nasal Vowels

### Primary source (near-universal)
Loss of a following nasal consonant: VN -> V&#x0303; (accounts for vast majority of NV systems)

### Secondary sources (exceptions to Ferguson #14)
1. **Spontaneous nasalization**: Sundanese /a/ -> [&atilde;] in certain morphological contexts (Anderson 1972; Robins 1953, 1957) — no historical NC
2. **Nasalization from laryngeals**: East Gurage (Semitic) vowels nasalized near historical laryngeals (Hetzron 1969)
3. **Contact/borrowing**: Can introduce NVs without native diachronic development

## Implementation Details

### Relevance to Klatt synthesis
This paper is primarily a phonological/typological study rather than an acoustic study. Its implementation relevance is indirect:

1. **Nasal vowel inventory design**: When modeling languages with NV systems, the inventory asymmetries documented here constrain which NV targets need to be defined. Not every oral vowel needs a nasal counterpart.

2. **Allophonic nasalization rules**: The paper's three-level taxonomy (phonetic/phonemic/phonological NVs) maps to different rule types in the synthesizer:
   - Phonetic NVs: Coarticulatory rules that gradually open the velar port before/after nasal consonants (language-specific degree)
   - Phonemic NVs: Postlexical rules that nasalize vowels based on morphophonemic context
   - Phonological NVs: Underlying inventory targets with full nasal coupling

3. **Cross-language nasalization strength**: The paper notes that allophonic nasalization strength varies by language (Portuguese: strong allophonic nasalization; French: weak allophonic nasalization). This implies the velar port coupling parameter in coarticulatory rules should be language-specific, not universal.

4. **Vowel-height interaction**: The inventory asymmetries (mid-vowel distinctions lost in NV systems) relate to the acoustic finding that nasal coupling shifts F1 and can blur height distinctions (see House & Stevens 1956, Beddor 1986 in collection).

## Limitations

- No acoustic measurements or spectral data — purely phonological/distributional
- OCR quality of the ERIC scan is poor in places (garbled characters)
- Some language data is from older sources that may have been revised
- The appendix vowel charts use inconsistent IPA conventions across sources
- Does not address the acoustic mechanisms underlying the universals (see House & Stevens 1956, Chen 1997, Maeda 1982 for acoustic work)

## Testable Properties

1. **For any language with NVs**: the NV inventory should be a subset of (or equal to) the oral vowel inventory
2. **Mid-vowel quality distinctions** (e.g. /e/ vs /&epsilon;/) should neutralize in the NV system before high/low distinctions do
3. **Allophonic nasalization** before nasal consonants should be present in all languages, but its strength should vary
4. **NV systems should historically trace to loss of nasal consonants** in the vast majority of cases

## Figures of Interest

The paper contains no figures in the traditional sense. The appendix (pp. 23-26) provides vowel inventory charts for 50+ languages showing oral vowels, nasal vowels, and nasal consonant inventories in tabular form. These are the primary data contribution.

## Collection Cross-References

### Already in Collection
- **House & Stevens 1956** ("Analog Studies of the Nasalization of Vowels") — cited in this paper; provides the acoustic model (F1 amplitude reduction, bandwidth increase) that explains why mid-vowel distinctions are vulnerable to nasalization
- **Beddor 1986** ("Perceptual Constraints and Phonological Change: Nasal Vowel Height") — extends this work with perceptual experiments showing how nasal coupling shifts perceived vowel height
- **Chen 1997** ("Nasalized Vowel Acoustics") — acoustic measurements of nasalized vowels relevant to understanding the universals described here
- **Maeda 1982** ("Vowel Nasalization Cues") — computational model of nasal coupling effects on vowel spectra
- **Fant 1960** ("Acoustic Theory of Speech Production") — cited for the acoustic theory underlying nasal pole-zero pairs

### Conceptual Links (not citation-based)
