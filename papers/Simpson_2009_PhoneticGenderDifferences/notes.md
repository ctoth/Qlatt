# Phonetic Differences Between Male and Female Speech

**Authors:** Adrian P. Simpson
**Year:** 2009
**Venue:** Language and Linguistics Compass, 3/2, 621-640
**DOI:** 10.1111/j.1749-818x.2009.00125.x

## One-Sentence Summary
A comprehensive review of phonetic differences between male and female speech covering phonation/pitch, voice quality, vowel formants, vocal tract dimensions, VOT, duration, reduction, and children's speech, critically evaluating biophysical versus sociophonetic explanations for each difference.

## Problem Addressed
Why do male and female voices sound different, and to what extent are these differences attributable to anatomy/physiology versus learned sociophonetic behaviors?

## Key Contributions
- Synthesizes research on gender differences across five domains: phonation/pitch, vowel articulation, pitch-articulation interaction, VOT, and duration/reduction
- Demonstrates that a simple uniform scaling factor cannot map male-to-female formants -- the scaling is non-uniform and vowel-dependent
- Shows that many gender differences (pitch range, voice quality, vowel space size, VOT differences) emerge before puberty in children with anatomically similar vocal tracts, implicating learned sociophonetic behavior
- Critically evaluates articulatory, acoustic, and sociophonetic explanations for each difference

## Methodology
Review article synthesizing empirical findings from multiple studies across languages (English, German, Swedish, Dutch, French, Chinese, Creek, Jamaican English). No original data collection.

## Key Equations

No formal equations. Key quantitative relationships described narratively:

- Semitone conversion: doubling of frequency = one octave = 12 semitones (100-200 Hz = 200-400 Hz in semitones)
- Male tongue [i]-to-[a] distance ~11% greater than female (from Goldstein 1980 growth data)

## Implementation Details

### Voice Quality
- Males use more creaky voice than females (Henton & Bladon 1988 for British English)
- Females have breathier voice quality (Klatt & Klatt 1990 for American English)
- Both differences appear to be sociophonetic (learned) rather than anatomically determined
- Titze (1989) model predicts female vocal folds never fully close during each cycle, producing constant airflow (breathy voice)
- Creaky voice: vocal folds close tightly at back, vibrate at low frequency (generally below 80 Hz)

### Pitch Range
- When measured in semitones (perceptually appropriate), males and females do NOT differ significantly in pitch range (Henton 1989)
- Earlier claims of larger female pitch range were artifacts of linear Hz representation
- Some evidence males may have slightly larger pitch range (Herbst 1964, 1969 for German)
- Female speakers produce wider pitch range in interrogative utterances (Haan & van Heuven 1999, 2002)

### Vowel Formants -- Non-Uniform Scaling
- Female formants are NOT a simple uniform scalar multiple of male formants
- Each vowel requires a different scaling constant to map male-to-female
- Back rounded vowels [o], [u] show relatively small male-female differences (Fant suggests compensatory female articulation)
- Female vowel space is larger than male vowel space (Hillenbrand et al. 1995 data)
- The [i] vowel differs from male chiefly in F1; [a] differs chiefly in both F1 and F2
- Explanations for non-uniform scaling:
  1. Anatomical: longer male pharynx (Fant 1966, 1975; Nordstrom 1977)
  2. Articulatory speed: female tongue travels 11% less distance for same vowel pair
  3. F0-formant interaction: wider harmonic spacing in females may affect perceived vowel space (Diehl et al. 1996)
  4. Sociophonetic: females speak more clearly, producing larger acoustic contrasts (Henton 1995)

### Pitch-Articulation Interaction (Fig. 4)
- Male F0=110 Hz gives harmonics at 220, 330, 440, 550, 660, 770, 880 Hz (closely spaced)
- Female F0=200 Hz gives harmonics at 400, 600, 800, 1000 Hz (widely spaced)
- Wider female harmonic spacing means formant frequencies are less precisely defined acoustically
- This may account for larger acoustic vowel space as a compensatory mechanism

### VOT
- Females generally have longer VOTs for voiceless plosives /p, t, k/ than males
- Children (especially girls at age 11) show the largest VOT contrast between voiced/voiceless pairs
- VOT varies with menstrual cycle: at high hormone levels, females exhibit significantly longer VOTs for fortis plosives and shorter VOTs for lenis plosives, increasing the voicing contrast
- Posterior opening of female vocal folds (Bless & Abbs 1983) may increase air pressure during closure, delaying voicing onset

### Duration and Reduction
- Male vowels ~11% shorter than female vowels consistently across all categories (Simpson 1998, German)
- Female speakers produce greater durational differences between stressed and unstressed vowels
- Males show higher speaking rate, more vowel reduction to schwa, and more likely to elide (Byrd 1992; Whiteside 1996)
- Female "clearer" speech may relate to role as primary care-givers/language input to children

### Children (Pre-Puberty)
- Preadolescent boys and girls have anatomically similar vocal tracts
- Girls have higher F1 and F2 values than boys for most vowel categories (Busby & Plant 1995; Lee et al. 1999)
- F0 differences between boys and girls emerge from age 12 (puberty onset)
- Voice quality judgments (clear/dull, soft/loud, high/low pitch) differ between boys and girls even at ages 7-8 (Gunzburger et al. 1987)
- Gender identification from isolated vowels: only slightly better than chance; from sentences: >70% accuracy

## Results Summary
This is a review paper, not an empirical study. Key synthesis:
- Gender differences in speech arise from a combination of biophysical inevitabilities (vocal fold mass, vocal tract length) and learned sociophonetic behaviors
- The sociophonetic component is substantial: many differences appear before anatomical divergence at puberty
- No single acoustic parameter reliably separates male and female speech when considered in isolation
- Non-uniform formant scaling is well-documented but poorly explained -- articulatory, acoustic, and sociophonetic factors all contribute

## Limitations
- Review article -- no original data
- Heavily weighted toward English and German; other languages discussed but less thoroughly
- The relative contributions of biophysical vs. sociophonetic factors remain unresolved for most phenomena
- No quantitative model for male-female voice quality differences is provided
- Specific formant values for male vs female across vowels are not tabulated (references Hillenbrand et al. 1995)

## Testable Properties
- Female formant frequencies should NOT be derivable from male formants by a single uniform scaling constant
- Male vowel durations should be approximately 11% shorter than female vowel durations
- In semitone representation, male and female pitch ranges should be approximately equal
- Female VOTs for voiceless plosives should be longer than male VOTs
- Pre-pubescent boys and girls should show formant differences despite similar vocal tract dimensions
- Female speakers should show larger acoustic vowel space area than males

## Relevance to Project
For Qlatt's voice preset system, this paper establishes that:
1. Male and female voice presets cannot use a simple uniform formant scaling factor -- each vowel needs independent male/female formant targets
2. Voice quality differences (breathiness, creakiness) are important gender cues alongside F0 and formant frequencies, mapping to Klatt parameters TL, OQ, AH, and B1
3. The F0-formant interaction means that at higher F0 (female range), formant definition degrades due to wider harmonic spacing, which affects synthesis quality
4. Duration rules should incorporate gender-specific adjustments (~11% longer for females), and VOT rules should be gender-aware
5. Many gender differences are sociophonetic rather than anatomical, meaning they are parameters of speaking style rather than fixed physical constraints
