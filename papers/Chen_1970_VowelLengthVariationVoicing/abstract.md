# Abstract

Spectrographic measurements from 376 utterances of four languages (English, French, Russian, Korean) demonstrate that vowel duration varies as a function of the voicing of the following consonant, with vowels being longer before voiced consonants. This is argued to be a language-universal phenomenon, though its magnitude is language-specific (English shows the largest effect with a voiceless/voiced ratio of 0.61). Several articulatory hypotheses are evaluated: articulatory distance, energy expenditure, perceptual distance, compensatory temporal adjustment, laryngeal adjustment, and closure transition rate. Photoelectric lip-tracking experiments show that closure movement velocity is faster for voiceless stops (mean 0.87) than voiced stops (mean 0.69), with a mean time difference of 27 ms closely matching the cross-linguistic vowel duration differences for most languages.

## Interpretation

This paper provides the foundational cross-linguistic evidence for voicing-conditioned vowel duration rules in speech synthesis. The key implementable finding is that English vowels before voiceless consonants are approximately 61-69% the duration of vowels before voiced consonants. The effect is strongest in simple CVC words with final stops and weakest in consonant clusters. For a Klatt synthesizer, this translates to a duration multiplier of approximately 0.8 before voiceless obstruents and 1.2 before voiced obstruents (relative to a neutral baseline), yielding the target ratio of ~0.67. The paper also establishes that the effect propagates through intervening sonorants, which is relevant for implementing duration rules in complex codas.
