# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

This paper examines spectral correlates of stress and accent in a corpus of sentences with varying
focus, produced by four speakers of American English. Analyses of the spectrum at vowel centre show
a clear effect on the spectral tilt - i.e. more energy at higher frequencies relative to energy
nearer to the fundamental - when the vowel is in a nuclear-accented syllable. However, unlike in the
Dutch corpus examined by Sluijter & van Heuven, there was no difference in spectral tilt between
vowels in stressed versus unstressed syllables in the absence of the accompanying intonational
prominence contrast. These results lend support to the hypothesis that syllables marking focal
prominence are phonated in a more emphatic way than other syllables. That is, accented syllables may
be louder and not simply intonationally prominent, but this effect does not distinguish an
independent lexically specified level of 'primary stress' between the intonational prominence of
accent and the basic rhythmic contrast between strong (full) and weak (reduced) syllables.

## Our Interpretation

The English literature on stress could never separate lexical stress from pitch accent because the
two almost always co-occur, so Sluijter & van Heuven's Dutch finding that spectral balance signals
lexical stress had no clean English test. Campbell and Beckman build one, using `Baddle` versus
`Badd-Ellis` in accented and postnuclear positions, and find the spectral tilt effect belongs
entirely to nuclear accent: accented vowels carry considerably more energy above ERB channel 60,
while stressed and unstressed vowels in the same intonational context are indistinguishable. For a
source-filter synthesizer this says prominence must be implemented as a source-spectrum change on
accented syllables specifically, since duration and F0 manipulation alone will not produce it, and
that H2-H1 is the wrong control parameter because low-tone creak captures it.
