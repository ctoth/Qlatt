## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Stress vs. Accent (Summary, p. 61)
Stress is "acoustic salience" -- a complex of properties related to greater force of articulation (increased intensity, duration, shallower spectral tilt). Pitch accents are elements of the intonation contour that do not in themselves represent the acoustic realisation of stress. They serve as an indirect cue to syllable prominence because they must be associated with metrically strong syllables.

### Levels vs. Configurations Debate (pp. 62-64)
The classic theoretical controversy in intonation research (1950s-1980s): whether the primitives of intonation are distinctive pitch levels (Low, Mid, High, Overhigh in the American structuralist analysis) or pitch configurations (rise, fall). The AM theory resolves this by:
1. Reducing distinctive levels to two (H and L)
2. Acknowledging the existence of pitch accents as units

### Tonal Space (pp. 74-75)
Ladd's informal construct (analogous to "vowel space") -- the subset of the overall speaking range available for realising tonal distinctions at any given point in the utterance. Tonal space changes continuously during the utterance due to declination. Related terms: Clements's "tone level frame", Pierrehumbert's "transform space", Beckman's "register".

### Downstep (pp. 76-78)
The stepwise lowering of pitch (or of the tonal space) at specific pitch accents. In Pierrehumbert's AM model, declination is analyzed as the result of repeated local downstep events rather than a separate global overlay component. In a downstep series, each accent peak is a constant proportion of the previous peak.

### Nuclear Accent (p. 90)
In Beckman and Pierrehumbert's revised analysis, "nuclear accent" is defined as the last accent of the intermediate phrase. The ToBI training materials instruct users to think of the nuclear accent as more prominent than prenuclear accents.

### Edge Tones (pp. 88, 100)
Two types in Pierrehumbert's original analysis:
- **Phrase accents** (H-, L-): free-standing unstarred tones after the last pitch accent, serving as edge tones for the intermediate phrase
- **Boundary tones** (H%, L%): single tones associated with the very end of the intonation phrase

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Pierrehumbert's Finite-State Grammar (p. 89, Figure 3.1)
A formal grammar that generates all legal English tunes. Structure:
```
Boundary tone -> Pitch accent(s) -> Phrase accent -> Boundary tone
```
- Initial boundary tone: H% or L%
- Pitch accents: H*, L*, L*+H, L+H*, H*+L, H+L*, H*+H (original 7; reduced to 6 in revised standard)
- Phrase accent: H- or L-
- Final boundary tone: H% or L%

All possible sequences of pitch accents and edge tones are legal (free combination).

### Revised Beckman-Pierrehumbert Analysis (p. 88)
Adds the intermediate phrase (ip) level between pitch accent and intonation phrase (IP):
- Each IP consists of one or more ips
- Boundary tone T% = edge tone of IP
- Phrase accent T- = edge tone of ip
- In an IP of a single ip, no practical difference from original

### Bruce and Garding's Realisation Model (pp. 73-74, Figure 2.4)
Distinguishes between:
- **Focal lines**: horizontal lines marking the top and bottom of the speaker's range (unchanged)
- **Statement lines**: gradually declining topline and baseline connecting peaks and valleys of non-focal word accents
- The space between statement lines = **tonal space** (shrinks over utterance due to declination)

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### Peak Height Consistency (pp. 67-69)
Bruce's work on Swedish: the "word-accent maximum" (highest F0 associated with accented syllable) is the most reliable anchor for phonetic description. Its F0 level relative to the accented syllable is effectively constant in a given utterance context. The invariant features are the turning points, not the transitions between them.

### Constant Proportion in Downstep (pp. 78-79)
In Pierrehumbert's model, each accent peak in a downstep series is a constant proportion of the previous peak. This has been confirmed for:
- English (Liberman and Pierrehumbert 1984)
- Dutch (van den Berg, Gussenhoven, and Rietveld 1992)
- German (Truckenbrodt 2002)

The downstep ratio k is approximately 0.6 (from Pierrehumbert 1980).

### Declination as Downstep vs. Global Trend (p. 76, Figure 2.5)
Two competing models:
- (a) Gradual declination: overall contour slope directly specified (Fujisaki, IPO)
- (b) Iterated downstep: apparent slope emerges from repeated local lowering events at each accent

AM models prefer (b) -- target values generated left-to-right with local lookback only.

### Final Lowering (pp. 79-80)
Liberman and Pierrehumbert found the final accent in a series is invariably lower than the constant-proportion downstep model predicts. They attribute this to two distinct effects: downstep AND final lowering, each making its own contribution.

### Pitch Level Control Precision (pp. 69)
Standard deviations of sentence-initial accent peaks for English speakers average only 7.5 Hz (range 3.7-11.5 Hz), comparable to Yoruba speakers controlling lexical level tones (14.8 Hz average). This demonstrates English speakers control pitch level at least as precisely as tone language speakers.

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

### Pierrehumbert's Original 7 Pitch Accent Types (p. 92)
H*, L*, L+H*, L*+H, H+L*, H*+L, H*+H

### Revised Standard (Beckman and Pierrehumbert 1986): 6 Types
H*+H eliminated. Remaining six:
- **Monotonal**: H* (local peak), L* (local valley)
- **Bitonal**: L+H* (rise to peak on accented syllable), L*+H (rise from accented syllable), H*+L (fall from accented syllable), H+L* (movement from higher preceding to low accented syllable)

### Phonetic Descriptions of Key Accents (pp. 92-96)
- **H***: Local F0 peak aligned with accented syllable. Two H* accents in sequence show a "sag" (local jump) rather than straight transition (pp. 92, 112)
- **L***: Local F0 valley on accented syllable
- **L+H***: Rise to H* with preceding L target; differs from plain H* by "more substantial rising pitch movement" leading to H* target (p. 96)
- **L*+H**: L target on accented syllable, rise follows (aligned later than L+H*)
- **H+L***: Movement from higher pitch to low accented syllable
- **H*+L**: "Terraced" downstepping contour; now written H+!H* in ToBI standard (p. 100)

### Downstep Notation (pp. 97-100)
- Original Pierrehumbert: downstep triggered by H...L...H sequences in pitch accents
- Revised Beckman-Pierrehumbert: all bitonal accents (H*+L, H+L*, L*+H, L+H*) trigger downstep on following accents
- Ladd's alternative (1983a): downstep as independently selectable feature, notated with ! (e.g., !H*)

## Alignment and Timing

### Peak Alignment as Primary Cue (pp. 67-68)
Bruce's key discovery: a precisely aligned peak is the most reliable correlate of Swedish word accent, not a rise or fall. The pitch configurations that span the accented syllable are transitions from one phonologically specified turning point to another. The F0 value at the turning point is effectively constant; the movements between them play no useful role in phonetic description.

### H* Realisation: "Sag" Transition (pp. 92, 112)
Two H* accents in sequence are NOT connected by a straight high transition. Instead there is a "sag" -- the pitch drops to a local minimum between the two peaks, then the second H* is realised as a local pitch jump. This is described as Anderson, Pierrehumbert, and Liberman (1984).

### L+H* vs. H* Alignment (p. 96)
The difference between L+H* and plain H* is the presence of a more substantial rising pitch movement leading to the H* target. The L target precedes the accented syllable. The distinction is often difficult to make on phrase-initial accented syllables where there is no preceding syllable to display the L target level.

### L*+H vs. L+H*: Starred Tone Alignment (p. 96)
In L*+H, the starred (defining) tone L is aligned with the accented syllable, with the H following. In L+H*, the starred tone H is aligned with the accented syllable, with L preceding. The star notation indicates which tone is the defining one aligned with the accented syllable.

## Cross-linguistic Data

### Indonesian (pp. 60-61)
Pitch movements can be defined with respect to the penultimate syllable, making it "accented" in AM terms, but it is not necessarily "stressed" in the sense of acoustic salience. Dissociation between accent and stress.

### Efik Tone System (p. 66)
Three tonal patterns for monosyllabic verbs: High, Low, Rise. Two-syllable stems show only three patterns (High-High, Low-Low, Low-High), motivating analysis as two level tones (H, L) with surface realisation depending on association to available syllables.

### Yoruba (pp. 65-66)
Three lexical level tones. System of lexical tones based on distinctive levels is uncontroversial. When vowels are elided, tones reassociate to adjacent syllables, creating contour tones from underlying level tone sequences.

### Hungarian Question Intonation (pp. 81-84)
Two major AM differences from English/French question contours:
1. Nuclear accent location: on the finite verb (not sentence-final)
2. Tonal sequence: L* pitch accent followed by H_L edge tones (falling from penultimate, not rising)

Analysis: L* H L edge tone sequence -- unique among Western European languages. The H edge tone associates with penultimate syllable, L with final syllable. Focus/emphasis conveyed by changing word order AND nucleus location.

Examples:
- (23) L* HL: "Beszel a tanar?" (Is the teacher talking?)
- (27a) L* HL: "Vettel szodat?" (neutral: Did you buy soda?)
- (27b) L* HL: "Szodat vettel?" (focus on SODA)
- (27c) H L*HL: "Szodat vettel?" (What about soda? Did you buy that?)

### African Tone Languages and AM Theory (pp. 65-67)
Two motivations for analysing contours as level tone sequences in African languages:
1. Segmental elision preserving tones (Yoruba example)
2. Economy of description (Efik: 3 surface patterns for 2-syllable stems predicted by just H and L)

These patterns formed key impetus for autosegmental theory (Leben 1973, Goldsmith 1976, Williams 1976).

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation from AM Representation
For a TTS system implementing AM-style intonation:

1. **Two-level tone targets**: All F0 contours derive from sequences of H and L targets. The mapping from abstract H/L to actual Hz values requires a "scaling" component that implements tonal space.

2. **Tonal space model**: Implement declining topline and baseline (Bruce and Garding's statement lines) to define the available range at each point in the utterance. H targets map to the top of the tonal space, L targets to the bottom. The tonal space shrinks over the utterance.

3. **Downstep implementation**: Each downstepped accent reduces tonal space by a constant proportion (k ~ 0.6). This is a multiplicative scaling applied to subsequent H targets. Implement as left-to-right with local lookback only.

4. **Final lowering**: Apply additional lowering to the final accent beyond what the downstep ratio predicts. This is a separate effect from downstep.

5. **Pitch accent alignment**:
   - H* peaks align with accented syllable
   - In L+H*, the L precedes the accented syllable, H peak on it
   - In L*+H, the L valley is on the accented syllable, H follows
   - H*+L / H+!H*: peak on accented syllable with following step-down
   - Between two H* accents, interpolate with a "sag" (local dip), NOT a straight line

6. **Edge tone implementation**:
   - Phrase accent (H- or L-) controls the pitch between the nuclear accent and the boundary
   - Boundary tone (H% or L%) controls the final pitch target at the phrase edge
   - Together they produce the post-nuclear contour shape

7. **Finite-state tune grammar**: Legal tunes follow the pattern:
   ```
   [%T] T* ... T* T- T%
   ```
   where T = H or L, T* can be monotonal or bitonal, and all combinations of accent types with edge tones are legal.

8. **Peak height control precision**: English speakers control accent peak F0 with standard deviations of only ~7.5 Hz, indicating that TTS systems need precise F0 target control (not just approximate shapes).

9. **Constant-proportion downstep**: For a sequence of n downstepped accents starting at F0_initial:
   ```
   F0_n = F0_initial * k^n  (where k ~ 0.6)
   ```

10. **Hungarian-style question intonation**: Demonstrates that question tunes are NOT universal "rising pitch at end". Different languages use different tonal sequences (L*_H_L in Hungarian vs. H*_H_H% in English yes-no questions). Language-specific tune inventories are required.
