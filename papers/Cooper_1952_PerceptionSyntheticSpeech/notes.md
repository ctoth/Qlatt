# Some Experiments on the Perception of Synthetic Speech Sounds

**Authors:** Franklin S. Cooper, Pierre C. Delattre, Alvin M. Liberman, John M. Borst, Louis J. Gerstman
**Year:** 1952
**Venue:** The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, Vol. 24, No. 6, pp. 597-606
**DOI:** 10.1121/1.1906940

## One-Sentence Summary
This paper establishes through systematic perception experiments on the Haskins pattern playback that stop consonant place of articulation is cued by burst frequency position and second-formant transition direction/extent, and that these cues interact with the following vowel context.

## Problem Addressed
How do listeners perceive consonant identity from simplified synthetic speech patterns? Specifically, what are the minimal acoustic cues (burst frequency, formant transitions) that enable identification of stop consonants and nasals, and how do these cues interact with vowel context?

## Key Contributions
- Demonstrated that burst frequency position relative to vowel formants provides a basis for distinguishing stop place of articulation (p, t, k)
- Showed that second-formant (F2) transitions are the primary cue for stop consonant place, with rising transitions heard as /b/ or /d/ and falling transitions as /d/ or /g/ depending on the vowel
- Established that the consonant-vowel syllable is the minimal acoustic unit for consonant perception, not the consonant segment alone
- Identified the complementary/trading relationship between burst and transition cues
- Provided early evidence for the acoustic cue system later formalized by Stevens, Blumstein, and others

## Methodology
Used the Haskins Laboratories "pattern playback" device, which converts hand-painted spectrographic patterns on acetate into sound. Experiments systematically varied:

1. **Burst experiment (PTK):** 12 burst frequency positions x 7 cardinal vowels (two-formant only) = 84 burst+vowel syllables, presented to 30 college students who identified each initial consonant as p, t, or k.
2. **Transition experiment (BDG):** 11 degrees of second-formant transition (from steeply rising to steeply falling) x 7 vowels x 2 first-formant shapes (rising = voiced, no transition = voiceless) = 77 consonant-vowel combinations per condition, judged by 33 subjects.
3. **Nasal resonant experiment (M and L):** Transitions combined with a steady-state nasal resonant portion, exploring cues for m, n, ng and l.

## Key Findings

### Burst Frequency and Stop Place (Fig. 3)
- High-frequency bursts heard as /t/ for all vowels
- Low-frequency bursts heard as /p/ for all vowels
- Bursts at or slightly above the second formant of the vowel heard as /k/
- The /k/ burst frequency therefore varies with vowel context (follows F2)

### Second-Formant Transitions and Stop Place (Figs. 5, 6)
- Rising F2 transitions: heard as /b/ (or /p/) -- associated with bilabial place
- F2 transitions rising sharply from a low starting point or falling from a high point show a crossover between /d/ and /g/ depending on vowel
- The voiced/unvoiced distinction is carried primarily by first-formant transitions (rising F1 = voiced) and voice bar presence
- Second-formant transitions provide the main basis for place of articulation among stops

### Complementary Cue Relationship
- Bursts and transitions complement each other: when one cue is weak, the other tends to be strong
- Some consonant-vowel combinations require both cues for unambiguous identification
- The perceptual system treats the burst+transition+vowel as a unit

### Nasal Consonants (M, N, NG)
- Same second-formant transition patterns as for voiced stops (m parallels b, n parallels d, ng parallels g)
- Distinguished from stops by the presence of a steady-state nasal resonant portion
- A transition from higher frequency followed by a nasal resonant: heard as /m/ or sometimes /l/
- Distinctions between /l/ and /m/ involve: (a) rate of F2 transition (gradual favors /l/), (b) low formant position of resonant portion favors /l/, (c) plausible continuation of F2 into resonant favors /l/, sudden discontinuity favors /m/

### Vowels
- Two-formant synthetic vowels suffice for vowel identification
- Back vowels can sometimes be produced with a single formant (perceptual averaging of close F1 and F2)
- Third formant transitions may contribute to consonant identification (exploratory finding)

### Binary Decision Framework (Fig. 8, p. 604)
The authors propose that stop perception may reduce to binary decisions:
- **Bursts:** High (+) = /t/; Low (-) = /p/ or /k/
- **Transitions:** Falling (+) = /t/ or /k/; Rising (-) = /p/
- Combined: p = (- -), t = (+ +), k = (- +) or (+ -)

This anticipates later distinctive feature approaches.

## Results Summary
- Stop consonant place can be identified from burst frequency alone (84 stimuli, 30 listeners)
- Second-formant transitions provide a complementary and often dominant cue for place
- The syllable (CV) is the perceptual unit, not the consonant segment in isolation
- Transitions carry information about both the consonant and the vowel simultaneously
- Connected speech synthesis was demonstrated using rules derived from these experiments

## Limitations
- Only two-formant vowels used (F3 not systematically studied)
- Pattern playback produces simplified, non-naturalistic stimuli
- Only seven vowel contexts tested
- No systematic study of consonant clusters or connected speech coarticulation
- Limited to stop consonants and nasals; fricatives, affricates, and liquids not studied
- Burst and transition cues not tested in combination systematically (only separately)

## Testable Properties
- Rising F2 transitions before any vowel should be perceived as labial (/b/ or /p/)
- High-frequency bursts (~3600-4320 Hz) should be perceived as /t/ regardless of vowel context
- The frequency at which a burst is heard as /k/ should track the F2 of the following vowel
- Adding a steady-state nasal resonant portion to a stop pattern should shift perception from stop to nasal (b->m, d->n, g->ng)
- Voiced/voiceless distinction should depend on F1 transition presence (rising F1 = voiced)

## Relevance to Project
This paper is foundational for understanding the acoustic cues that Qlatt must generate correctly for stop consonant synthesis. Specifically:
- The burst frequency rules (Fig. 3) validate context-dependent burst placement in Qlatt's parallel branch
- The F2 transition findings inform the formant transition rules in `phases/formant.yaml` -- rising F2 for labials, falling for velars (before back vowels), context-dependent locus for alveolars
- The complementary burst/transition relationship justifies Qlatt's dual-cue approach to stop synthesis (AF burst + cascade formant transitions)
- The finding that the CV syllable is the perceptual unit supports Qlatt's structural phase that expands stops into sub-segments (closure, burst, aspiration, transition)
- The nasal consonant findings (same transitions as cognate stops + steady resonant) inform the nasal synthesis rules
