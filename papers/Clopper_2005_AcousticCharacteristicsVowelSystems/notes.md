# Acoustic characteristics of the vowel systems of six regional varieties of American English

## One-Sentence Summary
Reports acoustic measures (duration, F1, F2) for 11 American English vowels produced by 48 talkers (4M/4F x 6 dialect regions: New England, Mid-Atlantic, North, Midland, South, West) from the Nationwide Speech Project corpus, showing that each region has a distinct vowel system reflecting known sociolinguistic sound changes (Northern Cities Chain Shift, Southern Vowel Shift, low-back merger).

## Problem Addressed
Prior acoustic vowel-formant benchmark studies (Peterson & Barney 1952, Hillenbrand et al. 1995, Hagiwara 1997) each described only a single (often geographically mixed or single-region) variety of American English, while sociolinguists have documented substantial regional phonological variation (chain shifts, mergers) that is not captured by a single "General American" formant baseline. This paper provides a systematic, comparable acoustic-phonetic description across six major US dialect regions using one consistent methodology. *(p.1-3)*

## Key Contributions
- Acoustic benchmark data (duration, F1, F2 at 1/3 and 2/3 vowel duration) for 11 vowels across 6 US dialect regions, 48 talkers, both sexes.
- Confirms/quantifies three well-known sociolinguistic sound changes acoustically in a single unified dataset: Northern Cities Chain Shift (North), Southern Vowel Shift (South), and the low-back (/ɑ/-/ɔ/) merger (New England, Midland, West, variably Mid-Atlantic).
- Identifies previously under-described patterns: back-vowel (/u/, /o/) raising among Southern talkers, an apparent new low-back-vowel F2 alignment among Mid-Atlantic talkers (/ɑ/, /ɔ/, /ʌ/), and widespread /u/-fronting across regions/sexes.
- Documents an artifact of Lobanov z-score normalization (apparent spurious "backing" of already-fronted back vowels for talkers with extreme fronting) directly relevant to anyone using z-scored vowel data as absolute targets.
- Evidence that the Midland dialect region is a transition zone between North and South (some Midland talkers, esp. from Indianapolis metro area, show both Northern /æ/-raising and Southern /u/-fronting).

## Study Design
- **Type:** Cross-sectional, controlled multi-region acoustic-phonetic study (repeated-measures ANOVA design).
- **Population:** 48 talkers (24 male, 24 female), ages 18-25, from the Nationwide Speech Project (NSP) corpus (Clopper, 2004): 4M + 4F from each of 6 US dialect regions (New England, Mid-Atlantic, North, Midland, South, West), defined per Labov, Ash & Boberg (forthcoming Atlas of North American English). All monolingual native AmE speakers, no hearing/speech disorders, both parents native English speakers, lived in a single dialect region their whole life (parents too), recorded at Indiana University (Bloomington) but each had lived there <2 years at recording time to minimize dialect leveling. *(p.3)*
- Full talker demographic table given in Appendix (Talker ID, sex, age, hometown city/state, education, occupational field/major), p.15-16. Ages 18-24, mostly undergraduates from a wide range of US states matching each dialect region's geography (e.g. New England talkers from NH/MA/VT/CT, South talkers from AL/TX/KY/IN(border), West talkers from NM/CA/MT/NV).
- **Stimulus materials:** 11 vowels /i, ɪ, e, ɛ, æ, ɑ, ɔ, ʌ, o, ʊ, u/ (Table I, p.28). 5 repetitions of each vowel except /ɔ/ (which is not available in hVd context in the NSP corpus due to a design oversight) obtained in hVd nonsense-word utterances (50 tokens/talker: heed, hid, hayed, head, had, hod, hud, hoed, hood, who'd); 6 tokens of /ɔ/ obtained in sentence-final position (3x "frogs", 3x "logs") per talker. Total 56 vowel tokens/talker, 5-6 tokens/vowel. *(p.4, p.28)*
- Recording: Macintosh Powerbook G3, Shure SM10A head-mounted mic, mic preamp, Roland UA-30 USB interface, sound-attenuated IAC chamber, 16-bit .aiff, 44.1kHz. *(p.4)*

## Methodology
- 5 acoustic measures per token: vowel duration, F1 at 1/3 temporal point, F2 at 1/3 point, F1 at 2/3 point, F2 at 2/3 point -> 13,440 total measurements (56 tokens x 48 talkers x 5 measures). *(p.4)*
- Measured with WaveSurfer 1.6.2 Speech Analysis tool (Sjölander & Beskow 2004): time-aligned waveform, f0 trace, wideband spectrogram with F1-F4 formant tracks; automatic formant tracking via 12th-order LPC over 49ms window, 10ms frame interval. *(p.4)*
- Vowel onset: onset of voicing (after voiceless C) or sudden intensity/formant change (after voiced C); offset: offset of voicing or sudden intensity drop. For post-liquid vowels, onset determined by visual+auditory inspection. Formant values hand-corrected by first author via visual inspection/cursor tool as needed. *(p.4)*
- 28/13440 tokens (1%; note: this is 1% of tokens not measurements, i.e. ~28 of 2688 tokens) excluded (25 misreadings, 3 recording errors). All excluded tokens from the hVd materials (disfluencies/mispronunciations not repeated in that set). *(p.4)*
- 37 of 13,440 measurements (0.3%) rechecked as potential outliers: 1 measurement error, 5 typographic errors, 14 formant-tracking errors corrected; remaining 17 were natural variation, left unaltered. *(p.4-5)*
- Reliability: 1% subset remeasured by first author (all 6 dialects, all 11 vowels represented); duration absolute difference range 0-11ms (mean 3ms); formant frequency absolute difference range 0-44Hz (mode 0Hz, mean 0.3Hz) - "within acceptable range of error" per Hillenbrand et al. (1995). *(p.5)*
- Normalization: Lobanov (1971) z-score transform applied per talker, separately for F1 and F2, using values from the 1/3 temporal point, prior to all statistical analyses (chosen per Adank, Smits & van Hout 2004 finding that Lobanov's metric best reduces anatomical variation while preserving phonological/sociolinguistic variation). Duration NOT normalized. *(p.5-6)*
- Statistical design: repeated-measures ANOVA, vowel category (11 levels) as within-subjects factor, dialect (6) and gender (2) as between-subjects factors, run separately for duration, F1 z-score, F2 z-score (1/3 point). Because 3 ANOVAs computed, p threshold set to 0.01. Follow-up post-hoc one-way ANOVAs per vowel with dialect as factor, p threshold 0.001 (correcting for many comparisons); post-hoc Tukey tests for pairwise dialect comparisons. Three-way (dialect x vowel x gender) interaction was significant for nucleus formants, so post-hoc analyses were run separately for males and females. Merger assessment: paired-sample t-tests per dialect on /ɑ/ vs /ɔ/ F1 and F2 z-scores, p threshold 0.005. *(p.6-9)*
- Descriptive/qualitative analysis: Figs. 5-10 plot every raw-Hz token (not normalized) for each of the 11 vowels, separately by dialect and sex, with hand-drawn ellipses enclosing every token per vowel category - used to inspect within-dialect/within-speaker variability not visible in the group-mean ANOVA results. *(p.10)*

## Key Equations / Statistical Models

$$
z = (f - \mu)/\sigma
$$
Where: z = Lobanov-normalized formant value, f = raw formant frequency (Hz) of a given vowel token, mu = overall mean frequency of that formant (F1 or F2) for that talker across all their tokens, sigma = standard deviation of that talker's same-formant tokens. Computed separately per talker, per formant (F1, F2), using values at the vowel's first-third (1/3) temporal point. *(p.6)*

## Methods & Implementation Details
- Dialect region definitions (Labov et al., forthcoming Atlas of North American English) and their expected acoustic signatures, set out a priori as hypotheses (p.3, Figs 1 and 3):
  - **North:** Northern Cities Chain Shift (NCCS) - clockwise rotation: /æ/ raised+fronted, /ɑ/ lowered+fronted, /ɛ/ and /ʌ/ backed, /ɔ/ lowered (Fig. 1 schematic, p.18).
  - **South:** Southern Vowel Shift (SVS) - fronting of back vowels /u/, /o/; front lax vowels /ɪ/, /ɛ/ raised/fronted toward periphery; front tense vowels /i/, /e/ centralized via lowering/backing (Fig. 3 schematic, p.20).
  - **Midland, Western, New England:** merger of /ɑ/ and /ɔ/ (cot/caught, Dawn/Don). Midland = least marked, no other distinct features predicted. Western New England: some NCCS features (/æ/ raising, /ɑ/ fronting, /ɛ/ backing). Western: low-back merger + /u/ fronting only (not full SVS back-vowel fronting).
  - **Mid-Atlantic:** does NOT show low-back merger; instead /ɔ/ raising reported in prior literature, plus partial /æ/ raising (historical long/short /æ/ split). *(p.3)*
- Individual talker-level vowel-space plots (with formant trajectories) for all 48 talkers are in Clopper (2004), the associated PhD dissertation, not included in this article. *(p.10, footnote 2)*
- Southern male talkers: back-vowel fronting (/u/, /o/) is present in ALL tokens of ALL 4 talkers (Fig.9 top), yet each individual talker still maintains a distinguishable /o/-/ʊ/ contrast via nucleus position + trajectory, despite substantial group-level overlap. *(p.10-11)*
- Midland males (Fig. 8 top) show a striking within-dialect split: 2/4 talkers (MI3, MI4) show /u/-fronting (a Southern feature) while 2/4 (MI3 [also], M12) show /æ/-raising (a Northern feature) - notably MI3 shows BOTH features simultaneously; all 3 talkers exhibiting either feature are from the Indianapolis metro area, supporting the Midland-as-transition-zone hypothesis (Davis & Houck 1992). *(p.11, p.13)*
- New England talkers (Fig. 5): split on /æ/-raising - 3/4 males show raised /æ/ (Northern-like), 1 (NE1) retains lowered /æ/; 1/4 females (NE8) raised, other 3 maintain /æ/-/ɛ/ distinction. ALL New England talkers (both sexes, both Eastern and Western New England) show the /ɑ/-/ɔ/ merger/partial merger - broader than expected if only Eastern New England had the merger, suggesting Eastern and Western New England are phonologically more homogeneous than the literature (Boberg 2001) implies. *(p.10, p.13)*
- Mid-Atlantic talkers (Fig. 6): most variable region for the low-back merger - 2/4 males (AT1, AT3) show merger, other 2 distinct; 1/4 females (AT9) clear merger, other 3 more distinct. Only 1/4 females (A18) shows the expected /ɔ/-raising described in prior literature; that same talker (A18) is also the only one with fronted /u/ (others backed). Considerable /ʊ/-/ʌ/ overlap in females attributed to genuine cross-talker F1 variation, not individual-level mergers. *(p.10)*
- Northern talkers (Fig. 7): NCCS present in essentially all 4 male talkers; talkers from upstate NY and Wisconsin show backed /ɛ/, while 2 talkers from northern Indiana retain more fronted /ɛ/ - sub-regional variation within the "North" label. Despite nucleus-level /æ/-/ɛ/ overlap, their trajectories remain distinguishable (/ɛ/ moves up over the vowel; /æ/ moves back/down), so offsets stay separated. *(p.10-11)*
- Southern females (Fig. 9 bottom) are "by far the most variable subgroup of talkers in the NSP corpus" - individual /u/,/o/-fronting ranges from virtually none (SO6, Kentucky) to very extreme (SO7, Texas). *(p.12)*
- Western talkers (Fig. 10): large F2 variance for /u,ʊ,o/ among males driven by one talker (WE4, Montana) with unusually low back-vowel F2s; low-back merger present as a group and individually in all 4 male talkers. /u/-fronting present in 2/4 Western females (WE7, WE9) but not the other 2 or in most Western males (Western /u/-fronting still mostly female-led at the time of this study). *(p.12)*

## Results Summary
Northern talkers reliably produced the Northern Cities Chain Shift (lowered+fronted /ɑ/, raised+fronted /æ/, backed /ɛ/ and /ʌ/ in females). Southern talkers reliably produced core features of the Southern Vowel Shift (fronted /o/, centralized /e/) plus new/unexpected features: fronted /æ/ (males only) and raised /ʊ/,/u/ (both sexes). New England, Midland, and Western talkers produced the /ɑ/-/ɔ/ low-back merger (statistically at least a partial merger); Northern and Southern talkers kept /ɑ/ and /ɔ/ clearly distinct. Mid-Atlantic talkers did not show the expected /ɔ/-raising but instead showed an unexpected/new F2 alignment of /ɑ/, /ɔ/, and /ʌ/. Southern talkers had significantly longer lax vowels (/ɪ ɛ ʌ ʊ/) than New England, Mid-Atlantic, and Western talkers, reducing their tense/lax durational contrast; female talkers had longer vowels than males for the same 5 vowels, independent of dialect. Some apparent Southern back-vowel "backing" of /ɑ/, /ʌ/, /ɔ/ in the z-score data is judged to be a normalization artifact rather than a genuine phonetic pattern. *(p.10-15)*

## Limitations
- Elicited via reading nonsense-word (hVd) and short-sentence stimuli displayed on a computer monitor, which may produce somewhat unnatural, more careful/citation-like speech relative to spontaneous conversation (cf. Labov 1972; Ladefoged, Kameny & Brackenridge 1976). *(p.13-14)*
- Only the hVd phonetic context was examined; no pre-/r/, pre-/l/, or pre-nasal vowel contexts were included, despite known strong consonantal conditioning effects on vowel quality documented by sociolinguists. *(p.14)*
- Most reported effects are based on a single temporal point (1/3 duration) per vowel token; a preliminary look at trajectories suggests additional dialect-relevant variation exists in how vowels change shape over their course (spectral movement), not fully explored here. *(p.14)*
- Despite ~1hr of speech per talker recorded in the full NSP corpus, only a small token subset (56/talker) was analyzed here, and the statistical comparisons rest on only 4 talkers per sex per dialect (n=8 per dialect) - a relatively small and demographically homogeneous sample (age, education, ethnicity all narrow). *(p.14)*
- The /ɔ/ (frogs/logs) measure is a lexically restricted item (only 2 words) that prior literature (Trager 1930; Wells 1982) flags as sometimes idiosyncratic/non-representative of the broader /ɔ/ class - a design oversight in the original NSP corpus that lacked /ɔ/ in hVd context altogether. The unexpected Mid-Atlantic non-merger/[ɔ] non-raising results may be partly a lexically-specific artifact rather than a general phonological fact about the dialect. *(p.13)*
- The Lobanov z-score transform is demonstrably inadequate in at least two specific ways documented in this paper: (1) it can produce artifactually "backed" low-back-vowel z-scores for talkers whose back vowels are strongly fronted (raises overall F2 mean, shrinks F2 SD, mechanically pushing the still-more-back low vowels to appear "backed" in z-score terms even with unchanged raw Hz) - documented for Southern male /ɑ/,/ʌ/,/ɔ/; (2) the single-talker/single-formant Lobanov transform does not properly handle formant measures taken at multiple temporal points within a spectrally-moving vowel, since the vowel-space "shape" differs by temporal slice - the authors explicitly recommend caution using z-score comparisons across vowel systems that differ in overall shape. *(p.8, p.14-15)*

## Design Rationale
- Chose the Lobanov (1971) per-talker z-score transform specifically because Adank, Smits & van Hout (2004) found it best balances removing anatomical (vocal-tract-length/gender) variation while preserving genuine phonological/sociolinguistic variation - a deliberate methodological choice over other normalization schemes. *(p.5-6)*
- Chose to also plot ALL raw-Hz tokens per dialect/vowel/sex (Figs 5-10), not just group z-score means, specifically because group-level ANOVA effects can mask (or, per the z-score artifact, mislead about) substantial within-dialect individual-talker variability - explicit methodological stance that qualitative/descriptive inspection of raw distributions is necessary alongside inferential statistics. *(p.10)*
- Chose talkers who had lived in their home dialect region their entire life (parents too) but had lived in Bloomington, IN <2 years at recording time, specifically to capture each region's "native" vowel system while minimizing contamination from dialect leveling/accommodation to the recording location. *(p.3)*
- Explicitly declined to pick one single "General American" acoustic baseline, instead proposing that a baseline should be identified per-vowel by inspecting which dialect(s) show the least-shifted (i.e., most other dialects converge toward it) variant - illustrated for /æ/ (only the North shows the raised/fronted variant, so lowered/backed = baseline) and /o/ (only South shows the fronted variant, so backed = baseline). This is presented as a general recommended heuristic, not resolved definitively. *(p.14-15)*

## Testable Properties
- Northern talkers show significantly fronted+lowered /ɑ/ and significantly fronted+raised /æ/ relative to all other dialect groups (Northern Cities Chain Shift), for both males and females. *(p.7-9, Table II)*
- Southern talkers show significantly fronted /o/ and /u/ (both sexes) and significantly raised /ʊ/ (males) - the fronting of /æ/ (males only) and raising of high back vowels are described as new/unanticipated Southern features not previously well documented. *(p.7-9, Table II)*
- New England, Midland, and Western talkers show a statistically confirmed partial merger of /ɑ/ and /ɔ/ (nonsignificant or only marginally significant paired F1/F2 differences); Northern and Southern talkers keep /ɑ/ and /ɔ/ significantly distinct in both F1 and F2 (p<0.001-0.003). Mid-Atlantic talkers show a partial-merger pattern for F1 (p=0.01) but not F2 (p=0.08, n.s.). *(p.9)*
- Southern talkers produce significantly longer lax vowels (/ɪ ɛ ʌ ʊ/) than New England, Mid-Atlantic, and Western talkers, without an overall vowel-duration difference - i.e., selectively reduced tense/lax durational contrast, not uniformly slower speech. *(p.6)*
- Female talkers produce significantly longer /ɪ ɛ ɑ ʌ ʊ/ than male talkers, collapsed across dialect - a vowel-specific (not overall) gender duration effect. *(p.6)*
- Apparent "backing" of /ɑ/, /ʌ/, /ɔ/ in Southern-male z-scored F2 is attributable to a z-score-transform artifact (larger mean + smaller SD from extreme back-vowel fronting mechanically inflates the apparent backing of the remaining low vowels) rather than a genuine phonetic pattern - a testable methodological caution for anyone using Lobanov normalization on strongly-shifted vowel systems. *(p.8, p.14)*
- Mid-Atlantic talkers (both sexes, most clearly males) show a distinctive alignment of /ɑ/, /ɔ/, and /ʌ/ along F2 not predicted by prior sociolinguistic literature - a genuinely new empirical finding of this study. *(p.8-9, p.13)*

## Relevance to Project
This paper is the single most directly usable American-English source for a per-region formant synthesizer's vowel targets: it gives statistically validated, sex-split acoustic signatures (which vowels shift, in which direction, by how much, with what significance) for 6 real US dialect regions, tied to well-known named sound changes (NCCS, SVS, low-back merger) that a synthesizer's accent model could implement as parameterized shifts from a baseline vowel system. Table II is a ready-made per-vowel/per-sex "what changes and how" specification. The explicit within-dialect variability data (Figs 5-10, showing e.g. only half of Midland talkers show any shift at all) is a critical caution against treating any single region's vowel system as monolithic - useful for deciding how much "accent strength" variance a synthesizer should allow within one named regional voice. The documented Lobanov z-score artifact is directly actionable: raw Hz values (not the z-scored/plotted values) should be used as ground truth wherever available, and z-score-derived apparent shifts should be sanity-checked against raw distributions before being encoded as target rules.

### Conceptual Links (not citation-based)
- [Formant frequencies of vowels in 13 accents of the British Isles](../Ferragne_2010_FormantFrequenciesVowels13/notes.md) - Strong. Both papers are large-scale, multi-accent/multi-dialect formant studies of English published within 5 years of each other, both use Lobanov z-score normalization on a Hz-derived scale (Bark for Ferragne & Pellegrino, raw z-score here) as their primary between-accent comparison method, both use hVd or hVd-like test words, and both explicitly discuss and caution about the same normalization artifact: Ferragne & Pellegrino note (p.31) that z-scoring erases real between-accent centroid/spread differences, while this paper independently documents (p.8, p.14) that z-scoring can artifactually "back" already-fronted vowels for talkers with extreme shifts - convergent methodological warnings from two independent research groups about the same normalization technique, directly relevant to any per-accent/per-dialect vowel-target pipeline that uses Lobanov normalization as an intermediate step. Both papers also independently document within-region/within-accent heterogeneity as a first-class finding rather than noise (Ferragne & Pellegrino's brm FOOT-STRUT bimodal split parallels this paper's Midland talkers' individually-inconsistent NCCS/SVS features).
- [The Formants of Monophthong Vowels in Standard Southern British English Pronunciation](../Deterding_1997_FormantsMonophthongVowelsStandard/notes.md) - Moderate. Both are English-vowel formant benchmark studies motivated explicitly by the inadequacy of prior single-variety/citation-word baselines (Deterding compares connected speech to citation words; Clopper et al. compare six US regions to a presumed single "General American" baseline), and both conclude that a single idealized baseline vowel system is inadequate for their respective source of variation (register vs. region). Deterding's SSB monophthong set (11 vowels) and this paper's American monophthong set (11 vowels, slightly different inventory: /ɔ/ present, no /ɜː/) are the two most directly comparable non-regional-British vowel inventories in the collection for cross-variety formant-target comparison.
- [Kent & Vorperian 2018 - Static Measurements of Vowel Formant Frequencies and Bandwidths: A Review](../Kent_Vorperian_2018_VowelFormantBandwidths/notes.md) - Moderate. Kent & Vorperian's review explicitly cites Hillenbrand et al. (1995) and Peterson & Barney (1952) as core American-English formant benchmarks - the same two studies this paper positions itself against/extends regionally - so Kent & Vorperian's age/sex-based normative synthesis and this paper's region-based acoustic differentiation together bound the two major non-lexical axes (age/sex vs. dialect region) of American-English vowel-formant variation a synthesizer's target system would need.
