This paper proposes that phrase boundary lengthening arises from prosodic gestures (pi-gestures) that locally slow a central clock controlling the temporal unfolding of an utterance, rather than through ad-hoc duration multipliers or gestural stiffness modulation. Computational simulations demonstrate that clock-slowing captures three empirically observed boundary phenomena under a single mechanism: increased segmental duration, reduced intergestural overlap, and increased spatial magnitude of boundary-adjacent gestures, with the degree of effect controlled by a continuous boundary strength parameter. For Qlatt's duration modeling, this work provides the theoretical justification for gradient, distance-decaying boundary lengthening rules where multiplier magnitude scales with prosodic boundary strength and is greatest for segments immediately adjacent to the boundary edge.
