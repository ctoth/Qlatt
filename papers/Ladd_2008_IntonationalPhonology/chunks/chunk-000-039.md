## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Definition of Intonation (p. 23 / book p. 4)
Ladd defines **intonation** with three key properties:
1. **Suprasegmental**: Refers to features of fundamental frequency (F0), intensity, and duration -- the traditional suprasegmental features. Ladd uses "pitch" and "F0" interchangeably throughout, noting F0 is the physical property and pitch is its psychophysical correlate.
2. **Sentence-level or postlexical**: Conveys meanings that apply to phrases or utterances as a whole (sentence type, speech act, focus, information structure). Excludes features of stress, accent, and tone that are determined in the lexicon (e.g., English *permit* noun vs. verb; Chinese tone distinctions).
3. **Linguistically structured**: Organised in terms of categorically distinct entities (e.g., low tone vs. boundary rise, stronger-than/weaker-than). Excludes "paralinguistic" features where continuously variable physical parameters directly signal continuously variable states (e.g., tempo/loudness signaling arousal).

### Pitch vs. Relative Prominence (p. 25-27 / book pp. 6-8)
Two **independently variable aspects** of intonation:
- **Pitch/Tune**: The melodic pattern (e.g., falling vs. rising). A falling pattern on "five pounds" signals a reply; rising signals doubt/question.
- **Relative prominence**: Which word bears the main stress (e.g., weak-strong "five POUNDS" vs. strong-weak "FIVE pounds"). The prominence pattern determines information focus independently of the tune.

These combine in a 2x2 matrix (Figure 1.1, p. 26 / book p. 7) yielding four distinct intonational possibilities for any two-word phrase.

### Three Aspects of Intonation (p. 27 / book p. 8)
In earlier work (American phonemic tradition: Trager and Smith 1951; Halliday 1967a), three aspects were identified:
1. **Pitch/tune** ("tone" in Halliday) -- the pitch pattern
2. **Relative prominence** ("tonicity" in Halliday) -- which word is prominent
3. **Phrasing/tonality** ("tonality" in Halliday) -- division of speech into intonational chunks

Ladd organizes his book around the centrality of pitch and prominence (Part II: Pitch; Part III: Phrasing and Prominence).

### Phonological vs. Phonetic Description (p. 29 / book p. 10)
A complete phonological description requires:
- (a) A level where sounds are characterized as **categorically distinct entities** (phonemes, features)
- (b) A **mapping** between this categorical description and the continuous physical parameters of the acoustic signal

### Instrumental vs. Impressionistic Approaches (pp. 29-31 / book pp. 10-12)
- **Instrumental/phonetic tradition**: Experimental psychologists/phoneticians studying acoustic cues to intonational phenomena (finality, continuation, interrogation, emotion, stress). Sought direct physical correlates of meaning.
- **Impressionistic/proto-phonological tradition**: Linguists describing intonation in terms of categorically distinct elements (pitch phonemes, nuclear tones). Used auditory methods, lacked instrumental validation.

Neither approach alone constituted a true phonology. The convergence since the 1970s produced the AM (autosegmental-metrical) framework.

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### The IPO Model (Section 1.2, pp. 31-38 / book pp. 12-18)
Developed at the Institute for Perception Research (IPO) in Eindhoven (~1965-1995). Originally motivated by the need for a model of Dutch intonation for speech synthesis. Key features:

**Phonological structure** (1.2.1):
- Contours are idealised as sequences of **pitch movements** and connecting line segments
- Pitch movements are **discrete commands** to the vocal cords recoverable as discrete events from continuous F0 variation
- Basic distinction: relatively **high** vs. relatively **low** pitch levels
- Two classes of pitch movements:
  - **Prominence-lending**: Associated with stressed syllables (following Bolinger 1958). Include rise+fall on accented syllable, late rise, half-fall.
  - **Non-prominence-lending**: Boundary-associated (rise and fall at phrase edges, rise+fall spanning multiple syllables). Phonologically distinctive in that they change contour type (e.g., boundary rise distinguishes question from statement).

**The "hat pattern"** (pp. 33-34 / book pp. 14-15):
- Best-known IPO construct
- Consists minimally of Type 1 Rise (low-to-high early in accented syllable) + Type A Fall (high-to-low early in accented syllable)
- Two variants: "pointed hat" (rise+fall on same syllable) and "flat hat" (rise and fall on different accented syllables)
- Abstract formula: `(0) 1 (empty) A (0)` -- obligatory rise (1) and fall (A), with optional stretches of 0 on non-prominent syllables
- The stretches between movements are idealised as straight line segments (empty for upper, 0 for lower)

**Phonetic realisation** (1.2.2, pp. 35-36 / book pp. 16-17):
- All phonological elements described in considerable phonetic detail
- Pitch movements are aligned with respect to the stressed syllable
- Based on perceptual experiments determining limits of perceptible variation
- **Declination**: The downward trend of F0 observable in many utterances (term from Cohen and 't Hart, 1967). In IPO model, refers to the trend of top and bottom lines (empty and 0 segments) that set limits on local pitch movements. Even when "nothing is happening" phonologically, F0 continues to decline slightly. A pitch movement early in a phrase will be higher than the same movement later in the phrase.

**Key theoretical assumptions** (1.2.3, pp. 36-38 / book pp. 17-18):
- **Sequential structure**: An IPO description is a string of discrete intonational elements in sequence (like segmental phonology)
- **Phonetic definition**: Elements are defined by their phonetic characteristics, NOT by their meaning or function. "Meaning or function plays no role in the analysis." Elements are identified solely by perceptual distinctness.
- These two properties (sequential structure + phonetic definition) parallel segmental phonology, where the F0 contour -- like the "second formant contour" -- arises from a sequence of categorically distinct elements

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### Declination (p. 35 / book p. 16)
- F0 shows a **downward trend** (declination) across utterances
- In the IPO model, the top and bottom reference lines decline gradually
- This means the same phonological pitch movement is realized at a progressively lower F0 as the utterance proceeds
- Declination is treated as part of phonetic realisation, not as part of the abstract phonological description
- Extensive research on declination within and outside IPO framework (references: 't Hart 1979; Cohen, Collier, and 't Hart 1982; Terken 1991; Vaissiere 1983; Ladd 1984, 1993c)

### Categorical vs. Continuous Pitch Variation (pp. 38-39 / book pp. 19-20)
- Pierrehumbert and Liberman (1982) criticised purely continuous approaches (like Cooper and Sorensen 1981) for failing to identify phonological categories
- The "fall-rise" patterns studied by Cooper et al. actually fall into TWO categories: one with a fall to the bottom of the speaker's range (between intonation phrases), and one with a less extreme fall within a single phrase
- Continuous parametric variation obscures this categorical distinction
- Key insight: at least some languages must reference **local phonological events** at specific points in the utterance, not just global parametric manipulation

## Alignment and Timing

### IPO Pitch Movement Alignment (p. 35 / book p. 16)
- Pitch movements in the IPO model are aligned with respect to the **stressed syllable**
- Prominence-lending movements occur on the lexically stressed syllable of accented words
- Phonetic descriptions specify alignment details based on perceptual experiments

## Implementation Notes

### For TTS F0 Generation
1. **Pitch and prominence are orthogonal**: A TTS system must independently control (a) the tune/pitch pattern (falling, rising) and (b) the prominence pattern (which word is focused). These combine multiplicatively, not additively.

2. **Declination must be modeled**: F0 trends downward across an utterance. The IPO model implements this as gradually declining reference lines (topline and baseline) within which local pitch movements operate. Same pitch movement type realized at lower absolute F0 later in phrase.

3. **Categorical pitch movements**: F0 contours should be generated from sequences of discrete pitch events (rises, falls) with specific alignment to stressed syllables, not from continuous parametric functions. The IPO approach validates that listeners perceive discrete movements even when the physical F0 is continuous.

4. **Prominence-lending vs. boundary movements**: Two distinct classes of pitch events serve different functions:
   - Prominence-lending movements mark accented words (occur on stressed syllables)
   - Boundary movements mark phrase edges (not tied to prominence)
   Both must be generated by separate mechanisms in the F0 module.

5. **The hat pattern as basic template**: For declarative sentences, the fundamental F0 shape is a rise to a high target on the first accented syllable, maintained or slightly declining through the phrase, then falling at or before the last accented syllable. This "hat pattern" (IPO) provides a simple but effective baseline for statement intonation in TTS.

6. **Phonetic definition principle**: When implementing intonation categories, define them by their acoustic/phonetic properties (rise rate, fall extent, alignment) rather than by their intended meaning. The mapping from communicative intent to intonation category is a separate higher-level decision.

7. **Fall-rise patterns are categorically distinct**: The boundary between two intonation phrases uses a fall to the bottom of the pitch range, while a continuation within a phrase uses a shallower fall. These must be distinct F0 generation rules, not just parametric variants of the same rule.
