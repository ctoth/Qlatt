# Intonational variation in urban dialects of English spoken in the British Isles

## One-Sentence Summary
Reports IViE-project findings showing that pitch-accent phonology, its phonetic realization (truncation vs. compression), and the location of stressed/accented syllables all vary systematically by British-Isles urban dialect, utterance type, and speaker, and gives quantitative nuclear-accent distributions per dialect and sentence type that a rule-based per-accent intonation model can draw parameters from directly.

## Problem Addressed
Prosodic/intonational phonology research has traditionally treated variation (across dialect, speaker, style) as "noise" to be averaged away, restricting descriptions to a single "standard" variety (Southern British English) and presenting the result as representative of English as a whole. This paper argues variation must be described explicitly and shows, via the IViE corpus, how large that variation actually is across seven/nine British Isles dialects. *(p.1-2)*

## Key Contributions
- Describes the IViE (Intonational Variation in English) project: corpus design, five-tier transcription system (IViE), and its use for cross-dialect comparison *(p.3-12)*
- Demonstrates cross-dialect variation in phonetic realization: truncation vs. compression is dialect-specific, not language-specific as previously proposed *(p.12-13)*
- Demonstrates cross-dialect, cross-utterance-type, and cross-speaker variation in phonological nuclear-accent choice across 7 dialects x 4 sentence types, with full percentage tables *(p.13-16)*
- Extends Haan's (2002) "trade-off between question-markers and final rise" prediction (originally for Dutch) to English dialects, showing the same universal tendency holds with dialect-specific deviations (Belfast, Bradford) *(p.17-21)*
- Proposes that dialects offering multiple rising nuclear-accent options (e.g., low rise L*H vs. high rise L*H H%) may use them to grade degree of interrogativity *(p.20-21)*

## Methodology
Not a single controlled experiment; synthesizes findings from the IViE project (Grabe et al. 2000, 2001; Grabe & Post 2002; Grabe 2002) plus a re-analysis (recoding into rising/not-rising) that makes IViE English data directly comparable to Haan's (2002) Dutch question-marker/final-rise dataset.

## IViE Corpus / Project Design (Study Design equivalent)
- **Funding/PIs:** ESRC award to F. Nolan (Cambridge) and E. Grabe (Cambridge & Oxford); Research Associates K. Farrar and B. Post; funding period 1997-2002. *(p.3)*
- **Recording period:** 1997-2000, at nine urban UK/Ireland locations. *(p.4)*
- **Locations (9):** London, Cambridge, Cardiff, Liverpool, Leeds, Bradford, Newcastle, Belfast, Dublin. Includes 3 ethnic-minority varieties: Punjabi English (Bradford, bilingual speakers, ~10% of Bradford adults speak Punjabi), Welsh English (Cardiff, bilingual, recorded in a Welsh-medium school), West Indian/Caribbean English (London, monolingual speakers). *(p.4-6)*
- **Speakers:** 12 per location (6 male, 6 female) = 108 speakers total; all 16 years old at recording; chosen by teachers on criterion of being born and raised locally. *(p.4, p.6)*
- **Corpus size:** 36 hours of speech, released 2001 on 5 CD-ROMs (.wav) and via the internet. *(p.4)*
- **Prosodically transcribed subset:** 1 hour, from 7 of the 9 dialects (London, Cambridge, Leeds, Bradford, Newcastle, Belfast, Dublin — Cardiff and Liverpool excluded from this subset), 1 male + 1 female speaker per dialect, all 5 speaking styles; available online since June 2002. *(p.4, p.14, footnote 10)*
- **Five speaking-style elicitation tasks, identical battery across all speakers** *(p.6-7)*:
  1. 22 context-free fully-voiced sentences: declaratives, wh-questions, yes/no questions, questions without morphosyntactic question markers (declarative questions), coordination structures.
  2. Reading aloud the fairy tale "Cinderella" (chosen for familiarity, to limit interpretive variation).
  3. Retelling "Cinderella" from memory, aided by pictures.
  4. Map task (Anderson et al. 1991): two screened speakers, one has a route drawn on a map of buildings, the other has to reconstruct it by dialogue; landmark names controlled for syllable count (monosyllabic/disyllabic/trisyllabic, e.g. Farm/Jeweller/Alleyway) and stress position (initial/medial/final, e.g. ˈJeweller / Muˈseum / Ann's ˈArms); contrastive stress elicited via minimal-pair landmark names (Ann's Arms/Jolly Miller on Map 1 vs John's Arms/Jolly Sailor on Map 2).
  5. 3-5 minute free conversation between the same speaker pair, on the suggested topic "smoking"; same-sex pairs (with one exception); all speakers knew each other.
- **Recording fieldworkers (native-dialect awareness caveat):** Kimberley Farrar recorded Cambridge, Leeds, Newcastle, Dublin; Brechtje Post recorded Bradford, London, Cardiff; Catherine Sangster recorded Liverpool; Orla Lowry recorded Belfast. Farrar, Post, Sangster speak Southern Standard British English; Post is a native Dutch speaker; Lowry is from Belfast. *(p.6, footnote 6)*
- **Post-recording questionnaire:** place of birth (self + parents), area of city lived in, house type, parental occupation, plus filler questions (music taste, school subjects). *(p.7)*

## The IViE Transcription System (5 tiers) — directly relevant to building a rule-based accent model
| Tier | Content |
|------|---------|
| Orthographic | Words spoken + their location in the acoustic signal |
| Prominence | Location of prominent (stressed and/or accented) syllables + rhythmic boundaries + hesitations/repairs |
| Phonetic | Syllable-by-syllable transcription of pitch pattern shape surrounding prominent syllables and IP boundaries (e.g. "H-l", "HL", "H") |
| Phonological | Formal autosegmental-metrical (AM) two-tone labels for accented syllables and IP boundaries (based on Gussenhoven 1984, Grabe 1998a; symbols similar to ToDI, Gussenhoven/Rietveld/Terken 1999) |
| Comment | Alternative transcriptions and notes |
*(p.7-8, Table 1)*

- **Design rationale — why not plain ToBI:** original ToBI-style systems are mono-lingual/mono-dialectal; IViE extends the AM/ToBI concept to allow *directly comparable* analyses across several dialects of one language in a single transcription system, and separates the prominence-identification step from the phonological-classification step (ToBI conflates stressed vs. accented syllable identification implicitly; IViE makes it explicit and overt). *(p.7, p.12)*
- **Labelling method (comparative approach, Grabe 1998a):** utterances not labelled in isolation. The fairy-tale recordings from six speakers within one dialect are compared IP-by-IP before moving to the next dialect, so labelling decisions are made relative to directly comparable material. *(p.8)*
- **All accent labels are left-headed** (i.e. begin with the tone associated with the stressed syllable). *(p.8)*

### Table 2 — AM accent labels in the IViE pool *(p.9)*
| Label | Typical implementation |
|-------|------------------------|
| H*L | High target on prominent syllable, followed by low target |
| H* | High target, common phrase-initially in "flat hat" patterns |
| !H*L | Downstepped high target, low target |
| L*HL | IP-internal/IP-final rise-fall: low target on prominent syllable, high on next syllable, then low |
| L*H | Low target on prominent syllable followed by high target |
| L* | Low target |
| H*LH | IP-internal/IP-final fall-rise: high on strong syllable, then low, then high |

### Table 3 — IP boundary tone specifications *(p.9)*
| Phrase-initial | Phrase-final | Transcribes |
|---|---|---|
| %H | H% | high target |
| % | % | no pitch movement at boundary (level held to IP boundary) |
| %L | L% | low target |

### Table 4 — Belfast vs. Southern British English boundary-tone transcription of rise-plateau family *(p.9-10)*
| Contour | Belfast label | Southern British label |
|---|---|---|
| plain rise-plateau (level held to IP boundary) | L*H % | L*H % |
| rise-plateau-rise | L*H H% | L*H H% |
| rise-plateau-fall | L*H L% | **Not attested** in Southern British English |

- **Design rationale for the % boundary symbol:** Standard ToBI would transcribe a rise-plateau as L*H-L%, but Glasgow English and Belfast English both independently have a genuine rise-plateau-**fall** tune (L*H L%) as well as a plain rise-plateau and (in Belfast) a rise-plateau-**rise** (L*H H%). If plain rise-plateau were also transcribed L*H-L%, the same label string would denote a rise-plateau in one dialect and a rise-fall in another — breaking cross-dialect comparability. IViE resolves this by reserving the toneless `%` boundary specifically for "pitch sustained to the IP boundary" (i.e., true plateau), freeing L% and H% to mean actual final fall/rise. *(p.9, citing Ladd 1996:145ff on Glasgow)*
- **This is the key formal device for encoding Belfast's characteristic rise-plateau family** — directly load-bearing for a per-accent model that needs Belfast-specific tune inventory: {L*H %, L*H H%, L*H L%} vs. Southern British's more restricted {L*H %, L*H H%} (no attested rise-plateau-fall). *(p.9-10)*

## Truncation vs. Compression: Phonetic-Tier Findings *(Section 4.2.2, p.10-11; Section 5.1, p.12-13)*
- **Definitions:** For an IP-final falling accent (H*L) realized on a short word with little scope for voicing (e.g. monosyllabic "hat"/"lip"), truncation = the pitch movement remains incomplete (pattern cut off); compression = the same pattern is produced faster so it still completes within the available voicing. *(p.10)*
- **Illustrative minimal pair (Figure 2, p.11):** word "limousine" (long, plenty of voicing) — both Newcastle and Leeds realize H*L as a full fall, phonetic transcription "H-l" in both. Word "lip" (short) — Newcastle compresses: phonetic transcription "HL" (sharp fall on one syllable, pattern completed); Leeds truncates: phonetic transcription "H" only (no fall present at all, pattern incomplete).
- **Phonetic-tier notation:** capital letter = pitch target; lower-case letter = target reached only via interpolation; "-" = interpolation diacritic between two targets (can span 2+ syllables), used for economy of transcription. *(p.10, footnote 8)*
- **Cross-dialect experiment (Section 5.1, replicating/extending Grabe 1998b):** Grabe (1998b) previously found the truncation/compression distinction differentiated Southern British English (compressing) from Northern Standard German (truncating), suggested by Ladd (1996) as possibly a *language*-specific parameter — but Swedish dialect data (Bannert & Bredvad 1975) suggested it might instead be *dialect*-specific. IViE tested this directly. *(p.12-13)*
- **Stimuli:** surnames "Mr. Sheafer" / "Mr. Sheaf" / "Mr. Shift" (decreasing scope for voicing) embedded in identical carrier phrases with identical pragmatic-intent precursors, across Cambridge, Leeds, Newcastle, Belfast. Expectation under "compression is a property of English" was that all four dialects would compress (increase rate of f0 change from longest to shortest word). *(p.13)*
- **RESULT — dialect-specific, not language-specific *(p.13, Figure 3 map)*:**

| Dialect | Truncation or Compression |
|---|---|
| Cambridge | Compression (C) |
| Newcastle | Compression (C) |
| Leeds | Truncation (T) |
| Belfast | Truncation (T) |

This directly falsifies the "English is a compressing language" hypothesis and establishes truncation/compression as a per-dialect (not per-language) parameter — a testable property for any rule-based synthesis model that wants dialect-accurate short-word accent realization.

## Prominence-Tier Finding: Stress-Location Dialect Shift *(Section 4.2.3, p.11)*
- Dialects can differ in *where* stress falls, not just in accent shape. Example: "suddenly" is stressed on the first syllable in (Southern) British English but heard as stressed on the *last* syllable in Singapore English, especially by British-English-background listeners (Low & Grabe 1999). A similar effect was observed in Newcastle IViE data for "What is the matter?" → /ˈma-ˈʔə/ (glottal-stop realization with altered stress/prominence pattern noted). Footnote: Singapore English speakers reportedly cannot make confident prominence judgments themselves. *(p.11)*

## Phonological (Nuclear Accent) Variation — Section 5.2, the core quantitative dataset *(p.13-16)*
- **Data source:** sentence-reading task (style 1) from the prosodically-transcribed IViE subset. 6 speakers (3M/3F) per dialect x 7 dialects (Belfast, Bradford, Cambridge, Dublin, Leeds, London, Newcastle — Cardiff/Liverpool excluded here). Each speaker read: 8 declaratives (DEC, e.g. "We arrived in a limo"), 3 wh-questions (WH-Q, e.g. "Where's the manual?"), 3 yes/no questions (Y/N-Q, e.g. "May I lean on the railings?"), 3 declarative questions/no morphosyntactic marker (DEC-Q, e.g. "You remembered the lillies?"). Stimuli presented as a randomized list. *(p.14)*

### Key phonological findings from Table 5
- **Falling nuclear accent (H*L %) dominates declaratives in 6 of 7 dialects.** The sole exception is **Belfast**, where declaratives are predominantly **rising (L*H %, 83.3%)** — confirming earlier predictions (Rahilly 1991; Cruttenden 1995) that Belfast does not use the fall/rise declarative/question distinction that Southern British English uses. *(p.15)*
- **Newcastle and Dublin are intermediate**: rising declaratives occur but are minority patterns (Newcastle 16.7% [reported as ~15% in prose], Dublin 6%). *(p.15)*
- **Questions show far more nuclear-accent-type variation than declaratives**: typically 2 accent options per dialect in declaratives vs. 3-4 options per dialect in questions. *(p.15)*
- **Only two dialect x utterance-type combinations were categorical (100% single accent across all 6 speakers) in the whole dataset: Leeds declaratives and Bradford declaratives** (both 100% H*L%). This is presented as evidence that even "canonical" categorical descriptions are the exception, not the rule, once individual speaker variation is examined. *(p.16)*

### Figure 4 (p.16) — cross-dialect overlap in declarative nuclear accents (Newcastle, Cambridge, Belfast)
- Newcastle declaratives: 2 options — H*L% (shared with Cambridge and Belfast, though rare in Belfast) and L*H% (shared with Belfast, not with Cambridge).
- Cambridge declaratives: 2 options — H*LH% (unique to Cambridge, a falling-rising nuclear accent not shared with Newcastle or Belfast) and H*L% (shared with both).
- Belfast declaratives: produces L*HL% (rise-plateau-fall) which, in this read-sentence subset, was not produced by Newcastle or Cambridge speakers.
- **Three simultaneous sources of variation identified: (1) Dialect** (e.g. Belfast vs. Cambridge both use rise-plateau L*H%, but only Belfast speakers produced it in this comparison — Cambridge speakers did not); **(2) Utterance type** (e.g. Leeds: H*L% used by 100% in DEC, 73% in WH-Q, 44% in Y/N-Q, 0% in DEC-Q — a smooth decline); **(3) Speaker** (e.g. Newcastle Y/N-Q: 44% of speakers use H*L%, 16% use H*L H%, 39% use L*H% — three different individual choices for the same dialect/utterance-type cell). *(p.16)*

## Section 6 Discussion: Theoretical framing — "Lopsided Sparsity" and lopsided-accent-frequency
- **Van Santen's (1994) "lopsided sparsity"** concept applied to intonation: some patterns occur very frequently, the vast majority of possible patterns are individually rare, but the *type count* of rare patterns is so large that encountering at least one non-conforming pattern in any sample is near certain. Grabe argues the structural nuclear-accent variation in Table 5 is itself evidence of lopsided sparsity in intonation, and that rare patterns are not necessarily linguistically unimportant just because they're infrequent. *(p.17)* **Design implication for a rule-based model: a per-accent grammar should generate a probability-weighted set of admissible nuclear-accent realizations per (dialect, utterance-type) cell, not a single categorical rule, and should not discard low-frequency but attested tunes.**

## Haan's (2002) Cross-Linguistic Trade-off, extended to English dialects (Section 6.1, p.17-21)
- **Haan's original claim (Dutch):** trade-off between the number of syntactic/lexical interrogativity markers in an utterance and the probability of a final high/rising f0. Declarative questions (DECQ, zero question markers) show maximal final rise; wh-questions (two markers: inversion + wh-word) show minimal final rise; yes/no questions (one marker: inversion) intermediate. Predicted ordering (Figure 5, p.18): DEC (not a question, near 0% rise) < WH (2 markers) < Y/N (1 marker) < DECQ (0 markers), rise probability increasing monotonically as marker count decreases. *(p.17-18)*
- **Recoding of IViE English nuclear accents into binary rising/not-rising (Table 6, p.18):**

| Transcription | Description | Recoded as |
|---|---|---|
| H*L % | fall | Not rising |
| L*H L% | rise-plateau-fall | Not rising |
| H*L H% | fall-rise | Rising |
| H* H% | high accent followed by a rise | Rising |
| (L) H* % | high accent preceded by low target | Rising |
| L*H % | rise-plateau | Rising |
| L* H% | late rise | Rising |
| L*H H% | double-rise | Rising |

| Bradford | 100.0 / 0 | 83.3 / 16.7 | 16.7 / 83.3 | 22.2 / 77.8 |
| Leeds | 100.0 / 0 | 72.2 / 27.8 | 44.4 / 55.6 | 0 / 100 |

| Dublin | 94 / 6 | 77.8 / 22.2 | 68.4 / 31.6 | 50 / 50 |
*(p.19; values are "Not rising" / "Rising" percentages)*

- **Statistical result:** Two-way ANOVA — increase in incidence of final rise across utterance types is significant, p<0.001 (full stats in Grabe 2002). *(p.19)*
- **Two exceptions to the monotonic trade-off pattern (Figure 6, p.20):**
  1. **Belfast:** Y/N-Q and DEC-Q both already at 100% rising (ceiling), because Belfast declaratives themselves are already predominantly rising (L*H%) — not "surprising" given the baseline. However Belfast still shows internal gradience: L*H H% (higher/double rise) incidence increases from Y/N-Q (6%) to DEC-Q (17%) — i.e., speakers use a *higher* rise for the utterance type with fewer question markers, even though the binary "rising" measure is already saturated. *(p.19, p.21)* **This is the key granular evidence for interrogativity-graded accent choice.**
  2. **Bradford:** final-rise incidence increases from DEC to Y/N-Q, but does NOT increase further from Y/N-Q to DEC-Q (83.3% → 77.8%, i.e. essentially flat/slightly down) — anomalous, more data called for. *(p.19)*
- **Cross-linguistic generality of the trade-off:** also independently reported for German (Brinckmann & Benzmüller 1999) and Polish (Grabe & Karpinski 2003) — suggested to be a genuine cross-linguistic (possibly universal) tendency, not idiosyncratic to Dutch/English. *(p.20)*
- **Proposed mechanism (biological/functional, not formal):** Ohala's (1983) "Frequency Code" — high pitch signals subordination/dependence/non-threat; a speaker asking a question is dependent on listener cooperation, hence favors high pitch when other interrogativity cues (syntax, lexical markers) are absent. Alternative/compatible account: Chafe (1994) and Wennerstrom (2001) — questions are demands for interactional attention, so speakers make them maximally prominent (i.e., high-pitched) in the speech stream. *(p.20)*
- **Grabe's own extension proposal:** dialects that offer *multiple* rising nuclear-accent options (e.g. a low rise L*H alongside a high/double rise L*H H%) may use the choice between them to grade *degree* of interrogativity — the higher-pitched rise signalling interrogativity more strongly. Evidence: in Belfast, L*H H% is essentially absent in wh-questions (weakest need for extra interrogative marking, already has wh-word+inversion), appears at 6% in yes/no questions, and rises to 17% in declarative questions (weakest syntactic marking, so most need for an extra prosodic interrogativity cue); by contrast the rise-plateau-fall L*H L% (a *falling* variant) is observed only in declaratives. Grabe flags this specific claim ("do the different high/low rise options add *only* a difference of degree, or something else communicatively") as an open empirical question, not yet settled. *(p.21)* **This is the single most directly actionable finding for a rule-based Belfast intonation policy: {WH-Q: ~0% double-rise, Y/N-Q: ~6% double-rise, DEC-Q: ~17% double-rise} as a probabilistic escalation rule keyed to how many syntactic/lexical question markers are present, layered on top of the L*H%/L*H L%/L*H H% tune inventory from Table 4.**

## Limitations
- Cardiff and Liverpool were excluded from the prosodically-annotated 1-hour subset (and hence from Tables 5-7) because they were not part of the *original* IViE project proposal, despite being in the full 9-dialect audio corpus. *(p.14, footnote 10)*
- Small per-cell sample sizes: only 6 speakers (3M/3F) per dialect, and only 3 tokens per speaker for each of WH-Q/Y/N-Q/DEC-Q (vs. 8 for DEC) — percentages in Tables 5/7 are consequently coarse-grained (multiples of ~5.6% = 1/18) and single-speaker choices can swing a cell noticeably.
- Bradford's failure to follow the monotonic rise-with-fewer-markers trend from Y/N-Q to DEC-Q is explicitly flagged as unexplained ("more data are required to investigate this anomaly"). *(p.19)*
- The claim that multiple rising options encode *graded* interrogativity (vs. some other communicative distinction) is explicitly labelled an open empirical question by the author, not a settled finding. *(p.21)*
- Truncation/compression cross-dialect test used only one lexical set (Sheafer/Sheaf/Shift) in one carrier-phrase context; generality across other segmental contexts not established here (details deferred to Grabe et al. 2000 — see companion Paper 1 in this collection).

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- Against treating variation as "noise": standard practice in intonational phonology research restricts description to one variety and masks cross-speaker variation via statistical processing, then presents results as representative of the whole language — the paper argues this obscures systematic dialect- and speaker-conditioned structure. *(p.1)*
- Against Ladd's (1996) language-specific truncation/compression parameter (built on Grabe 1998b's English-vs-German finding): the four-dialect English-internal test shows truncation/compression splits *within* English by dialect (Cambridge/Newcastle compress, Leeds/Belfast truncate) — so it cannot be a purely language-level parameter; Swedish-internal dialect variation (Bannert & Bredvad 1975) had already hinted at this. *(p.12-13)*
- Against plain ToBI-style ToBI transcription for cross-dialect work: standard ToBI (L*H-L% for rise-plateau) becomes ambiguous/non-comparable once a dialect (Glasgow, Belfast) has a genuine rise-plateau-fall contour distinct from a plain rise-plateau; IViE's `%` no-tone boundary symbol is the proposed fix. *(p.9)*

## Design Rationale
- **Comparative (not isolated) labelling procedure** was chosen specifically because intonation patterns show "considerable levels of variation, even in relatively well-controlled data from a single dialect" — comparing directly parallel fairy-tale recordings across the six speakers of one dialect before moving on reduces spurious inter-labeller disagreement. *(p.8)*
- **Explicit separation of prominence tier from phonological tier**: chosen so that the (often contentious) decision of whether a stressed syllable is *also* accented is made overtly and separately from low-level stress/prominence identification, rather than folded silently into the phonological label as in classic ToBI. Rationale explicitly stated: this increases transparency/replicability, and a prominence tier helps diagnose the specific locus of labeller disagreement (stress vs. accent status). *(p.8, p.12)*
- **Choice of adolescent (16-year-old), locally-born-and-raised speakers**: standard sociolinguistic control to maximize dialect authenticity/homogeneity and minimize accent levelling from mobility. *(p.6)*
- **Choice of "Cinderella" for read/retold-story tasks**: familiarity to virtually all speakers constrains interpretive/pragmatic variability that would otherwise confound cross-speaker prosodic comparison. *(p.6)*

## Testable Properties (for a rule-based per-dialect intonation model)
- Truncation-vs-compression is a **binary per-dialect switch**, not per-language: {Cambridge: compress, Newcastle: compress, Leeds: truncate, Belfast: truncate} for IP-final falling accents (H*L) on short/low-voicing-scope words. *(p.13)*
- Belfast declaratives must default to a **rising** nuclear tune (L*H%, ~83% of tokens) rather than the falling tune (H*L%, only ~4%) that dominates in the other 6 dialects (95-100% in 4 of 6). *(p.14-15)*
- Belfast's tune inventory must include the **rise-plateau-fall** (L*H L%) as a legal declarative-only tune, not attested in Southern British English. *(p.9-10, p.14)*
- A model of "rise strength as interrogativity marking" for Belfast should scale double-rise (L*H H%) usage roughly as: WH-Q ≈ 0%, Y/N-Q ≈ 6%, DEC-Q ≈ 17% — increasing as syntactic/lexical question-marking decreases. *(p.19, p.21)*
- Across all 7 dialects, probability of a rising nuclear tune should increase monotonically DEC < WH-Q < Y/N-Q < DEC-Q, EXCEPT Bradford (which should be modeled as flat/non-monotonic between Y/N-Q and DEC-Q) and Belfast (which should be modeled as already-ceiling from Y/N-Q onward, with the *degree* of rise, not presence/absence, carrying the further gradient). *(p.19-20)*
- Newcastle and Dublin should permit a **minority rising-declarative option** (~15-17% and ~6% of tokens respectively) alongside the majority falling declarative, rather than treating declaratives as categorically falling. *(p.15)*
- Even nominally "categorical" dialect/utterance-type cells (Leeds DEC, Bradford DEC) should still be modeled probabilistically at the population level if extended beyond the 6-speaker sample, since only these 2 of 28 dialect x utterance-type cells reached 100% single-pattern uniformity in a 6-speaker sample — treat near-100% empirical cells as strong-default-with-rare-alternative rather than hard-categorical. *(p.16-17, "lopsided sparsity")*
- Question-type-conditioned nuclear accent selection should be modeled with **3-4 candidate tunes per (dialect, question-type) cell**, not 1, since questions showed systematically more per-cell accent-type diversity than declaratives. *(p.15)*

## Relevance to Project
Directly usable as the quantitative backbone for a per-accent nuclear-tune selection policy in a rule-based English-dialect intonation model: (1) supplies a discrete per-dialect truncation/compression flag for phonetic realization of short-word falling accents; (2) supplies full empirical nuclear-accent-type probability tables per (dialect x DEC/WH-Q/Y/N-Q/DEC-Q) cell for 7 dialects, directly usable as sampling weights; (3) supplies the specific AM tune inventory and boundary-tone notation needed to represent Belfast's rise-plateau family (L*H%, L*H H%, L*H L%) distinctly from Southern British English's more restricted set; (4) supplies a graded-interrogativity submodel for Belfast tying double-rise usage to number of remaining syntactic/lexical question markers. Companion paper (Grabe, Post, Nolan & Farrar 2000, this collection — retrieval attempted but not obtained) is cited repeatedly here as the primary source for the underlying truncation/compression experimental methodology and additional detail; that paper should be sought again for methodological detail on peak alignment/F0-excursion measurement not given in this chapter.
