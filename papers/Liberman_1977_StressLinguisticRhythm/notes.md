# On Stress and Linguistic Rhythm

## Reading provenance

Direct reading of every canonical page image, with incremental notes. PDF image 000 is the JSTOR cover; image 001 is printed p.249. Citations use printed pages. Theory profile.

## Core thesis and evidence

Relative prominence is a relation between constituents; rhythmic organization additionally uses a metrical grid. The theory reanalyzes established observations rather than reporting a new controlled experiment. Reiterant-speech evidence is cited to separate rhythmic stress from pitch-accent placement; phonetic adequacy of the complete proposed analysis remains an empirical question. *(pp.249-251)*

## Phrasal stress and relational representation

- Baseline phrases favor the right member, lexical compounds the left; discourse can override these defaults. Relative prominence inside whale-oil survives embedding in whale-oil lamp. *(p.251)*
- The older numerical account cyclically reassigns primary stress and demotes all other stresses: dew-covered lawn yields 2-3-1. Its NSR selects the rightmost primary; CSR skips a final word where possible before selecting the remaining primary. Law degree requirement changes and law-degree language requirement demonstrate distinct nesting. These are prior-model derivations, not the proposed implementation. *(pp.252-254, examples 1-5)*
- Numerical accounts need extra flattening/readjustment, word-internal demotion, and a rhythm rule for thirteen men/Tennessee Ernie. *(p.255)*
- Strong/weak labels denote one local relation on sisters. Isolated s or w and sister pairs ss/ww are undefined. Local relations persist under higher embedding; no global demotion pass is needed. Root R is neither s nor w. *(p.256)*
- Rule (8): for [A B] under a phrasal category, B is strong; under a lexical category, B is strong iff it branches. Sibling labels are complementary. These local labeling constraints can be applied in any order when structure is fixed; the representation itself does not force cyclic execution. *(pp.257-258)*
- The designated terminal element (DTE) is reached by following only strong branches. Numerical mimicry (12) counts dominating nodes for a weak terminal, or nodes above the lowest dominating weak node for a strong terminal, then adds one. The authors explicitly do not adopt that total numerical ranking as the theory's phonetic interpretation. *(p.259)*
- Relational and numerical CSR differ when a complex right constituent itself has rightmost prominence. Motor unit neural control supplies a cautiously offered example; its unusual status limits the argument. *(pp.260-261)*
- The relational account explains stress's contextual character, lack of fixed local acoustic value, preservation under embedding, and local operation. The seven traditional peculiarities include arbitrary n-ary levels, syntagmatic dependence, nonlocal phonetics, cyclicity, widespread demotion, crucial variables, and disjunctive ordering; disjunction is revisited for word stress. *(pp.262-263)*

## Word structure and preliminary stress rule

- LP retains binary segmental +/-stress: gymnast versus modest and raccoon versus balloon share relative tree shape but differ in whether the weak vowel is stressed. Constraint (18): a strong vowel must be +stress; equivalently a -stress vowel must be weak. The converse does not hold. *(pp.264-265)*
- Initial construction (22): each + followed by a run of - syllables forms a left-branching tree; these feet join a right-branching word tree. Acacia/America resolve attachment ambiguity; reconciliation/execute illustrate right-to-left construction. *(pp.266-267)*
- Word Rule (28) labels the right sister strong iff branching. It explains execute/execution; labels across the entire tree locate main stress. Word boundaries require a prosodic-level node M: compound branching cannot inspect syllables inside a word. The unified Lexical Category Prominence Rule (33) operates at the same prosodic level. *(pp.268-270)*
- Explicit exceptions include verbs, certain prefixes, orthographic -y, final high tense vowels, names and sporadic lexical contrasts. Thus the preliminary LCPR is not a universal English default. *(p.270)*
- Underlying long vowels are tense vowels/true diphthongs (divine, obscene, vane, pounce, moon, vote, Bermuda, point); short-vowel examples include pit, pet, pat, put, putt, pot, impudent, pong. Phonetic duration does not define this class: pot's phonetically long vowel is underlyingly short. *(p.271)*
- America/aroma/defective-type contrasts motivate light-penult skipping versus heavy-penult stress. Preliminary ESR (38) chooses the longest applicable right-edge context disjunctively; a final long vowel receives +stress. Pre-vocalic and final tensing occur later, so surface tense vowels can remain stressless. Footnote 9 warns that the segmental formula is an abbreviation for syllable-based conditions, including onset-cluster cases. *(pp.271-272)*

## Retraction, iterative structure, and reduction (pp. 273-287)
- Rules (40)-(41), p.273: final nonlow vowel lengthening and prevocalic lengthening follow stress. Surface tense vowels therefore do not establish underlying weight; Ohio/radio and veto/motto illustrate hidden contrasts. Final stress and light-penult exceptions require lexical diacritics; the ESR's permitted count can be shortened, not freely skip a heavy syllable.
- Weak Retraction (43), p.274: count left from an already stressed vowel, skipping at most one light syllable; -oid examples contrast hominoid and ellipsoid, with solitary lithoid stressed. Weak classes (44), pp.274-275 include -ite, -on, -ode, -ide, -i and -ology; proper names and lexical exceptions also occur.
- Strong Retraction (45), p.275: skip at most one syllable independently of its weight. Most verbal -ate forms follow this mode (designate/concentrate); salivate/defecate support bypassing an underlying long vowel. Forms in (46) include recognize, recognition and several -ary/-ine words. Morphological classes admit lexical variation (p.276); -ation apparent counterexamples require cyclic analysis below.
- Long Retraction (47), pp.276-277: permit two intervening unstressed syllables only when the leftmost is light. Tatamagouchi contrasts with Monongahela. Table (48) separates noncomplex forms, vowel-vowel sequences, Greek prefixes, -atory forms and miscellaneous migrants; some apparent cases may instead involve external word-boundary affixation. Popocatepetl favors binary alternation when four light syllables precede the ESR stress.
- SRR (49), p.278, unifies weak and strong as restricted expansions of long retraction; lexicon/morphology specifies which expansion is admitted. Iterative ESR (51) combines word-edge placement and retraction: first iteration restricts the skipped final nucleus to short, later iterations count from a stressed vowel without that restriction. Iteration continues leftward, not merely to a second stress (p.279). The authors leave sophisticated iterative versus simultaneous formalizations undecided (fn.11).
- Initial Destressing (52), p.279, provisionally deletes stress from an initial short-vowel light syllable immediately preceding stress: police/parade versus Montana/fandango. It is revised later; do not implement it as the final account.
- Tree building accompanies each stress assignment (pp.279-280). Newly formed branching units attach to the previously built right-hand tree with LCPR labeling; teleological and reconciliation show right-branching word-level structure.
- Metrical Bracketing (57), p.281: (a) structure all syllables in the current rule domain; (b) adjoin any unstructured material left from the previous iteration; (c) link this result to prior structure, attaching leftover material at termination. A singleton stressed syllable does not by itself form a branching tree. Odontology (58), p.282, demonstrates how the next iteration groups the stray syllable. Contiguous stresses consequently group in binary trochaic units, from the right.
- Disjunctive ESR alternatives correspond to incompatible structures over the same string (pp.282-283); this supplies a structural reason for longest-context selection that purely segmental parenthesis notation lacks. Vowel lengthening subrules, by contrast, can both apply (rodeo/radio).
- Vowel reduction, pp.283-286: distinguish binary stress presence from relative prominence. Initial light syllables tend to destress, heavy ones retain stress; exceptions include raccoon/tattoo and shortening or r-colored-vowel cases. Latinate/Celtic prefixes can reduce even when heavy, but only before stronger prominence, not any [+stress]. Condensation/condense/contemplate (61) isolates the distinction. Some vowel-initial prefixes remain full despite being stressless; absence of schwa is not evidence of stress. Rhythm-shift participation distinguishes intense/exact from optionally destressed abstract (p.285).
- Medial open syllables undergo shortening/destressing, whereas closed syllables preserve stress: explanation/rotary/componential versus odontology/relaxation (62), pp.285-286. Advisory versus promissory requires comparing prominence rather than mere neighboring [+stress]. -ization and unfamiliar forms allow optionality. SPE-style rule (63), p.286, conditions reduction on a following stronger vowel and structural eligibility; (64)-(65), p.287, expand initial and medial cases. The initial rule must refer to syllable structure, not a literal consonant count (asparagus/mosquito/astronomy).

## Destressing, extrametricality, and the cycle (pp. 288-301)
- Prefix rule (66), p.288, is the third SPE expansion. Metrical destressing (67) replaces numerical comparison with immediate weak domination. Initial, medial and prefix reduction share this restriction; a strong leaf cannot become [-stress].
- Bisyllabic final prominence (pp.289-290) requires refined lexical-category prominence rather than ordering reduction before prominence. Police/manure versus rabbi/satire defeats a universal light-heavy nominal rule; verbs and adjectives have category regularities. Advisory and condensation show prominence must already exist when reduction applies.
- Well-formedness principle (69), p.290: "No rule may apply so as to produce an ill-formed representation." Thus final EDR (70) omits explicit metrical weakness: the global condition blocks changing a strong leaf to [-stress]. Initial reduction requires a short vowel; medial or prefix reduction may shorten a long vowel. The authors explicitly decline to claim that all segmental rules can dispense with access to richer metrical structure (p.291).
- Poststress reduction (72), pp.291-292, falls under the same EDR if the affected vowel is weak, medial and open, even when no stronger stress follows. -ory/-ary take stress by ESR but never primary prominence word-finally and often reduce immediately after primary stress: cursory/advisory versus promissory/sedentary. Merely marking a whole suffix weak leaves its internal strong leaf incompatible with destressing (75), p.293.
- Treat final -y as underlying nonsyllabic or abstractly extrametrical during ESR (76), p.293. This makes -ory a unitary metrical constituent: cursory receives left prominence, and the suffix vowel becomes weak and reducible. Separate later rules create schwa or r-assimilation; EDR itself is not a complete vowel-quality synthesizer.
- Left Foot (77), p.294: a uniformly left-branching tree whose leftmost terminal is strong (s w w ...). Stray Syllable Adjunction (78) weakly adjoins a syllable unaccounted for by ESR to the nearest maximal left foot, respecting word boundaries. Examples (79), pp.294-295: cursory, dispensary, contradictory. Evidence for exact adjunction details is admitted to be limited.
- Sonorant syllabification after ESR extends the analysis to ancestor/carbuncle/autopsy and curmudgeon/Carmichael/Hoboken; some require EDR exceptions or different ordering (p.295). Primary/rosary/binary contrast with plenary/granary/rotary.
- Articulatory (80), p.296, would incorrectly lose -or- under EDR after SSA. Foot Formation (81) restructures the end of a long left foot into a subordinate s-w foot when the would-be strong leaf is [+stress]. It preserves higher prominence and does NOT rerun LCPR (82), p.297. Thus articulatory retains suffix stress while contradictory reduces. The authors offer structural generalization as the rationale, not independent experimental proof of universal FF.
- Cycle, pp.298-301: morphological brackets and metrical brackets differ (compensation); relative prominence may reverse or disappear in derivatives (compensate/compensation/compensatory). Evidence for word-internal cyclicity is persistence of [+stress], unlike phrase-level preservation of relative prominence.
- Table (84), p.299, contrasts advantage/advantageous and infest/infestation with compensate/compensation and designate/designation. Base second-syllable stress predicts nonreduction in the derivative; prior unstressed syllables remain reducible. Weak nonlow vowels may coalesce with sonorants (complimentary/consolation/transformation), with lexical or optional variation; r-colored and syllabic-sonorant outcomes must not be confused with original stress loss.
- Secondary location in reciprocal/reciprocality, original/originality, municipal/municipality (85), p.300, differs from comparable monomorphemic Tatamagouchi/catamaran forms. Cyclic inheritance explains the shorter retraction distance without separately tagging every derived word as weak-retracting.
- Cyclic ESR (87), p.301, measures from a constituent edge (N/A/V), not only a word edge. Its optional light-syllable term may skip only [-stress] syllables, protecting inherited stress; its other skipped-syllable term must still admit stress for compensatory.
- Deforestation (88), p.301: before rules on each cycle, erase all prosodic structure within that domain, retaining segmental [+stress] marks. This is a substantive difference from Hayes (1982), which dispenses with the separate stress feature and preserves earlier feet except under specified redraw operations. Do not combine these cyclic state models without a new justified analysis.

## Final lexical rules and grid foundations (pp. 302-315)
- Derivation (89), pp.302-303 (landscape diagrams), tracks originality and compensatory across morphological cycles: retain stress marks, deforest, rerun ESR, then SSA/FF and word-level EDR. Final forms preserve original's medial stress while compensatory undergoes the appropriate restructuring/reduction. EDR remains word-level, not cyclic (p.304). Deforestation is explicitly a proposal needing further evidence; cyclic resyllabification (explain/explanation) is an analogy, not a proof.
- Final-prominence endings (90), p.305: -ier/-eer, -oon, -ique, -oo, -ise, -ade, -ette, -ee, -elle, -air, -che, -esce, -ane, -ar, -eau, -esque. A [+F] lexical class supplies local right prominence via LCPR (91); these are historical loanword/morpheme regularities, not a spelling-only guarantee.
- LCPR clause C (92), p.306, allows right prominence after an initial short-vowel open syllable unless the right node immediately dominates an affix; lithoid/ovoid/cathode remain left-prominent. The strong-leaf well-formedness constraint still blocks atom/gallop from assigning prominence to a stressless syllable.
- First ESR iteration must not skip an entire Latinate/Goidelic stem (consider/infer/repel); later iterations may do so (concentrate/recognize), and Greek stems differ. The authors leave these as lexical redundancies or possible Boolean conditions (p.306). Non-nominal ESR (93) is usually maximally penultimate: verbs and many adjectives stress a heavy final syllable; exceptions take the full nominal rule.
- Verbal/non-nominal prominence (95), p.307: right strong when left nonbranching, except direct -ate/-ize; verbs also right-strong when right directly dominates the stem. Table (94) records noun/verb contrasts survey, permit, record, etc. Some nouns retain the verbal pattern as lexical exceptions. American English -ate and -ize reject main stress in rotate/orate/baptize; the dialect qualification matters.
- Final LCPR (96), p.308: for sisters N1,N2 in category alpha, make N2 strong if (A) branching, (B) directly [+F], (C) N1 is word-initial short open and N2 is not an affix, or (D) alpha nonnominal/[+R] and either N1 nonbranching with N2 not -ate/-ize, or alpha verb with N2 directly stem. Otherwise N2 weak. [-I] sends exceptions straight to the elsewhere clause; [+R] permits the verbal clause for exceptional nouns. Sister complementarity determines N1.
- Adjectival compounds (97), pp.308-309, often follow nonnominal prominence (grass-green/Anglo-French/high-born); incorporating a noun often disables case D (color-blind). Wordlike verbal prefixes yield iambic compounds; zero-derived/backformed verbs may retain their source's prominence. The proposed locality restriction permits node category, parent category and immediately dominated material, not arbitrary remote inspection; crosslinguistic validation remains needed.
- Rhythm Rule, pp.309-311: thirteen men, Tennessee air and achromatic lens change relative prominence under embedding; changes also occur inside compound/phrase secondary relations. English retraction is asymmetric, unlike German; Hebrew is differently restricted. These differences rule out treating the process as a uniform phonetic universal. Separate pressure from rhythmic clash and language-specific permission to change.
- Adjacent syllable stresses are insufficient to define clash: -teen retains stress after retraction; achromatic lens clashes despite an intervening syllable. Grids (98)-(100), pp.312-313, expose adjacency at higher metrical levels.
- Grid formalism, pp.313-315: ordered finite levels L1...Ln, each an ordered set; an order-preserving counterpart function F maps each higher-level element into the immediately lower level. The adopted version defines F for every higher element, so all columns terminate on the lowest level. A more general partial-F version allows terminals on different levels but then requires an order-preserving alignment C to a temporal string; the paper sets it aside for exposition.
- Definitions (p.314): adjacent = same level with no intervening element; alternating = adjacent upper elements have nonadjacent counterparts one level down; clashing = those counterparts are adjacent. B maps a mark to its bottom/terminal counterpart, T to its highest counterpart, and L to its level. E1 is metrically stronger than E2 iff L(T(E1)) > L(T(E2)). Grid terminal marks map one-to-one onto syllables (p.315). These ordinal levels are not acoustic amplitude or duration values.

## Grid projection, rhythm, and limits (pp. 316-334)
- RPPR (104), p.316: the designated terminal of a strong daughter must be metrically stronger than the designated terminal of its weak sister. This is a standing well-formedness condition on order-preserving syllable/grid alignment, not a unique grid-construction algorithm. Formally, for each sister pair s,w: L(T(C^-1(DTE(s)))) > L(T(C^-1(DTE(w)))). Other inequalities need not be fixed.
- Input/output grids (105)-(107), pp.316-317, illustrate thirteen men, Tennessee air, achromatic lens. Montana cowboy (108) lacks the required clash and is less likely to retract; authors acknowledge occasional retraction (Salvation Army). Productive word/prefix boundaries alter the pattern (fn.18): pretonic lengthening versus frozen prevailing winds, invalid sample versus insipid coffee.
- Retraction does not simply shift a grid mark (109), p.318: Tennessee air can eliminate clashes at multiple levels and a superfluous grid element. The operation reverses a local tree prominence relation, and RPPR requires a new compatible grid.
- Iambic Reversal (111), p.319: optionally change sisters w-s to s-w, provided the second constituent does not contain the intonational phrase's designated terminal and the first is not an unstressed syllable. The latter follows from strong-leaf well-formedness. Chinese-expert and kangaroo-rider preserve their nuclear syllable; kangaroo-rider's saddle permits retraction (112), pp.319-320.
- Optionality (pp.320-321): clash raises preference for retraction but is not a formal trigger of the rule. Unretracted thirteen men remains possible with compensatory lengthening; constituent-final pseudopause can make clashes less costly. Marcel Proust/Marcel's book/Marcel's finished illustrate different preferences. Duration nuances exceed reliable intuitive investigation here; no measured probability or millisecond rule is supplied.
- Monosyllabic lexical entries need more grid representation than an ordinary pretonic syllable. Addendum, p.322: main stress, if any, of a #-level unit has at least two levels. This distinguishes good-looking lifeguard/pretonic lengthening from Montana cowboy (113)-(114), and predicts gym mat versus gymnast, tentacle tip versus catamaran (p.323). Unstressed function words are excepted; broader consequences remain to be explored.
- Partial ordering, pp.323-327: left-branching all-s-w and right-branching all-w-s chains require only the dominant terminal above the others; they do not force successive numerical demotions. Execute, knowledgeable, law degree requirement changes (115)-(116); polyvinyl chloride, John's three red shirts, reports of threats of violence (117)-(118). Extra prominence is permitted but not required; rebracketing may explain polyvinyl chloride (119), and an extra grid mark can strengthen three (120).
- The asymmetry between optional alternation among prenuclear upbeats and posttonic sequences is NOT predicted by the symmetric theory (p.327). A possible additional grid-foot partition is proposed in (121), not established as a required universal component.
- Relative clauses (122), p.328, obtain even prenuclear stress without syntactic flattening; normal clause boundaries can independently require intonational breaks. Possessive chains (123)-(124), pp.328-329, create triangular clash-rich grids that Iambic Reversal can simplify without changing constituency. Both parses of American history teacher (125), p.330, receive identical grid alignments; boundary timing may distinguish them without artificial secondary-stress number differences.
- Prior work, pp.330-333: principal foundation is Liberman (1975) and SPE. Rischel (1972) anticipates relational compound prominence but lacks subword hierarchy and grids; LP disagree that pitch/timing can generally replace syllabic stress order. Earlier Rischel (1964) and Fischer-Jorgensen (1948) were unavailable to these authors, so their priority attribution is explicitly indirect. Martin (1972) proposes rhythmic trees but alignment is underconstrained and can distort observed secondary stress (the big dog); LP's criticism is qualified by uncertainty about Martin's intended alignment guidance.
- Conclusion, pp.333-334: constituency supports local simultaneous prominence rules and gives disjunction/iteration a structural rationale. The separate grid describes clash, alternation and timing-related pressures; it is not interchangeable with the constituent tree. Binary stress presence still constrains prominence, destressing and reversal. These are theoretical explanatory claims over linguistic examples, without held-out TTS accuracy, acoustic tuning tables or a new controlled experiment.

## Implementation synthesis and load-bearing propositions
1. Keep ordered syllables, phonological weight, morphological domains/categories, lexical exceptions, binary stress presence and relative prominence distinct. ARPAbet 0/1/2 is an engineering output projection, not the paper's complete representation.
2. For this paper's lexical model, process inner morphological cycles first: deforest within the new domain, retain inherited stress marks, run cyclic ESR with disjunctive longest admissible expansion and metrical bracketing, then apply the word-level SSA/FF/EDR sequence and final prominence requirements. Derived stress cannot be recovered from syllable count or only the final suffix string.
3. Verify invariants after transformations: sister labels complementary; strong terminal has [+stress]; every final syllable attached; morphological and metrical bracketing not equated; weak does not imply [-stress].
4. Vowel length means the phonological input class, not synthesized duration. Surface vowel quality may obscure that class through later lengthening/reduction. Missing lexical/category inputs must be exposed, not silently guessed under a paper citation.
5. For phrase rhythm, use RPPR-compatible grids and optional local reversal with nuclear protection. Keep this downstream from lexical assignment; a lexical stress ticket does not require implementing phrase grids.
6. Qlatt relevance (engineering inference): stress policy must state its model, input vocabulary, rule ordering and exceptional/fallback behavior, preserve lexical output through inventory/HRG boundaries, and emit derivation provenance. Using Hayes's later foot-based cyclic mechanism is a different model choice, not a parameter setting of LP deforestation.

## Reproducible qualitative checks
- Contrast long/light/heavy syllable placement and weak/strong/long retraction, including hominoid/ellipsoid and designate.
- Trace odontology through stray singleton grouping, cursory through extrametricality and EDR, articulatory through FF without LCPR reevaluation.
- Contrast advantage/advantageous with compensate/compensation and original/originality with noncomplex retraction examples; assert intermediate inherited stress as well as output.
- Keep noun/verb category contrasts and lexical exceptions explicit; use the paper's American-English qualification for -ate.
- If implementing phrase rhythm separately, exercise thirteen men, achromatic lens, Montana cowboy, nuclear Chinese-expert and both American history teacher parses. Do not invent deterministic optionality from these judgments.

## Open questions and related work
- How should an operational lexicon encode underlying vowel length, category, stem boundaries, [+F]/[+R]/[-I], and retraction exceptions? The paper motivates but does not supply a machine-readable lexicon.
- Deforestation and cyclic resyllabification need additional evidence; Hayes (1982) directly revises the cyclic representation. Compare their complete derivations rather than combining individual formulas.
- The exact mapping from ordinal metrical strength to F0, duration, intensity and vowel quality requires separate phonetic sources. RPPR allows multiple grids; no unique acoustic contour follows.
- Further leads: Liberman (1975), Nanni (1976), Kahn (1976), Zonneveld (1976), Oehrle (1971), Halle (1973), Rischel (1972), and Kiparsky (1977). The bibliography is transcribed separately in citations.md.
