## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Compression vs Truncation**: Two strategies for dealing with phonetic pressure on nuclear accents in monosyllables. Compression (English type) squeezes all tones of a contour onto limited segmental material. Truncation (Hungarian type) leaves trailing tones unrealised when insufficient segmental material is available. (pp. 181-183)

- **Tonal space**: A speaker-specific normalised pitch range defined by upper and lower F0 reference points, analogous to "vowel space" for formants. Tonal targets are defined relative to this space rather than in absolute Hz. (pp. 192-193)

- **Pitch range level vs span**: Two partially independent dimensions of pitch range variation. *Level* = overall height of the voice (higher or lower). *Span* = width of the frequency range used (wider or narrower). These are at least partially independent and cannot be collapsed into a single "pitch range" parameter. (pp. 197-198)

- **Initialising approach**: Describes pitch features as relative to what has preceded in the utterance (syntagmatic, sequential). Each successive target is specified with respect to the previous one. Requires only a starting point and relative specifications. (pp. 189-190)

- **Normalising approach**: Describes pitch features as relative to the speaker's overall voice range (paradigmatic, speaker-specific). Defines an idealised tonal space with reference points and positions targets within it. (pp. 192-193)

- **Intrinsic vs extrinsic scaling factors**: Intrinsic factors involve the relative position of tonal targets within the tonal space (the T term in the pitch range equation). Extrinsic factors involve modifications of the tonal space itself (speaker level F_R, span N, and utterance range r). (pp. 205-206)

- **Broad focus**: Focus that applies to a whole constituent or sentence rather than a single word. The pattern that signals broad focus is similar or identical to the pattern signalling narrow focus on the final content word. (pp. 213-215)

- **Focus projection**: The idea that focus can "project" up a hierarchical syntactic structure from an accented word to a higher node that dominates an entire constituent, so the entire constituent is treated as focused. (p. 218)

- **Focus-to-Accent (FTA) approach**: A productive compromise between the "normal stress" and "highlighting" theories: it separates the semantic/pragmatic specification of which part of the utterance is focused from the phonological specification of where the accent falls. Normal stress = broad focus; contrastive stress = narrow focus; both involve the same kind of accent placement. (pp. 217-218)

- **Secondary association**: A mechanism where component tones of a pitch accent have primary association with the accented syllable but secondary association with specific structural edges or constituents (e.g., end of accented syllable, first mora). Used to describe alignment details in languages like Japanese and Catalan. (pp. 187-188)

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Pitch Range Normalisation Models

1. **Simple multiplicative model** (p. 204):
   - `F0 = F_R * T * r`
   - F_R = bottom-of-range reference level (Hz)
   - T = abstract pitch value for a given target in normal range
   - r = range multiplier (1.00 for normal range)

2. **Extended model with span normalisation** (p. 204):
   - `F0 = F_R * T * N * r`
   - N = speaker-specific span normalisation factor
   - This assigns invariant abstract pitch values T across speakers

3. **Additive + multiplicative model** (pp. 205-206):
   - Pure multiplicative model is inadequate because:
     - The utterance-final low is not perfectly constant
     - The "reference frequency" cannot be simply identified from a single utterance
     - The multiplicative model fails to predict quantitative regularities in range modification experiments
   - Pitch range modification involves both multiplicative scaling AND an additive component modifying the reference level itself
   - Raising the voice = expanding the pitch span from the bottom up (bottom remains relatively constant, top scales up)

### Earle's Normalisation (Vietnamese tones, p. 193):
- Assign 100 to top of speaker's average F0 range
- Assign 0 to bottom
- Express all tonal targets as percentage positions on this speaker-specific scale
- Result: normalised descriptions show high inter-speaker agreement for tone contour shapes

### Rose's z-score Normalisation (Wu Chinese, pp. 193-194):
- Based on speaker's mean F0 = 0
- Scale units in standard deviations
- F0 value 2 SD above mean = z-score of +2.00
- F0 value 1 SD below mean = z-score of -1.00

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

- **Compression in English**: When multiple tones must be realised on limited segmental material, English compresses them (all tones present, temporal spacing reduced). Example: L*+H L H% on monosyllable "Sue!?" produces rise-fall-rise compressed onto one syllable. (p. 181)

- **Truncation in Hungarian**: When material is insufficient, trailing tones are dropped. Hungarian question tune L*_H_L% on monosyllable only realises L*+H; final L% is left unrealised, producing simple rise. (pp. 181-182)

- **German/Dutch replacement strategy**: When high-fall-rise on final stressed syllable is under phonetic pressure, speakers use a different tune (simple rise) rather than compress or truncate. Dutch data shows significantly fewer high-fall-rise tunes on final accented syllables than expected. (pp. 183-184)

- **Pitch range bottom constancy**: The bottom of the speaking range (utterance-final low) is a fairly constant feature of an individual's voice; raising the voice primarily involves expanding the pitch span FROM THE BOTTOM UP. (p. 203)

- **Target correlation across speakers**: For Danish data (Gronnum), correlation between target F0 values for pairs of speakers reading the same sentences is extremely high (r >= 0.89, often 0.92). Same pattern holds across languages (Dutch, English, Scottish English, Cantonese). (pp. 201-202)

- **Within-speaker range correlation**: When the same speaker produces the same contours in high range ("involved") vs low range ("detached"), the correlation between target sets is also ~0.90. (p. 202)

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

- **English rise-fall-rise**: L*+H L H% -- compression on monosyllables produces complex pitch movement on single syllable (p. 181)

- **Hungarian question tune**: L*_H_L% -- truncation reduces to simple rise on monosyllables (p. 182)

- **German high-fall-rise question**: H* L H% on multi-syllable words; replaced by simple rise (H* HH%) or compressed (H* LH%) on monosyllables (pp. 183-184)

- **Efik three-way tonal distinction**: H, !H (downstepped H), and L on second syllable; demonstrates downstep as extrinsic scaling (pp. 207)

- **Spanish accent inventory** (Sosa 1999): Includes H*+L (triggers downstep), H*+H (sustained high level, as in Pierrehumbert's original analysis), and H+H* (extra height on nuclear accent in questions) (p. 209)

- **Star notation issues**: Three unresolved questions about the starred tone in bitonal accents: (1) Does the star have intrinsic paradigmatic meaning? (2) What exactly does syllable association mean for alignment? (3) What relationship between starred tone and perceived accent height? (pp. 185-186)

## Alignment and Timing

- **Starred tone alignment**: In Greek prenuclear rising accents, neither L nor H aligns in any obvious way with the accented syllable or vowel -- nor is there a fixed time interval between the two tones. The phonetic facts are not reducible to a simple distinction like L+H* vs L*+H. (p. 186)

- **Alignment as quantitative variable**: Atterer and Ladd (2004) suggested that alignment should be treated as a quantitative phonetic variable like VOT, and that it is misleading to categorise rising accents as L+H* or L*+H based on different languages/dialects. (pp. 186-187)

- **Primary vs secondary association**: Pitch accent has primary association with accented syllable; individual component tones may have secondary association with structural edges (end of syllable, first mora, etc.). (pp. 187-188)

## Cross-linguistic Data

- **English**: Compressing language -- all tones compressed onto available segmental material, even on monosyllables (p. 182)

- **Hungarian**: Truncating language -- limit of two tones per syllable; excess tones dropped (p. 182)

- **Greek**: Also compresses, at least for questions; L* of question accent is aligned earlier when on final stressed syllable (Arvaniti, Ladd, and Mennen 2006) (p. 182)

- **German**: Uses replacement strategy -- avoids high-fall-rise on final stressed syllables, substitutes simpler tunes (pp. 183-184)

- **Dutch**: Like German, avoids high-fall-rise on final accented syllables in questions (Lickley, Schepman, and Ladd 2005: 178f.) (p. 184)

- **Mambila** (Cameroon-Nigeria): Four level tones (High, High-Mid, Low-Mid, Low); normalised percentage scale (High=100, Low=0) yields High-Mid ~51-59% and Low-Mid ~27-35% consistently across five speakers despite large absolute Hz differences (Table 5.1, p. 196)

- **Vietnamese**: Earle (1975) normalised six tone contours using percentage scale; shows high inter-speaker agreement (pp. 193-194, Figure 5.3)

- **Wu Chinese**: Rose (1987) used z-score normalisation for tone contours (p. 193)

- **Danish**: Gronnum data shows extremely high inter-speaker target correlations (r >= 0.89) for sentence intonation (pp. 200-201)

- **Efik**: Three-way H/!H/L distinction demonstrates downstep as extrinsic scaling factor (Connell and Ladd 1990) (p. 207)

- **Spanish**: Sosa (1999) proposes H+H* accent type to represent extra pitch height in questions, arguing for extending initialising approach rather than using extrinsic scaling (pp. 209-210)

(p. 218)

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation / Pitch Scaling

- **Pitch range model for TTS**: Use a normalising model with at least two independent parameters:
  - **F_R** (reference frequency): The speaker's baseline low F0 (~utterance-final low). Relatively constant within a speaker.
  - **Span factor**: How far above F_R the tonal space extends. Can vary by speaking style, emotion, emphasis.
  - Equation: `F0 = F_R * T * N * r` where T is the abstract target, N normalises for speaker span, r adjusts for utterance-level range modification.

- **Pitch range bottom is approximately constant**: When raising or lowering the voice, the utterance-final low F0 is largely unaffected. Pitch range modification primarily involves expanding or contracting the span above this floor. Implement as: hold floor constant, scale targets proportionally above it.

- **Additive component needed**: Simple multiplicative scaling is insufficient. Pitch range modification also involves a shift in the reference level (additive component). For a TTS system, this means pitch range changes should combine multiplicative target scaling with a small additive shift to the baseline.

- **Normalised target values are highly consistent**: Across speakers, when F0 targets are expressed as percentages of speaker range (H*=100%, L%=0%), the proportional positions of intermediate targets (phrase accent, elbow, etc.) are remarkably consistent (r >= 0.89). This means a TTS system can use fixed proportional target values and simply scale them to the speaker's range.

### Compression/Truncation Strategies

- **For English TTS**: Implement compression -- when multiple tones must be realised on a single syllable or very short segmental material, compress all tones into the available time (shorten temporal gaps between targets, possibly reduce excursion size).
- The entire L*+H L H% sequence should be realisable on a single syllable (e.g., monosyllabic question "Sue!?").

### Accent Placement / Focus

- **Focus-to-Accent (FTA) framework for TTS accent placement**:
  1. Determine which constituent is focused (default: broad focus on entire sentence)
  2. Within the focused constituent, place the main accent on the most prominent word according to language-specific rules (typically last content word in English)
  3. Narrow focus overrides this: accent goes on the narrowly focused word
  4. The phonological form of the accent (H*, L+H*, etc.) is the same regardless of focus type -- only placement differs

- **Normal stress placement rule**: In broad focus, accent falls on the last "accentable" word in the phrase (typically the last content word). This is not contrastive; it is the default/neutral pattern.

### Level vs Span in Pitch Range

- For emotional/pragmatic modulation of pitch range in TTS, manipulate level and span independently:
  - Excited/emphatic speech: raise level AND widen span
  - Bored/depressed speech: lower level AND narrow span
  - But they can also be adjusted independently (e.g., raised level with narrow span for certain speaking styles)
