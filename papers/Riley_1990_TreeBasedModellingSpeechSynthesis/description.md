Riley applies CART decision trees to two speech-synthesis problems: a classification tree that decides whether a period ends a declarative sentence, and a regression tree that predicts segmental duration from a seven-segment phonetic window plus stress, lexical position, and phrase position. The duration tree, trained on 1500 hand-segmented utterances from one male speaker with each of 48 phones encoded as four multi-valued phonetic features, gives residuals with a 23 ms standard deviation against over 35 ms for the hand-derived Bell Labs rules on the same data, while end-of-sentence detection reaches 99.8% on the Brown corpus. This is the founding paper for data-driven segmental duration modelling and supplies a concrete feature set, a phone-encoding scheme, and calibrated error targets for any duration model in a formant or source-filter synthesizer, along with Riley's warning that lower mean error did not translate into strikingly better-sounding speech because the tree's occasional bad predictions in sparsely-trained contexts hurt more than a flatter rule set's uniform mediocrity.
