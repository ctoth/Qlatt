# Abstract

Harris, K. S. (1958). Cues for the discrimination of American English fricatives in spoken syllables. *Language and Speech*, 1(1), 1-7.

## Verbatim Abstract

It has sometimes been assumed that the identification of the fricatives of American English in CV syllables depends primarily on the characteristics of the noise (i.e., nonvocalic) portion of the speech sound. A second possibility is that characteristics of the vocalic portion -- previously shown to be cues for the perception of other consonants -- are important for the fricatives. These alternatives were tested by combining the noise from one spoken fricative-vowel syllable with the voiced portion of another. Results indicate that the important cues for the fricatives /s/ and /S/ are given by the noise but that the differentiation of /f/ and /theta/ is accomplished primarily on the basis of cues contained in the vocalic part of the syllable. Similar results were obtained for the voiced counterparts of these sounds.

## Interpretation

This is one of the earliest experimental demonstrations that different fricative classes rely on fundamentally different perceptual cues. For sibilants (/s/, /S/, /z/, /Z/), the friction noise spectrum alone is sufficient for identification. For non-sibilants (/f/, /theta/, /v/, /delta/), the vocalic transition (formant transitions into the following vowel) is the primary cue, and the friction noise alone is insufficient to distinguish them. This has direct implications for synthesizer design: sibilants need accurate noise spectral shaping, while non-sibilant fricatives need accurate formant transitions.
