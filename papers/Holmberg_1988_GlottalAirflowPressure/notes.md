# Glottal Airflow and Transglottal Air Pressure Measurements for Male and Female Speakers in Soft, Normal, and Loud Voice

**Authors:** Eva B. Holmberg, Robert E. Hillman, Joseph S. Perkell
**Year:** 1988
**Venue:** Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, Vol. 84, No. 2, August 1988, pp. 511-529
**DOI:** 0001-4966/88/080511-19$00.80

## One-Sentence Summary
Provides comprehensive normative data on glottal airflow waveform parameters and transglottal pressure across 45 speakers at three loudness levels, establishing quantitative male-female differences and loudness-dependent parameter relationships essential for voice source modeling.

## Problem Addressed
Prior to this study, quantitative group data for glottal airflow waveform measures and transglottal pressure were limited to small subject pools, making it difficult to establish reliable norms for normal vocal function or to understand how these parameters vary with loudness and sex.

## Key Contributions
- Establishes normative glottal waveform parameter values for 25 males and 20 females across soft, normal, and loud voice
- Identifies which parameters show significant male-female differences (period, peak flow, ac flow, open quotient, closing quotient, ac/dc ratio, maximum airflow declination rate)
- Demonstrates that soft, normal, and loud voice represent three different "vocal modes" with distinct vocal fold adjustments
- Provides extensive correlation tables showing parameter interrelationships within and across loudness conditions
- Introduces ac/dc ratio and vocal efficiency as measures of voice quality/breathiness

## Methodology
- 25 male, 20 female speakers; no speech/voice/hearing problems; age 17-30
- Speech material: /pae/ syllable strings repeated 5 times at 3 loudness levels (soft, normal, loud) plus 2 pitch conditions
- Noninvasive recording: oral airflow via circumferentially vented face mask, intraoral air pressure via catheter, sound pressure via microphone
- Transglottal pressure estimated from intraoral pressure during /p/ closures
- Glottal airflow derived by inverse-filtering oral airflow signal at 900 Hz (8-pole Bessel filter), with antiresonator canceling F1
- Measurements extracted interactively from middle 4 glottal cycles of the most typical token per loudness condition

## Key Equations

No formal equations are presented as derivations; the paper focuses on measurement definitions and statistical analyses. Key measurement definitions:

**Time-based parameters:**
- $T$ = fundamental period
- Open quotient: $(t_1 + t_2) / T$
- Speed quotient: $t_1 / t_2$ (ratio of opening to closing time)
- Closing quotient: $t_2 / T$

**Amplitude-based parameters:**
- Peak flow (l/s): maximum flow from zero baseline
- Minimum flow (l/s): flow at minimum during "closed" phase
- AC flow (l/s): peak flow minus minimum flow
- AC/DC ratio: RMS of ac portion around its mean / mean of ac portion (over 60-ms window centered at cycle midpoint)
- Maximum airflow declination rate (l/s²): max negative peak of first derivative of inverse-filtered flow

**Derived measures:**
- Vocal efficiency: sound power / (air pressure × average flow)
- Glottal resistance: pressure / average flow (cm H₂O/l/s)

**Acoustic theory prediction:**
- 6-dB SPL increase per doubling of maximum airflow declination rate (Fant, 1979)

## Implementation Details

### Signal Processing Pipeline (Fig. 1)
1. FM tape recording at 7½ in/s
2. **High-bandwidth flow**: Low-pass at 900 Hz (8-pole Bessel) → digitize at 8192 sps → inverse filter (remove F1 via antiresonator at ~70 Hz bandwidth, frequency from LPC) → "glottal waveform"
3. **Low-bandwidth flow**: Low-pass at 80 Hz (10th-order Butterworth) → digitize at 256 sps → "average flow" and "dc flow"
4. **Voice signal**: Low-pass at 3.5 kHz (elliptical, 110 dB/oct) → digitize at 8192 sps → RMS energy extraction
5. **Pressure**: Low-pass at 80 Hz → digitize at 256 sps → transglottal pressure estimation

### Glottal Waveform Extraction
- Inverse filtering at 900 Hz removes effects of vocal tract formants above F1
- Antiresonator fixed at 70 Hz bandwidth, frequency set to F1 center from LPC analysis of the selected token
- Interactive extraction: experimenter selects measurement points on glottal cycle visually
- 4 middle cycles from the most SPL-typical token (of 15 per loudness condition) used for waveform measures

### DC Flow Offset
- Almost universal finding of non-zero minimum flow during "closed" phase
- DC offset of 0.02-0.03 l/s attributed to vertical vocal fold movements (Stevens)
- Higher dc flow may indicate posterior glottal chink (incomplete closure)
- In soft voice, dc flow increases significantly; loud voice dc flow often decreases

## Results Summary

### Loudness Effects
- **Normal → Loud**: Increased pressure, increased peak/ac flow, increased MFDR, smaller open quotient, larger speed quotient, higher ac/dc. SPL increase ~13 dB for males predicted by 6 dB/doubling of MFDR; females show ~9 dB per doubling.
- **Normal → Soft**: Decreased pressure, decreased ac flow, increased minimum flow, more rounded/symmetrical waveform, larger open quotient, lower ac/dc. Suggests more relaxed vocal folds with more airflow leakage.
- Three loudness levels represent three distinct "vocal modes" with separate vocal fold adjustments, not a continuous change.

### Male-Female Differences
- **Normal voice**: Significant differences in period, MFDR, peak flow, ac flow, open quotient, closing quotient, ac/dc, speed quotient. Males have shorter closing phase, higher MFDR, higher ac flow, smaller open quotient.
- **Loud voice**: Significant differences for ALL glottal waveform parameters except minimum flow.
- **Soft voice**: Fewest differences — only period, peak flow, ac flow differ significantly. Male and female waveforms most similar in soft voice.
- Males have higher MFDR in normal and loud → more high-frequency energy in source spectrum.
- Females have more gradual closure → less high-frequency energy, contributing to perceived breathiness.

### Key Correlations (Appendix B)
- SPL correlates strongly with vocal efficiency (r > 0.87) and MFDR (r > 0.79) within loudness conditions
- MFDR correlates strongly with ac flow (r > 0.82) within conditions
- Average flow negatively correlates with minimum flow (r > -0.72 for males)
- Glottal resistance negatively correlates with average flow (r < -0.78)
- ac/dc ratio is a strong indicator of voice quality/breathiness

## Limitations
- Measurements from syllable repetitions (/pae/), not connected speech — may not generalize to natural speech
- Inverse filtering at 900 Hz means relationship to true vocal-tract excitation is approximate
- Time-based quotients (open, speed, closing) become unreliable when waveform is very rounded (soft voice)
- Glottal resistance limited by: sensitivity to speech rate, posterior chink effects, extreme values canceling out
- Large intersubject variation within each loudness condition
- Subjects chose "comfortable" loudness levels rather than prescribed SPL targets

## Testable Properties

- Open quotient decreases from soft to loud voice for males (0.73 → 0.60 → 0.57)
- Peak flow increases monotonically from soft to loud for males (0.27 → 0.38 → 0.49 l/s)
- MFDR increases monotonically from soft to loud for males (171 → 280 → 481 l/s²)
- Pressure increases monotonically from soft to loud for both sexes
- ac/dc ratio increases from soft to loud for males (0.39 → 0.60 → 0.74)
- Speed quotient increases from soft to loud for males (1.56 → 1.82 → 2.05)
- Within loudness conditions: SPL and MFDR positively correlated (r > 0.79)
- Within loudness conditions: SPL and vocal efficiency positively correlated (r > 0.87)
- Minimum flow is never significantly different between males and females
- Soft voice male and female waveforms are most similar (fewest significant differences)
- A 6-dB SPL increase should correspond approximately to a doubling of MFDR (acoustic theory)
- Vocal efficiency values range from 0.35×10⁻⁵ to 145.2×10⁻⁵ (×10⁵ units)
- ac/dc ≈ 0.5 is a rough critical boundary between "normal" and "breathy" voice (Isshiki, 1981)

## Relevance to Project

This paper provides essential normative data for Klatt voice source parameters. The quantitative values for open quotient, speed quotient, ac/dc ratio, and MFDR across loudness levels directly map to LF model parameters (Oq, αm) and Klatt parameters (OQ, TL, AV). The male-female differences in closing quotient and MFDR inform sex-dependent voice source presets: males need sharper closure (higher MFDR, lower OQ) while females need more gradual closure (lower MFDR, higher OQ, more spectral tilt). The loudness-dependent parameter changes provide the empirical basis for linking vocal effort to source parameters in the voice quality synthesis system.

Key mappings to Klatt/LF parameters:
- Open quotient → OQ (Klatt) / Oq (LF)
- Speed quotient → αm asymmetry (LF)
- AC/DC ratio → inversely related to TL spectral tilt (Klatt)
- MFDR → Ee excitation strength (LF)
- Minimum flow → relates to AH aspiration noise level
- Pressure → drives AV amplitude scaling
