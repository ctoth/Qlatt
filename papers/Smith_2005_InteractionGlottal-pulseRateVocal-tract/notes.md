# The interaction of glottal-pulse rate and vocal-tract length in judgements of speaker size, sex, and age

## One-Sentence Summary
Measures how glottal-pulse rate (GPR, the F0/source rate) and vocal-tract length (VTL, the formant-scale factor) jointly determine listeners' judgements of speaker height and speaker category (man/woman/boy/girl), using STRAIGHT-resynthesized isolated vowels scaled on a 7x7 log grid far beyond the natural human range, and shows VTL dominates size judgements while GPR and VTL contribute roughly equally to sex/age judgements inside the normal range. *(p.1)*

## Problem Addressed
GPR and VTL both covary with speaker size, sex, and age, but how the two acoustic dimensions **combine** in perception was unknown. Prior work characterized each cue separately, and was limited to natural recordings in which GPR and VTL are confounded and confined to the normal population range. This paper measures the full 2D interaction surface, including physically impossible GPR/VTL combinations. *(p.1, p.3, p.9)*

## Key Contributions
- A 2D perceptual surface of judged speaker **height** over the GPR x VTL plane, sampled on two overlapping 7x7 logarithmic grids. *(p.5-6, p.17)*
- A 2D perceptual surface of the four **sex/age categories** (man, woman, boy, girl) over the same plane, with p >= 0.5 consistency contours. *(p.7-8, p.18)*
- Quantified regression slopes of perceived size against ln(GPR) and ln(VTL), showing an **interaction**: the GPR slope depends on VTL and vice versa. *(p.6)*
- Demonstration that listeners categorize **unnatural** GPR/VTL combinations consistently, and that VTL decides the category in the low-GPR/short-VTL region (the "male dwarf" region, heard as "boy"). *(p.8)*
- Confirmation that two very different stimulus ranges sample the same underlying size surface, so range/context effects on the rating scale are modest. *(p.5-6)*

## Study Design
- **Type:** Within-subject psychophysical rating plus forced-choice categorization. Single-interval, two-response paradigm. *(p.4)*
- **Population:** 8 listeners (3 male, 5 female), aged 21-39, normal absolute thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 kHz. *(p.5)*
- **Stimuli:** 5 sustained English vowels /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/ from one adult male speaker (author R.P.), recorded in /hVd/ frames (haad, hayed, heed, hoed, who'd) with a Shure SM58-LCE microphone at 44.1 kHz. Vowels sustained (e.g. "haaaad") so a long stationary portion free of coarticulation with /h/ and /d/ could be isolated. *(p.3)*
- **Manipulation:** STRAIGHT resynthesis independently scaling GPR (time axis of the glottal-event sequence) and VTL (linear scaling of the spectral envelope on a linear frequency axis). *(p.3-4)*
- **Design:** 7 GPRs x 7 VTLs x 5 vowels = 245 trials per run, pseudo-random order; 5 runs per range per listener; two ranges (narrow, wide), starting range counterbalanced across listeners. Each run ~30 min. *(p.5)*
- **Tasks per trial:** (1) speaker height on a 7-point descriptive scale: very short, short, quite short, average, quite tall, tall, very tall; (2) speaker category: man, woman, boy, girl. Order of the two judgements left to the listener. Responses via buttons on a monitor-displayed response box. *(p.4)*
- **No feedback** (judgements are subjective). Level roved over 10 dB. *(p.4)*
- **Trials per plotted datum:** 200 for each GPR-VTL sample point in Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 (5 vowels x 8 listeners x 5 runs); 1400 for each collapsed point in Fig. 2 (200 x 7). *(p.16, p.17, p.18, p.19)*

## Methodology

### Source-filter scaling model (STRAIGHT)
STRAIGHT (Kawahara, Masuda-Kasuse, de Cheveigne 1999; Kawahara and Irino 2004) applies classical source-filter theory (Dudley 1939) to segregate GPR information from the spectral-envelope information tied to vocal-tract shape and length. It produces a **GPR-independent spectral envelope** that accurately tracks vocal-tract motion throughout the utterance. Once a vowel is decomposed into a GPR contour plus a sequence of spectral-envelope frames, it can be resynthesized with the spectral-envelope (frequency) dimension expanded/contracted and the GPR (time) dimension expanded/contracted, and **the two operations are largely independent**. *(p.3)*

- **GPR scaling** = expanding or contracting the **time axis of the sequence of glottal events**. *(p.3)*
- **VTL scaling** = compressing or expanding the **spectral envelope linearly along a linear frequency axis**. On a logarithmic frequency axis this is a rigid **translation of the whole envelope as a unit**. *(p.3-4)*
- **Quality caveat:** resynthesis remains high quality well beyond the normal range of human speech, **provided GPR is not much greater than the frequency of the first formant** (cf. Smith et al. 2005). *(p.3)*
- Liu and Kewley-Port (2004) reviewed STRAIGHT and commented favourably on its ability to manipulate formant-related information. *(p.3)*

### Spectral Envelope Ratio (SER) and its conversion to VTL
Change in VTL is expressed as the **spectral envelope ratio (SER)**: the ratio of the unit on the new frequency axis to that of the axis associated with the original recording. *(p.4)*

$$
\mathrm{SER} = \frac{\text{frequency-axis unit after scaling}}{\text{frequency-axis unit of the original recording}}
$$
*(p.4)*

- SER < 1 => vocal-tract **lengthening** (simulating larger men).
- SER > 1 => vocal-tract **shortening** (simulating smaller men, women, and children).

$$
\mathrm{VTL}_{\text{new}} = \frac{\mathrm{VTL}_{\text{ref}}}{\mathrm{SER}}
$$
Where VTL_ref = 15.5 cm, the VTL of the average adult male (Fitch and Giedd 1999); SER is dimensionless; VTL in cm. This follows from the three stated assumptions: (a) the speaker of the original vowels was of normal height, (b) the average adult male VTL is 15.5 cm, and (c) **formant frequencies scale linearly with VTL** (Fant 1970). *(p.4)*

Worked examples (footnote 5, p.4): SER = 0.58 means the spectral envelope of the initial vowel has been compressed by a factor of 1.72 (= 1/0.58) -> VTL = 26.8 cm, "a giant" of about 3.7 m. SER = 2.39 means the envelope has been dilated by 0.42 -> VTL = 6.5 cm, "a tiny child" about 0.6 m tall. Size-for-SER estimates were extrapolated from the VTL-versus-height data in Fitch and Giedd (1999, Fig. 2a), where the average VTL for seven men aged 19-25 was 15.54 cm. *(p.4)*

### Stimulus post-processing chain *(p.4)*
1. Scale GPR and VTL with STRAIGHT.
2. Apply a **cosine-squared gating function**: 10 ms onset, 465 ms plateau, 30 ms offset, selecting a stationary portion of the vowel.
3. Set RMS level to **0.025** relative to maximum (+/-1 full scale).
4. Play out via a 24-bit sound card (Audigy 2, Sound Blaster) -> TDT anti-aliasing filter with sharp cutoff at **10 kHz** -> final attenuator -> binaural presentation over AKG K240DF headphones.
5. Presentation level **66 dB SPL**, roved in intensity over a **10 dB** range. Listeners seated in a double-walled IAC sound-attenuating booth.

### Analysis / surface construction
- The two 7x7 grids were merged into one **13 x 13 matrix** (the middle row and column of both ranges coincide, at GPR 179 Hz and VTL 13.2 cm). Any empty cell in that matrix was filled by the **average of all adjoining cells** in which a rating had been collected. The plotted surface was then derived by **interpolation** between sample points and their averaged neighbours. *(p.6, footnote 6)*
- The 7 size categories were assigned **ordinal values 1 to 7** ("very short" = 1 to "very tall" = 7) and mapped to colour from dark blue (1) to brown-red (7). *(p.6)*
- Size ratings were regressed on the **natural logarithm** of GPR and on the natural logarithm of VTL. *(p.6)*
- Significance of the GPR/VTL-versus-size relationships was tested with a **one-tailed Spearman rank-order correlation** (nonparametric). *(p.6)*
- Sex/age panels plot P(category) with the four panels summing to 1.0 at each GPR-VTL point; the **classification threshold contour is p >= 0.5**, marking regions where listeners consistently choose one of the four categories. *(p.7, p.18)*

## Key Equations / Statistical Models

$$
S = a \cdot \ln(\mathrm{GPR}) + b
$$
Where S is the speaker-size rating on the 1-7 ordinal scale; GPR in Hz; a is the fitted slope, reported per VTL condition (Fig. 5). *(p.6)*

$$
S = c \cdot \ln(\mathrm{VTL}) + d
$$
Where S is the size rating (1-7); VTL in cm; c is the fitted slope, reported per GPR condition (Fig. 6). Slopes are reported as positive gradients with respect to decreasing VTL along the plotted abscissa; i.e. size falls as VTL shortens. *(p.6)*

$$
\sum_{k \in \{\text{man},\text{woman},\text{boy},\text{girl}\}} P_k(\mathrm{GPR}, \mathrm{VTL}) = 1
$$
Where P_k is the probability of assigning a vowel with the given GPR-VTL combination to category k. A region is declared a consistent category region when P_k >= 0.5. *(p.7, p.18)*

### Normal-population ellipse geometry *(p.7)*
- Each ellipse = mean +/- 3 SD, containing 99% of individuals in that Peterson and Barney category. *(p.4, p.15)*
- The **men's ellipse does not intersect** the boys' or girls' ellipses.
- The **women's ellipse intersects the ellipses of all three other groups**.
- The **boys' ellipse lies almost entirely inside the girls' ellipse**.
- Overlap of boys' and women's ellipses is about **50%**.

## Methods & Implementation Details
- STRAIGHT is used purely as a source-filter analysis/resynthesis engine: F0 contour + spectral-envelope frame sequence, then independent rescaling of the two. *(p.3)*
- The vowel identity is carried by vocal-tract **shape** (tongue placement), which fixes the relative positions of formants; VTL sets the **scale** of the whole pattern along the frequency axis. Footnote 1 states the paper assumes a fixed vocal-tract shape across all simulated speakers, i.e. every speaker utters the same vowel. *(p.2)*
- Because all vowels derive from one adult male speaker, **formant ratios (F2/F1) for a given vowel are identical across all GPR-VTL combinations**. This minimizes one source of variability but is not typical of the population: F2/F1 increases slowly with height, because throat length grows relatively more than mouth length during development (Diehl, Lindblom, Hoemeke, Fahey 1996; Turner, Walters, Patterson 2004 using Fitch and Giedd MRI mouth/throat measurements). *(p.9)*
- Ellipse calibration: formant values for each of the 76 speakers in Peterson and Barney (1952) were converted to VTL using the Fitch and Giedd (1999) MRI-derived VTL data; details in Turner, Walters, and Patterson (2004). *(p.4, footnote 4)*
- Footnote 3 flags a wording risk in the size scale: in British English "quite" means "to some extent", and since the scale was presented graphically and continuously, "quite short"/"quite tall" were most likely read as halfway between the neighbouring labels. *(p.4)*
- Scaled-vowel demonstrations were published at http://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/cnbh/web2002/framesets/Soundsframeset.htm (footnote 2, p.3); ellipse calibration poster at .../posters_talksframeset.htm (footnote 4, p.4).

## Results Summary
- Both stimulus ranges show the same effects, and the combined-fit lines describe the pooled data excellently, so the two ranges were pooled for subsequent analysis. For GPR column averages the narrower-range slope is slightly shallower than the wider-range slope; for VTL row averages the reverse holds. *(p.5-6)*
- Very low GPR combined with very long VTL is reliably heard as a very tall person; raising GPR or shortening VTL reliably reduces reported size, with a marked fall-off as VTL shortens. *(p.5)*
- **Little evidence of learning** over the course of the experiment: listeners perform at near-asymptotic level almost straight away. *(p.5)*
- **Interaction in size judgements:** simulated speakers with VTL = 6.5 cm ("two feet tall") are always judged short regardless of GPR; simulated 12-foot giants (VTL = 26.8 cm) are always heard as above average height but their estimated height declines as GPR rises. The VTL effect weakens as GPR increases. *(p.6-7)*
- **Sex/age quadrant structure** *(p.7-8)*:
  - Low GPR + long VTL (lower-left): overwhelmingly **man**; contains the men's ellipse. Below the p >= 0.5 contour the probability drops rapidly, and few "man" responses appear above the negative diagonal.
  - High GPR + short VTL (upper-right): predominantly **girl**; contains both the girls' and boys' ellipses, but the girls' ellipse extends to higher GPR and shorter VTL so "girl" is the more natural extension. "Girl" is almost never used below the negative diagonal.
  - "Man" and "girl" are used consistently and their GPR-VTL regions are mutually exclusive. *(p.7)*
  - Low GPR + short VTL (upper-left): predominantly **boy**, P(boy) > 0.5 over most of the quadrant, even though the boys' normal-population ellipse does not fall in this quadrant at all. The authors' interpretation: these voices sound like males who are unusually short, the condition of **male dwarves** (pitch drops at puberty but the vocal tract does not lengthen proportionately). There is no corresponding female-dwarf vocal category: female dwarves continue to sound like girls because their pitch drop is relatively small. On the 179 Hz border, "boy" is far more likely than "woman" or "girl". *(p.8)*
  - High GPR + long VTL (lower-right): most common responses are **man** at relatively lower GPR and **boy** at relatively higher GPR, both with long VTL. "Boy" is reasonable for a high-pitched voice perceived to come from a tall person. *(p.8)*
- **"Woman" is predominant only in one small central region** where P > 0.5, and the peak of that region is close to the centre of the women's ellipse, despite listeners having no explicit knowledge of these distributions. "Woman" is also used for vowels with higher-than-normal-women GPRs **provided the VTL is the same or longer** than that of normal women. Shorten VTL and listeners switch to "girl"; lengthen it and they switch to "boy". *(p.8)*
- **Category cues are genuinely combined:** if GPR were the sole determinant, listeners could reliably classify only men below ~155 Hz and girls above ~330 Hz; if VTL were the sole determinant, only men with VTL >= ~16 cm and girls with VTL <= ~10 cm. Observed sex classification performance is much better than either single-cue bound. *(p.11)*

## Limitations
- All vowels come from **one adult male speaker**, so F2/F1 formant ratios are constant across the whole plane, unlike the real population where F2/F1 rises slowly with height. The effect of this on the size and sex/age judgements is unclear; the authors expect that rerunning with an adult female source speaker would raise the proportion of "woman" responses across much of the range. They declare this beyond the paper's scope. *(p.9)*
- Only **isolated, sustained, stationary vowels** were used, not connected speech; no prosody, no consonants, no dynamic formant motion. *(p.3-4)*
- The rating scale is ordinal and subjective, and no feedback or ground-truth height calibration was given, so ratings measure consistency rather than accuracy. *(p.4)*
- The rectangular sampling plane oversamples GPR-VTL combinations that do not occur naturally, producing a nonuniform marginal distribution of category responses (P(woman) only 0.11). *(p.8)*
- The size surface is built from **interpolated and neighbour-averaged** cells in the merged 13x13 matrix, so intermediate values are not directly measured. *(p.6, footnote 6)*
- Only 8 listeners, all aged 21-39, all presumably native/UK listeners; British English reading of "quite" may have shifted the scale mapping. *(p.4, p.5)*
- The strong VTL effect may be partly an artefact of the **very wide VTL range** used, which could encourage listeners to lend extra weight to VTL. In normal speech pitch is more salient than VTL, consistent with the 2% JND for pitch versus the 6-10% JND for VTL. *(p.10)*

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- Prior speaker-size perception studies were **limited to the restricted range of heights of natural speakers**, typically adult men only (Lass and Davis 1976; van Dommelen and Moxness 1995; Collins 2000). Listeners were consistent but **not accurate** (van Dommelen and Moxness 1995; Collins 2000), though Lass and Davis reported better-than-chance categorization. *(p.9)*
- Those studies used only normal-range adult voices, where the correlation between speaker size and formant frequency within same-sex adults is significant but **weak** (Gonzalez 2004; Rendall, Vokey, Nemeth, Ney 2005), so accurate height judgement should be hard, which conflicts with listeners' apparent confidence. The authors resolve this by hypothesizing that the height/VTL/formant correlation seen in close hominoid species such as rhesus monkeys (Fitch 1997) has become **dissociated in adult humans**, possibly through human-specific changes such as the descent of the larynx in adult men (Fitch 1997, 2000). *(p.9)*
- **For all natural recorded voices, GPR and VTL cues are confounded**; only synthetic speech can tease them apart while spanning a wide height range. *(p.9)*
- Fitch (1994, PhD thesis) used a rating scale with computerized vowels and found main effects of both GPR and VTL, but was **restricted to the middle-to-upper normal range for men only**. This paper uses a much greater range, simulating tiny children, giants, castrati, and dwarves, made possible by STRAIGHT. *(p.9-10)*
- Statistical clustering and perceptual classification studies of speaker sex are **limited to databases of normal, largely homogeneous adult male and female voices**, so the range over which the independent variables could be manipulated was necessarily limited. *(p.11)*
- The prior literature on the relative strength of GPR versus VTL as a sex cue is **contradictory**: some studies show vocal-tract information alone suffices (Schwartz 1968; Ingemann 1968; Schwartz and Rine 1968); others report GPR is a much stronger cue than VTL (Lass et al. 1976); Whiteside (1998) finds formant information important but pitch generally dominant; Bachorowski and Owren (1999) find excellent accuracy using only GPR or only VTL, but best using both, and separately find GPR much less effective than VTL at classifying individual speakers. *(p.9-11, p.2)*
- GPR is a poor cue to size within a sex because the vocal folds are **not constrained by any bony structure**, unlike the vocal tract which scales with the cranium (Negus 1949; discussed in Fitch 1997), and because prosody routinely repurposes GPR for sentence-level distinctions. *(p.10, p.2)*

## Design Rationale
- **Why STRAIGHT rather than natural recordings:** it is the only way to decouple the confounded GPR and VTL cues while covering a height range from tiny children to giants, and it maintains high resynthesis quality outside the natural range. *(p.9-10, p.11)*
- **Why two overlapping ranges:** the narrower range covers the normal population; the wider range extends far beyond it. The overlap (shared centre point and shared middle row/column) permits an **across-condition consistency test** revealing how different input ranges are stretched onto the fixed seven-point response scale and how the frame of reference shifts the mapping. *(p.5)*
- **Why counterbalance the starting range:** to prevent order/anchoring effects between the two frames of reference. *(p.5)*
- **Why a single-interval rather than 2AFC task:** previous work established relative discrimination thresholds (6-10%), but a single-interval rating task forces the listener to judge size against an internalized long-term frame of reference, testing whether **absolute size information can be extracted from an individual voiced sound**. The authors argue their listeners' good performance supports that claim. *(p.10)*
- **Why one source speaker and one vocal-tract shape:** minimizes variability from formant-ratio differences, isolating VTL as a pure scale factor. Acknowledged as a departure from population reality. *(p.2 footnote 1, p.9)*
- **Why the level rove and no feedback:** removes loudness as a size cue and keeps the judgement subjective. *(p.4)*
- **Theoretical motivation:** the authors hypothesize the auditory system applies a **scale-invariant neural transform** normalizing sounds for size before more central processing such as speaker identification (Irino and Patterson 2002, stabilised wavelet-Mellin transform; Turner et al. 2005). The experiments test whether the size information so extracted informs natural sex/age categorization. *(p.11)*

## Testable Properties
- Perceived size is a **monotonically decreasing** function of GPR at long VTL and **flat** at very short VTL: slope on ln(GPR) is -1.04 at VTL 26.8 cm, -0.40 at 13.2 cm, 0.01 at 6.5 cm. *(p.6)*
- Perceived size is a **monotonically decreasing** function of decreasing VTL at all GPRs, with gradient on ln(VTL) of 3.57, 3.06, and 1.50 at GPR 61, 179, and 523 Hz. *(p.6)*
- **Every VTL slope exceeds every GPR slope** in magnitude: VTL dominates size perception in this coordinate system. *(p.6)*
- The GPR and VTL slopes are **not independent**: the GPR slope magnitude decreases as VTL shortens, and the VTL slope magnitude decreases as GPR rises. This is the interaction. *(p.6-7)*
- Size ratings span nearly the full 1-7 scale over the tested plane: 1.7 to 6.2. *(p.6)*
- At each GPR-VTL point the four category probabilities sum to 1.0. *(p.7)*
- The P(man) >= 0.5 region and the P(girl) >= 0.5 region are **disjoint** and lie on opposite sides of the negative diagonal of the GPR-VTL plane. *(p.7)*
- P(boy) > 0.5 over most of the low-GPR/short-VTL quadrant, despite the boys' natural ellipse not lying there. *(p.8)*
- The P(woman) >= 0.5 region is small and its peak coincides approximately with the centre of the Peterson and Barney women's ellipse. *(p.8)*
- Holding GPR above the normal female range, "woman" survives as a response only if VTL >= normal female VTL; shortening VTL switches the response to "girl" and lengthening it switches to "boy". *(p.8)*
- Marginal category probabilities over the rectangular plane: man 0.36, woman 0.11, boy 0.36, girl 0.17. *(p.8)*
- STRAIGHT resynthesis quality holds as long as **GPR is not much greater than F1**. *(p.3)*
- SER and VTL are reciprocally related through VTL_ref = 15.5 cm. *(p.4)*
- Judgement performance is at asymptote from the first run: no learning effect. *(p.5)*

## Relevance to Project
This is a calibration and validation paper for any formant/source-filter synthesizer that wants to offer speaker-identity controls rather than raw acoustic knobs.

- **Two-knob speaker model.** It confirms that F0 (GPR) and a single formant-scale factor (VTL) are sufficient to move a synthetic voice convincingly across man/woman/boy/girl and short/tall. A synthesizer can expose exactly these two macro-controls over an existing vowel/formant specification.
- **Formant scaling law.** VTL scaling is implemented as a **uniform multiplicative scaling of all formant frequencies** (linear scaling of the spectral envelope on a linear frequency axis, equivalently a rigid translation on a log axis), with formant frequency inversely proportional to VTL. The SER is the direct multiplier: F_new = SER x F_original, and VTL_new = 15.5 / SER cm. Bandwidths are not discussed, which is itself worth noting as an open point.
- **Reference constant.** VTL 15.5 cm as the average adult male anchor lets a synthesizer convert between a physical VTL parameter and a formant multiplier with one division.
- **Target regions for preset voices.** Figure 1's ellipses (mean +/- 3 SD from Peterson and Barney 1952) give concrete GPR-VTL boxes for man, woman, boy, girl presets, and Figure 4 gives the regions where listeners actually agree with those labels. Notably these are not the same: the paper shows that the perceptual "boy" region extends into low-GPR/short-VTL territory no real boy occupies.
- **Where to place a "woman" preset.** P(woman) exceeds 0.5 only in a small central region. A synthesizer aiming for an unambiguous female adult voice has a narrow target and should sit near GPR ~179-220 Hz with VTL near 13-14 cm; drifting shorter in VTL yields "girl", longer yields "boy".
- **Quality guardrail.** Keep GPR below roughly F1 when scaling, or the source-filter resynthesis degrades. For a synthesizer this is a validity constraint on simultaneously raising F0 and shortening the tract.
- **Perceptual resolution budget.** VTL changes below about 6-7% are not reliably discriminable, while pitch resolution is about 2%. This sets a sensible quantization for a VTL/size control: roughly 5% steps are already near the perceptual floor, whereas F0 needs finer steps.
- **Test oracle.** The regression slopes (-1.04, -0.40, 0.01 for GPR at VTL 26.8/13.2/6.5 cm; 3.57, 3.06, 1.50 for VTL at GPR 61/179/523 Hz) are directly usable as a listening-test or model-based regression target for a synthesizer's speaker-size control.
- **Independence claim.** STRAIGHT's independence of source-rate and envelope scaling is the same architectural claim a source-filter synthesizer makes. This paper is evidence that the independence holds well enough perceptually over an enormous parameter range.

## Open Questions
- [ ] What happens to size and sex/age judgements when the source speaker is female, or when F2/F1 is allowed to vary with simulated height as it does in the real population? The authors flag this explicitly as unresolved. *(p.9)*
- [ ] Are formant **bandwidths** scaled along with formant frequencies by STRAIGHT's envelope scaling, and does bandwidth matter perceptually for size? Not addressed in the paper.
- [ ] Would connected speech with prosody, rather than isolated sustained vowels, shift the balance back toward GPR, given prosody's routine use of GPR? *(p.2, p.10)*
- [ ] Is the strong VTL weighting an artefact of the unusually wide VTL range presented, as the authors themselves speculate? *(p.10)*
- [ ] What is the exact functional form of the size surface away from the sampled grid points, given the interpolation and neighbour-averaging used to build it? *(p.6)*
- [ ] How does voice quality (breathiness, spectral tilt), which differs systematically between male and female speakers, interact with these two dimensions? Coleman (1976) is cited on voice-quality contributions but not tested here.
