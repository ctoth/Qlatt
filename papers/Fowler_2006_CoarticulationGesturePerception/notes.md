# Compensation for Coarticulation Reflects Gesture Perception, Not Spectral Contrast

**Authors:** Carol A. Fowler
**Year:** 2006
**Venue:** Perception & Psychophysics, 68(2), 161-177
**Institution:** Haskins Laboratories, New Haven, CT; University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT

## One-Sentence Summary
This paper provides strong experimental evidence that listeners compensate for coarticulation by tracking articulatory gestures rather than by applying general auditory spectral contrast, with direct implications for how coarticulation must be modeled in synthesis to sound natural.

## Problem Addressed
Three competing theories explain how listeners handle coarticulation in speech perception:
1. **Motor theory** (Liberman & Mattingly, 1985): Listeners recover intended gestures via an innate phonetic module
2. **Direct realism** (Fowler, 1986, 1996): Listeners perceive gestural sources directly from acoustic structure
3. **Spectral contrast** (Lotto, Holt, Kluender): Compensation is a general auditory process that reduces sensitivity near preceding spectral energy

Fowler argues the spectral contrast account is too narrow and presents three experiments to disconfirm it.

## Key Contributions
- Demonstrates that compensation for coarticulation occurs even with no left or right spectral context to induce contrast (Experiment 1)
- Shows an **enhancing** (not contrastive) effect of coarticulatory information on identification of preceding syllables (Experiment 2) — directly opposite to spectral contrast predictions
- Demonstrates that tone-syllable sequences show contrastive effects opposite to the enhancing effects found with speech (Experiment 3), ruling out a general auditory account
- Provides extensive formant data for /da/-to-/ga/ continua in VCV contexts with /l/ and /r/ coarticulation (Appendix Tables A1, A2)

## Methodology

### Experiment 1: Compensation Without Precursor Context
- **Stimuli**: Three 11-member /da/-to-/ga/ hybrid continua from natural speech
  - Continuum 1: /ada/ and /aga/ disyllables
  - Continuum 2: /alda/ and /alga/ (with /l/ carryover coarticulation)
  - Continuum 3: /arda/ and /arga/ (with /r/ carryover coarticulation)
- **Method**: Listeners heard just the second syllable (/da/-/ga/ portion), with NO precursor syllable present
- **Key finding**: Even without the precursor, listeners showed compensation — more /ga/ responses when the CV had been coarticulated with /al/ vs /ar/
- **Significance**: Rules out spectral contrast, since there was no preceding context to create contrast

### Experiment 2: Enhancing Effect of Coarticulatory Information
- **Stimuli**: Endpoint monosyllables from Experiment 1 continua, presented as VCs followed by ambiguous CVs
- **Method**: Listeners identified first syllable as containing /l/ or /r/
- **Key finding**: /l/ and /r/ information in /da/ and /ga/ syllables **enhanced** identification of /l/ and /r/ in the precursor — opposite to what contrast predicts
- **Significance**: Coarticulatory information enhances (not reduces) sensitivity to the gestural source

### Experiment 3: Tones Replacing Precursor Syllables
- **Stimuli**: Six tones (2000-3000 Hz in 200 Hz steps, 300 ms) appended to /da/ and /ga/ tokens from Experiment 2
- **Method**: Listeners classified tones as "high" or "low"
- **Key finding**: Contrastive effect — opposite direction to Experiment 2's enhancing effect
- **Significance**: When speech context is replaced by nonspeech, the effect reverses direction, disconfirming that a common auditory process underlies both

## Key Equations
No formal equations — this is a perceptual/experimental paper. Statistical results reported as ANOVAs.

## Implementation Details

This is primarily a perception study, not an implementation paper. However, it contains valuable data for synthesis:

### Coarticulation Patterns Documented
1. **Carryover coarticulation of /l/ and /r/**: Affects F3 of following stops — /r/ context dramatically lowers F3 at syllable boundaries (e.g., F3 of /arda/ syll 1 end = 1718 Hz female / 1687 Hz male vs /alda/ = 3187 Hz / 2968 Hz)
2. **Coarticulation persists into the following CV**: The /da/-/ga/ distinction is modified by the preceding /l/ or /r/, showing that listeners can extract this information even from the second syllable alone
3. **Hybrid continua**: Created by splicing the vowel remainder of one context (/da/) with the consonant closure + release of another (/ga/), systematically varying percent /d/ amplitude

### Critical F3 Observations for /r/ Coarticulation
- /r/ lowers F3 dramatically at syllable boundaries: ~1700-1800 Hz vs ~2800-3200 Hz for /l/ or plain /d,g/ contexts
- This F3 lowering carries over into the following syllable onset
- The ending F3 of /ar/ (~2150-2343 Hz) vs /al/ (~3059-3187 Hz) is the primary acoustic cue that listeners use

## Limitations
- Uses only two speakers (C.A.F. and D.H.)
- Only tests /da/-/ga/ continua with /l/ and /r/ coarticulation — doesn't generalize to all coarticulation types
- The hybrid splicing technique may introduce artifacts at splice points
- Does not provide a complete formal model of how gesture tracking works computationally
- The rebuttal of Lotto & Holt (2006) at the end suggests ongoing theoretical debate about interpretation

## Testable Properties
- Coarticulatory F3 lowering from /r/ should persist across a stop consonant boundary (~500 Hz lower than /l/ context)
- Listeners should identify ambiguous /da/-/ga/ members differently depending on coarticulatory context even without the preceding syllable
- Enhancing effects (not contrastive) should occur when coarticulatory information affects identification of the gestural source
- Tone-speech comparison should show opposite effect directions for same acoustic continuum

## Relevance to Project

### Primary relevance: projects/soliton-gestures

This paper is the strongest empirical anchor for Fowler's coproduction theory, which the soliton-gestures outline (Section 2.2) identifies as "philosophically soliton-native but never formalized dynamically." The specific connections:

1. **Gesture identity preservation through overlap (= elastic scattering)**: Experiment 1 shows that coarticulatory information from /l/ and /r/ survives through stop consonant closures and remains recoverable from the second syllable alone. This is exactly the "identity preservation through collision" property of solitons. In the lattice model, the /l/ or /r/ gesture and the /d/ or /g/ gesture overlap (collide), yet the /l/-/r/ identity emerges intact on the other side, detectable by listeners.

2. **Enhancing effect (= scattering preserves information, doesn't destroy it)**: Experiment 2's enhancing effect — where coarticulatory traces in the CV *help* identification of the gestural source — is the perceptual correlate of elastic scattering. If coarticulation were "blending" (inelastic collision), information should be lost. Instead, it's preserved and usable. This directly supports Section 4.3's claim that "coarticulation as elastic scattering" replaces "coarticulation as blending."

3. **Listeners as inverse scatterers (Section 8.3)**: Fowler's direct realism — listeners perceive gestural sources from acoustic structure — maps directly to "gesture recovery = inverse scattering." The paper provides the empirical ground truth: listeners DO recover individual gestures from coarticulated (overlapped/scattered) speech. The soliton framework provides the mathematics for how this is possible.

4. **Against spectral contrast = against diffusive coupling interpretation**: The spectral contrast account is formally analogous to diffusive coupling (monotonic frequency-dependent adaptation). Fowler's disconfirmation of spectral contrast as the mechanism is consistent with the soliton framework needing dispersive (not purely diffusive) coupling for elastic collision dynamics. Section 6.1's coupling character test should find non-monotonic decay, not the monotonic decay spectral contrast would predict.

5. **Coarticulation is not corruption**: Fowler's theoretical position — that coarticulation is overlap of invariant gestures, not smearing of a discrete plan — is quoted almost verbatim in the outline's introduction: "The standard explanation presupposes discreteness in the underlying plan and treats continuity as corruption (coarticulation as smearing). This inverts the explanatory burden."

### Key data for soliton-gestures empirical validation

- The appendix F3 data shows ~1000-1500 Hz difference between /l/ and /r/ coarticulatory contexts at syllable boundaries — this is a **measurable phase shift** in formant space. The question is whether this shift shows the functional form predicted by the equation parameters (Section 6.3).
- The VCCV sequences (/alda/, /arda/, /alga/, /arga/) are exactly the "different-target gestures on the same tract variable" that Section 4.3 identifies as proper soliton collision tests.
- Two speakers (female C.A.F. and male D.H.) provide within-experiment replication.

### Secondary relevance: Qlatt synthesis
- F3 carryover from /r/ must be modeled across stop boundaries
- Appendix tables provide formant transition targets for /l/ and /r/ contexts
- Listeners are highly sensitive to coarticulatory detail — synthesis must model cross-boundary coarticulation faithfully
