# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

The paper does not contain a traditional abstract section. The opening section (Section 1, "A gestural phonology") serves as the introduction:

Over the past few years, we have been investigating a particular hypothesis about the nature of the basic 'atoms' out of which phonological structures are formed. The atoms are assumed to be primitive actions of the vocal tract articulators that we call 'gestures'. Informally, a gesture is identified with the formation (and release) of a characteristic constriction within one of the relatively independent articulatory subsystems of the vocal tract (i.e. oral, laryngeal, velic). Within the oral subsystem, constrictions can be formed by the action of one of three relatively independent sets of articulators: the lips, the tongue/blade and the tongue body. As actions, gestures have some intrinsic time associated with them -- they are characterisations of movements through space and over time.

Within the view we are developing, phonological structures are stable 'constellations' (or 'molecules', to avoid mixing metaphors) assembled out of these gestural atoms. In this paper, we examine some of the evidence for, and some of the consequences of, the assumption that gestures are the basic atoms of phonological structures.

---

## Our Interpretation

Browman and Goldstein argue that the basic units of phonology are not abstract symbols (segments, features) but physical articulatory gestures -- coordinated movements that form constrictions in independent vocal tract subsystems. These gestures are formally specified in a computational task-dynamic model and organized into temporal patterns called "gestural scores." The framework explains connected-speech phenomena (deletion, assimilation, coarticulation) as natural consequences of varying gestural overlap and magnitude, and proposes a tube geometry that derives manner-class distinctions from the combined constriction degrees of concurrent gestures.
