## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Focus-to-Accent (FTA) view
The theory that accent placement in sentences is determined by the grammar -- the location of the main accent is governed by language-specific structural rules. Contrasts with the highlighting view.

### Highlighting view (Bolinger)
The theory that accent is a universal prelinguistic gesture that directly signals the speaker's assessment of relative informativeness or salience. Accent goes on the most informative point in the utterance.

### Broad focus vs. narrow focus
- **Broad focus**: the prominence pattern appropriate in a wide range of contexts (e.g., "five FRANCS")
- **Narrow focus**: the pattern appropriate in very few contexts (e.g., "FIVE francs")
- Ladd redefines "normal stress" as the prominence pattern that can convey broad focus, not requiring contextlessness (p. 223)

### Deaccenting
The removal of accent from a word that would normally bear it, typically because the word refers to something already given or repeated in discourse. A key cross-linguistic difference: some languages (English, Dutch, German) readily deaccent given material, while others (Italian, Romanian, Spanish) strongly resist direct deaccenting and use word-order changes instead (pp. 231-236).

### Plastic vs. non-plastic prominence (Vallduvi 1991)
- **Plastic languages** (English, Dutch, German): can freely move the main accent to different positions within the sentence
- **Non-plastic languages** (Italian, Catalan, Romance languages generally): constrained to vary word order to achieve the same accentual effects; the accent itself stays in a relatively fixed position (pp. 235-236)

### Nuclear accent
The last (rightmost) accent in a phrase, which has special status in signaling focus. Its location -- not its phonetic prominence -- determines whether a broad or narrow focus interpretation is available (pp. 257-259).

### Unaccusative predicates
Intransitive predicates where the subject is theme-like (not agentive); these tend to have the main accent on the subject rather than the verb: "The COFFEE machine broke," "The SUN came out," "His MOTHER died" (p. 245).

### Left-headed vs. right-headed phonological phrases
Some languages (Hungarian) prefer prominence on the initial element of a phrase; others (English) on the final element. This may be related to word-level stress patterns (Hungarian has initial word stress) (pp. 243-244).

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Optimality Theory approach to sentence stress
Ladd suggests (p. 251) that cross-linguistic variation in sentence stress might be captured by Optimality Theory with competing constraints:
- A constraint favoring rightmost accent (accent of power)
- A constraint favoring accent on content words
- Constraints related to informativeness/highlighting
- Constraints related to question focus, phonological headedness, etc.
Different languages rank these constraints differently, producing different sentence stress patterns.

### Typological classification of sentence stress
Three general types of cross-linguistic differences identified (p. 223):
1. **Questions**: Different languages place the main accent differently in yes-no questions and WH-questions
2. **Deaccenting and semantic weight**: Languages differ in how given/repeated material affects accent
3. **Predicates vs. arguments**: Languages differ in relative accentability of verbs vs. nouns

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### YNQ intonation patterns
- **Russian YNQs**: High accent peak on the verb (L+H*_L), regardless of whether a noun follows. The question peak is aligned later than the statement peak (p. 228)
- **Many Eastern European languages**: Normal YNQ intonation is L*_H_L% -- the "most prominent" syllable is phonetically *low*, and followed by a peak-and-fall that is acoustically more salient (p. 228)
- **English YNQs**: Treated like statements for sentence stress (main accent on last content word)

### WHQ accent patterns -- two typological types (pp. 226-228)
1. Languages where WHQs follow the same sentence stress rules as statements (English, Germanic, Romance)
2. Languages where the WH-word bears the main accent (Romanian, Hungarian, Greek, Turkish, Bengali)

### Phonological analysis of Romanian vs. Italian WHQs (p. 229)
Example: "Where are you going?"
- Romanian: H* L L% on "Unde mergi?" -- nuclear accent on the WH-word *unde*
- Italian: H* H+L* L L% on "Dove vai?" -- nuclear accent on the verb *vai*, with H+L* pattern (same as broad focus statement)
- Despite phonetic similarity, the phonological structure differs: Romanian has one accent (on WH-word), Italian has two accents

### Broad focus pattern and the "last accent" (pp. 257-259)
- The last accent need not be phonetically most prominent
- The IPO tradition identifies prominence with pitch excursion size
- The *location* of the last accent determines broad vs. narrow focus interpretation
- Example: "a cup of COFFEE" -- accent on *coffee* is required for broad focus; accent elsewhere signals narrow focus

### Downstepped vs. non-downstepped prenuclear accents (p. 260)
Example with AM representation:
- (7a) H* L L% -- "my MOTHER'S diaries" [strong-weak, focus on *mother*]
- (7b) H* H* LL% -- "my mother's DIARIES" [weak-strong, non-downstepped]
- (7c) H* !H* LL% -- "my mother's DIARIES" [weak-strong, downstepped]
The difference between (7b) and (7c) is whether the second accent is downstepped relative to the first.

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

### Tonal analyses referenced
- Romanian WHQ: H* (on WH-word) followed by L and L%
- Italian broad focus statement: H+L* nuclear accent
- Russian YNQ: L+H*_L (high peak aligned later than in statements)
- Eastern European YNQ: L*_H_L% (low nuclear accent with following rise-fall)

### Emphasis as paralinguistic modification (pp. 255-257)
- Two basic sentence stress patterns: accent on *five* vs. accent on *francs*
- Emphasis (increased pitch range, intensity) is a paralinguistic device that can be applied to either pattern
- Emphasis helps disambiguate broad vs. narrow focus when applied to the accented word
- Emphasis is gradient, while accent location is categorical

## Alignment and Timing

### Question peak alignment in Russian (p. 228)
In Russian YNQs, the high accent peak (L+H*) is aligned later than the statement peak. The question peak appears on the "most prominent syllable" which may be identified by the high peak rather than by acoustic salience per se.

### Sentence length effects on accent patterns (pp. 228-229, 248)
- In languages with WH-word accent (Romanian, Greek), longer sentences may have additional accents later in the sentence
- In short intransitive sentences, unaccusative predicates favor subject accent, but adding adverbial material can shift accent to the predicate region (p. 248)

## Cross-linguistic Data

### Language typology for YNQ sentence stress (p. 225)
- **Languages like English** (accent on last content word in both statements and YNQs): all Germanic languages, Romance languages (except Romanian and possibly Southern Italian)
- **Languages like Russian** (accent on verb in YNQs regardless of noun): Romanian, Hungarian, Greek, most/all Slavic languages

### Language typology for WHQ accent (pp. 226-228)
- **WH-word accented**: Languages without WH-movement where WH-word is normally in noun-phrase position (Turkish, Bengali), and some with WH-movement (Romanian, Hungarian, Greek)
- **WH-word not specially accented**: English and most Germanic/Romance languages

### Deaccenting typology (pp. 232-236)
- **Deaccenting languages**: English, Dutch, German -- readily deaccent given/repeated material
- **Non-deaccenting languages**: Italian, Romanian, Spanish -- resist deaccenting; use word order (right-dislocation, clefting) to achieve similar effects
- Within English: Hawaiian Pidgin and Indian/Caribbean English reportedly lack deaccenting

### Predicate-argument accentability (pp. 244-251)
- **English, Dutch, German**: Arguments (nouns) generally more accentable than predicates (verbs); the difference between nouns and verbs in sentence stress is a key feature
- **Italian, Spanish**: Put the main accent on the rightmost content word regardless; Italian phrases like *cose da fare* ('things to do') can only be accented on the verb
- **Turkish** (SOV): Last accent on the object, not the verb (p. 250)
- **Bengali** (SOV): Disputed -- possibly accent on verb (Hayes and Lahiri 1991) vs. accent on object (Kim 1988)

### Hungarian phrase-level accent (pp. 242-243)
- Hungarian is "left-headed": main accent tends to fall on the first element of a phrase
- Noun phrases: first-element prominence (*nehez nyelv* 'difficult language')
- Currency phrases: SZAZ otven forintot ('150 forints') -- accent on the number, not the unit (opposite of English *fifty CENTS*)
- Word stress is invariably on the first syllable

### Icelandic deaccenting (p. 242)
- Like English in having deaccentable nouns (*gamli* 'old' + man)
- But definiteness (not indefiniteness) triggers deaccenting: *GAMLI madurinn* 'the old man' vs. *gamall MADUR* 'an old man'

## Implementation Notes

### Accent placement rules for TTS (key findings from this chapter)

1. **Default accent placement**: For English TTS, the main accent (nuclear accent) should default to the last content word in the phrase/sentence. This is the "broad focus" pattern.

2. **Deaccenting rules for English**:
   - Words that are repeated or given in context should be deaccented
   - Indefinite pronouns (*something*, *someone*, *anything*) are typically deaccented; accent shifts to the verb: "They've DISCOVERED something" (not "discovered SOMETHING")
   - Semantically "empty" nouns (*man*, *guy*, *person*, *thing*, *stuff*) tend to be deaccented: "He was arrested because he KILLED a man" (vs. "he killed a POLICEMAN")
   - Sentence-final adverbs and prepositional phrases denoting place/time often deaccented: "I saw an ACCIDENT today", "There's a FLY in my soup"

3. **Predicate vs. argument accentability**:
   - In transitive sentences: accent the last argument (object noun), not the verb
   - In intransitive sentences: more complex -- unaccusative verbs (appear, break, die, come out) favor subject accent; unergative verbs (wrestle, weep, swear) favor verb accent
   - Generic/definitional sentences favor verb accent: "Wood FLOATS", "Penguins SWIM"
   - Topicalized/given subjects favor verb accent: "Truman DIED" (if Truman is in the news)

4. **Adjective+Noun phrases**:
   - Default: accent on the noun ("a silky SHEEN")
   - But with semantically vague nouns: accent on the adjective ("STATISTICAL approaches", "SIMILAR case")
   - Thoroughfare names: accent on first part if second part is *Street* ("WELLINGTON Street") but on second part otherwise ("Chesley DRIVE")

5. **Nuclear accent = last accent**: For broad focus, the system must ensure there is an accent on the last content word. The number of prenuclear accents and their relative prominence are less critical -- what matters for focus interpretation is which word bears the *last* accent.

6. **Emphasis is gradient, accent is categorical**: A TTS system should treat accent placement (which word gets an accent) as a categorical decision, and emphasis (how much pitch range/intensity to use) as a separate, gradient parameter. These two should be independently controllable.

7. **Downstep in prenuclear accents**: When multiple H* accents precede the nuclear accent, they may be downstepped (!H*) producing a declination-like pattern, or non-downstepped (flat H* sequence). The choice affects perceived relative prominence but not focus interpretation (p. 260).
