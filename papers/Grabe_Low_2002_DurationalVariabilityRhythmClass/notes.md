# Durational Variability in Speech and the Rhythm Class Hypothesis

## One-Sentence Summary
Introduces and applies the Pairwise Variability Index (PVI, both raw rPVI and normalized nPVI) to vowel and intervocalic (consonantal) interval durations across 18 languages, testing the traditional stress-timed/syllable-timed/mora-timed rhythm-class hypothesis and showing that durational-variability measures broadly separate stress-timed from syllable-timed languages while previously unclassified languages fall on a continuum between the two rather than forming a distinct class. *(p.2, abstract)*

## Problem Addressed
The traditional rhythm-class hypothesis (Pike 1946, Abercrombie 1967) claims languages are categorically stress-timed, syllable-timed, or mora-timed based on isochronous recurrence of stress-pulses, syllables, or morae respectively. Decades of interstress-interval and syllable-duration measurements failed to find empirical support for isochrony (Roach 1982, Dauer 1983, Beckman 1982). The paper asks: can acoustic-phonetic *durational variability* (not phonological units like interstress intervals) still support a classification of languages into rhythm classes, and where do languages with no traditional rhythm-class label fall along such a measure? *(p.2-11)*

## Key Contributions
- Extends Low, Grabe & Nolan's (2000) Pairwise Variability Index method from a two-variety (British/Singapore English) comparison to 18 languages, including 7 with no prior rhythmic classification (Estonian, Greek, Luxembourgish, Malay, Mandarin, Rumanian, Welsh). *(p.11-13)*
- Introduces the raw (unnormalized) PVI (rPVI, equation 1) alongside the previously-used normalized PVI (nPVI, equation 2), and applies rPVI to intervocalic (consonantal) intervals while continuing to apply nPVI to vocalic intervals. *(p.7-8, p.18)*
- Empirically tests and justifies the normalization decision: vocalic rPVI correlates strongly with speaking rate across speakers (should be normalized), intervocalic rPVI's correlation with rate is confounded with cross-language syllable-structure complexity (left un-normalized). *(p.17-18)*
- Provides the full 18-language target-value table (vocalic nPVI and intervocalic rPVI, Appendix Table 1) and an explicit discussion (Section 4.2) of why vowel reduction, not overall vowel-time proportion, is what drives foot-level near-isochrony and the stress-timed/syllable-timed rhythmic impression.

## Methodology
- **Corpus:** "The North Wind and the Sun," a standard IPA phonetic-research text, read once at the speaker's own pace by ONE speaker per language, 18 languages total (not 20 speakers per language as in Low et al. 2000 - this is a single-speaker-per-language cross-language survey). *(p.11)*
- Recordings made in sound-treated booths (Oxford Phonetics Lab: British English, French, German, Greek, Polish, Rumanian, Welsh; Cambridge Phonetics Lab: Dutch, Estonian, Japanese, Luxembourgish, Thai) or quiet rooms (UCL: Catalan; National Institute of Education Singapore: Tamil, Malay, Singapore English, Mandarin-as-spoken-in-Singapore); Spanish data supplied by Anders Eriksson, University of Stockholm. *(p.14)*
- Vocalic intervals: stretch of signal between vowel onset and vowel offset (characterized by vowel formants), regardless of how many vowels the section spans (monophthong, diphthong, or a vowel sequence spanning a word boundary). Intervocalic intervals: stretch of signal between vowel offset and vowel onset, regardless of the number of consonants included. Measured left-to-right on wide-band spectrograms via xwaves. *(p.14)*
- Segmentation criteria: fricative-vowel onset = onset of F2; vowel-voiceless-fricative offset = onset of the noise pattern; vowel-voiced-fricative offset = onset of high-frequency energy; nasal-vowel boundaries by formant transition faults; glides included in the vocalic portion unless clear formant/amplitude discontinuities mark them as separate. *(p.14-15)*
- Pauses between intonation phrases and hesitations excluded from analysis, but (unlike Low, Grabe & Nolan 2000 and the authors' own earlier work) intervals spanning an intonation-phrase-internal juncture are NOT excluded/split at that boundary in this study, and phrase-final syllables ARE included (not excluded as in earlier British/Singapore English work) - three stated reasons: (1) avoiding subjective/intuitive judgment calls about intonation-phrase-boundary placement, especially in languages the authors don't speak; (2) no cross-language data on relative phrase-final lengthening degree to justify differential exclusion; (3) the number of pause-adjacent intervals is small enough not to greatly affect results. *(p.15-16)*
- Vowel devoicing (e.g., English, Japanese) handled acoustically, not phonologically: a vowel's duration is measured only where the acoustic signal shows evidence of a *voiced* vowel (formant pattern present); devoiced vowels are not measured as vowels. *(p.16)*
- Normalization decision (empirically tested, not assumed): Pearson correlation between average vocalic/intervocalic interval duration (speaking rate proxy) and rPVI, tested both within British English across 20 speakers (from Low et al. 2000 data) and across the 18-language one-speaker-each dataset of the present study. Vocalic rPVI correlates strongly and significantly with speaking rate in both datasets (British English: r=0.867, p<0.01; 18-language set: r=0.613, p<0.01) → vocalic intervals ARE normalized (nPVI used). Intervocalic rPVI also correlates with rate (British English: r=0.801, p<0.01; 18-language set: r=0.808, p<0.01), but the authors argue this correlation is confounded with genuine cross-language differences in syllable-structure complexity (e.g., Polish intervocalic units can hold 1-5 segments vs. Japanese's 1-2), not purely a speech-rate artifact, and disentangling the two is judged beyond the paper's scope → intervocalic intervals are NOT normalized (raw rPVI used, not nPVI). *(p.17-18)*

## Key Equations / Statistical Models

$$
rPVI = \left[\sum_{k=1}^{m-1} |d_k - d_{k+1}| \right] / (m-1)
$$
Where: $m$ = number of vocalic or intervocalic intervals in the text; $d_k$ = duration of the $k$-th interval. This is the RAW Pairwise Variability Index - not normalized for speech rate. **Extraction note:** the equation's rendering in the source .doc-to-PDF conversion is corrupted (an embedded OLE equation object failed to convert and appears as a placeholder glyph in the PDF); the formula above is reconstructed from the paper's own prose description ("equation (2) differs only in omitting the third step [i.e., dividing by the mean of the pair]" and "the differences are then summed and divided by the number of differences, multiplied by 100 for nPVI only") and cross-checked against the identical rPVIc formula in Ferragne & Pellegrino (2004), which explicitly cites this paper as its formula source. *(p.8)*

$$
nPVI = 100 \times \left[\sum_{k=1}^{m-1} \left| \frac{d_k - d_{k+1}}{(d_k + d_{k+1})/2} \right| \right] / (m-1)
$$
Where: $m$ = number of items (vocalic intervals) in an utterance; $d_k$ = duration of the $k$-th item. Computed by taking the absolute difference in duration between each pair of successive measurements, dividing by the mean duration of the pair, summing over all pairs, dividing by the number of differences, and multiplying by 100 (since the normalization produces fractional values). *(p.8)*

## Design Rationale
- Chose NOT to measure interstress intervals or syllable durations (phonological units whose phonetic correlates are disputed); instead measured directly from the acoustic signal (vocalic and intervocalic interval durations), following Low, Grabe & Nolan (2000)'s "direct route from impressionistic observations... to the acoustic signal." *(p.7)*
- Departed explicitly from the isochrony research program: rather than testing for near-equal interval durations (isochrony), the paper measures *durational variability* between successive intervals directly, sidestepping the isochrony debate entirely. *(p.7)*
- Justifies choosing PVI over Ramus, Nespor & Mehler's (1999) %V/ΔC/ΔV measures: PVI captures the *sequential* pairwise pattern of durations rather than a global standard deviation, which conflates genuinely different durational patterns that happen to share the same variance (worked example on p.10: a language alternating three long vowels then three short vowels has the same standard deviation as one that strictly alternates long/short vowels pair by pair, despite very different rhythmic character) - SD is rate-of-change-blind, PVI is not. *(p.10)*
- Chose to include phrase-final syllables and not split multi-phrase text into per-phrase measurements (departing from their own earlier practice), specifically to avoid injecting undocumented/unverifiable subjective judgments about intonation-phrase-boundary placement into languages the authors do not speak natively. *(p.15-16)*

## Arguments Against Prior Work
- Against Pike (1946)/Abercrombie (1965/1967): their physiological chest-pulse/stress-pulse theory of categorical stress- vs. syllable-timing was empirically disproven (citing Ladefoged 1967), removing the physiological basis for a strictly categorical rhythm-class distinction. *(p.5)*
- Against decades of interstress-interval/syllable-duration studies (Roach 1982; Dauer 1983 comparing English/Thai/Spanish/Italian/Greek): found interstress intervals no more regular in stress-timed English than syllable-timed Spanish, and syllable-timed languages in Roach's sample showed *greater* syllable-duration variability than stress-timed languages - the opposite of the classical prediction - leading Dauer to conclude the search for stress-/syllable-timing acoustic correlates was "futile." The present paper's response is to measure differently (acoustic durational variability via PVI) rather than abandon the classification question. *(p.4)*
- Against Ramus, Nespor & Mehler (1999): their %V/ΔC/ΔV standard-deviation-based measures are argued (via a constructed worked example, p.10) to be blind to the *sequential/pairwise* pattern of duration alternation, which PVI captures and SD does not - two very differently-patterned duration sequences can share identical standard deviation. *(p.10)*
- Against Dauer's (1983/1987) continuous stress-based reanalysis and Nespor's (1990) view: the paper does not resolve this debate but positions its own approach (durational variability via PVI, without assuming any particular phonological primitive) as an empirical middle path that lets the data show whether "unclassified" languages pattern discretely or continuously. *(p.6, p.11)*

## Results Summary (Figures 1-2, approximate visual read-offs; exact values in Table 1 below)
- **Figure 1 (p.20), prototypical languages:** Plots vocalic nPVI (y) against intervocalic rPVI (x) for British English, Dutch, German (stress-timed, open circles), French, Spanish (syllable-timed, filled circles), and Japanese (mora-timed, filled square). Stress-timed languages cluster at high vocalic nPVI (~57-66); syllable-timed languages at low vocalic nPVI (~30-43); Japanese's vocalic nPVI (~41) is close to French's, supporting mora-timing patterning with syllable-timing on the vocalic dimension. Approximate read-off values from Figure 1: Dutch vocalic nPVI ~65, intervocalic rPVI ~55; German ~59/~55; British English ~57/~63; French ~43/~50; Japanese ~41/~62; Spanish ~30/~57. *(p.20)*
- Stress-timed vs syllable-timed separation on vocalic nPVI supports the classical Pike/Abercrombie classification, but NOT via isochrony - via durational variability instead. There is no support for a strict categorical split; languages instead appear "more or less" stress-timed or syllable-timed (continuous, not discrete). *(p.20-21)*
- Intervocalic rPVI predictions partly supported: French (simple syllable structure) has lower intervocalic rPVI than English/Dutch/German (complex syllable structure) as predicted, but Spanish (syllable-timed) unexpectedly has an intervocalic rPVI similar to Dutch/German rather than lower - contrary to prediction; authors call for more speakers to validate rPVI's role in capturing rhythmic patterning. *(p.21)*
- Japanese (mora-timed) confirmed to pattern with the syllable-timed group on vocalic nPVI (no vowel reduction, like French/Spanish), but has a HIGH intervocalic rPVI (in the stress-timed language range) - counterintuitive given Japanese's simple syllable structure. Explanation: potentially-devoiced vowels (16% of vowels in the Japanese "North Wind" passage) were counted as part of the intervocalic (not vocalic) interval since devoiced vowels show no formant pattern acoustically, which artificially inflates Japanese's intervocalic rPVI - a direct consequence of the paper's acoustic (not phonological) definition of vocalic/intervocalic intervals. *(p.19, p.21-22)*
- **Figure 2 (p.22), all 18 languages:** Full-corpus scatter plot (exact Table 1 values in Appendix, not yet extracted). Approximate visual read-offs: Thai patterns with the stress-timed group (vocalic nPVI ~66, near Dutch); Polish has vocalic nPVI similar to French (~47, syllable-timed range) but the HIGHEST intervocalic rPVI in the entire 18-language set (~80) - confirming Nespor's (1990) "mixed" characterization (no vowel reduction but complex syllable structure) with acoustic data for the first time. Catalan separates from Spanish on vocalic nPVI (Catalan ~44 vs Spanish ~30) despite similar syllable structure, attributed to Catalan HAVING vowel reduction unlike Spanish - directly linking vowel reduction to vocalic nPVI level. Singapore English (SE) patterns close to British English but with slightly LOWER vocalic variability, and is clearly NOT close to French/Spanish despite its "syllable-timed" label in prior literature (Tongue 1979, Platt & Weber 1980, Yeow 1987). Luxembourgish and Mandarin pattern with the syllable-timed group (Mandarin has the lowest vocalic nPVI of all 18 languages). Welsh, Greek, Malay, Tamil, Rumanian (previously unclassified) overlap the boundary between stress-timed and syllable-timed groups rather than forming a separate class. Estonian has the lowest intervocalic rPVI of the whole set (opposite of Polish). Tamil unexpectedly shows HIGH vocalic nPVI and HIGH intervocalic rPVI, contradicting its traditional syllable-timed classification (Corder 1973, Asher 1985). *(p.22-24)*
- The Singapore-English-vs-British-English and Catalan-vs-Spanish comparisons are both explicitly attributed by the authors to differences in *how* vowel reduction happens (not just whether it happens): citing Low et al. (2000)'s finding that Singapore English HAS phonological vowel reduction but its reduced vowels are less centralized in F1/F2 space AND longer in duration than British English's reduced vowels - i.e., a "weaker"/less extreme form of vowel reduction produces a smaller vocalic-nPVI difference from a no-reduction language than "full" reduction does. *(p.23)*
- **Speaker-stability check (p.24):** Because the 18-language dataset has only one speaker per language, the authors split each speaker's data into 3 equal subsections and tested cross-section correlation as a stability/reliability check. All correlations highly significant (vocalic: sections 1-2 r=0.926, 1-3 r=0.844, 2-3 r=0.788, all p<0.01) - PVI values are stable/consistent within a single speaker's passage, supporting the reliability of single-speaker-per-language comparisons despite the small per-language sample.

## Discussion: Rhythm Classes - Categorical or Gradient? (Section 4.1, p.28-30)
- On the vocalic axis alone, the three prototypical stress-timed languages (German, English, Dutch) are cleanly separated from the two prototypical syllable-timed languages (French, Spanish) - superficially supporting a categorical distinction. *(p.28)*
- But because languages with intermediate values are not significantly different from EITHER prototypical group (Table 2), and Thai/Tamil switch groups under a different measure (%V/ΔC vs PVI), a strict categorical distinction cannot be defended; the paper concludes "languages can be more or less stress-timed or syllable-timed." *(p.28-29)*
- The paper explicitly reaches for an analogy with categorical PERCEPTION research (Samuel 1977; Carney, Widen & Viehmeister 1977; Harnad 1987): speech-perception continua that are perceived categorically by naive listeners nonetheless show non-categorical/non-discrete underlying discrimination once tested carefully - a "weak categorical" effect. The authors propose their stress-timed/syllable-timed data show an analogous "weak categorical" distinction: the two labeled endpoint-groups differ robustly, but the underlying variable is continuous/gradient, not a true discrete category. *(p.29)*
- Three converging pieces of evidence for gradience, not strict categoricality: (1) overlap between the stress-timed group, the syllable-timed group, and the "unclassified" languages, with Japanese specifically shown NOT to occupy a rhythm class of its own but instead pattern with the syllable-timed group on the vocalic axis; (2) the vocalic nPVI separates languages into a stress-timed/syllable-timed pattern but the intervocalic rPVI does NOT - instead intervocalic rPVI's main job is showing why Polish (very high) and Estonian (very low) don't fit either prototypical class; (3) languages with an extreme value on ONE dimension (vocalic or intervocalic) tend to have a non-extreme (mid-range) value on the OTHER dimension, producing the "distinct diamond shape" of the full 18-language scatter plot (Figure 2) rather than points scattered into every corner - interpreted as consistent with a definition of rhythm as "recurrence of similar acoustic units at relatively regular intervals": if both vocalic and intervocalic intervals were simultaneously and independently extremely variable, there would be no recurring similar unit and hence no rhythmic impression at all. *(p.29-30)*

## Discussion: The Vocalic PVI, %V, and Vowel Reduction as the Driver of Rhythm Class (Section 4.2, p.30-31)
This section is the paper's most direct treatment of vowel reduction as a rhythm-class mechanism, and it is nuanced rather than a simple "vowel reduction causes stress-timing" claim:
- The vocalic nPVI and the %V (percent vowel time) measures largely AGREE at the extremes (Estonian, Polish, Mandarin, British English occupy similar relative positions in both spaces) but DISAGREE in the middle of the space. *(p.30)*
- Direct counter-example to a naive "more vowel reduction/lower %V implies more stress-timed" story: Greek, Catalan, Welsh, Luxembourgish, and Japanese all have LOWER %V than German (which would suggest, under a %V-only account, that they should be MORE stress-timed than German) - but their vocalic nPVI values are actually LOWER than German's, meaning the nPVI classifies them as LESS stress-timed than German. The two measures directly contradict each other for these languages. *(p.30)* **This is the key evidence that %V alone is not an adequate proxy for "how much vowel reduction/durational variability" a language has - the pattern of successive-vowel duration alternation (what nPVI captures) is doing separate work from the raw proportion of time occupied by vowels.**
- Thai and Tamil (both high vocalic nPVI, both high %V) "switch" from appearing stress-timed (under nPVI) to appearing syllable-timed (under %V/ΔC) depending which measure is used - i.e., a language can have a high proportion of vowel-heavy syllables (high %V, associated with simple, vowel-rich syllable structure) while ALSO having high vowel-to-vowel durational variability (high nPVI) - these are not mutually exclusive, contra the assumption embedded in choosing %V as a single summary rhythm statistic. Note this pattern does NOT hold for English (high nPVI, low %V) or Spanish (low nPVI, high %V), i.e. there is no consistent complementary relationship between %V and nPVI across all languages - only for some. *(p.28, p.30-31)*
- The English/German-vs-French/Spanish contrast is explicitly attributed to a *complementary* relationship between overall vowel-time proportion and vocalic-interval variability specific to those particular language pairs: English/German have LOW %V (little overall vowel time, because reduced vowels are short) but HIGH vocalic nPVI (because the same language ALSO has full/long vowels, creating high alternation-variability between long and short vowels); French/Spanish have HIGH %V (more overall vowel time, no reduction shortening vowels) but LOW vocalic nPVI (little alternation because there is no separate short/reduced-vowel category to alternate against). The authors suggest this complementarity "may contribute substantially to impressions of stress- or syllable-timing" for languages that do show it. *(p.30-31)*
- **Endnote 1 (p.33), directly on the vowel-reduction mechanism**: citing Wenk & Wioland (1982) and Brakel (1985), the authors note these researchers "suggested that the basis for stress- or syllable-timing may rest on vowels rather than syllables. In British English, there is the presence of vowel reduction and the effect of this is that the duration of each foot is nearly isochronous. Syllable-timed languages like French do not have reduced vowels and as such, do not seem to achieve foot isochrony." This is the clearest explicit statement in the paper connecting vowel reduction (as a phonological process) to the *foot*-level near-isochrony effect historically claimed for stress-timed languages - i.e., in this account vowel reduction is not merely a durational side-effect but the phonological mechanism that lets stressed-foot durations partially compensate/equalize despite varying numbers of intervening unstressed syllables.
- Vowel-quality-based reduction differences also drive cross-language contrasts even among languages with SOME reduction: citing Low et al. (2000)'s finding (also discussed on p.23) that Singapore English HAS phonological vowel reduction, but its reduced vowels are (a) less spectrally centralized in the F1/F2 vowel space and (b) LONGER in duration than British English's reduced vowels - "weaker"/less extreme reduction of this kind produces a smaller vocalic-nPVI gap from a no-reduction language (French) than "full" (British-English-style) reduction does; Catalan (has vowel reduction) vs Spanish (does not) shows an analogous, if smaller, split on the vocalic nPVI axis despite near-identical syllable structure. *(p.23, p.30)*

## Design Rationale (continued)
- Chose the normalized nPVI for vocalic intervals but the RAW (unnormalized) rPVI for intervocalic intervals, based on an explicit empirical test (Pearson correlation of rPVI with average interval duration/speaking rate) rather than a priori assumption - see Methodology section above for the numeric results (r=0.867/0.613 vocalic; r=0.801/0.808 intervocalic) and the argument that intervocalic-rPVI's rate-correlation is confounded with genuine cross-language syllable-structure-complexity differences rather than being a pure speech-rate artifact. A supporting citation (endnote 6, Gay 1978) notes that increases in English speaking rate shorten both vocalic and consonantal portions of syllables, but MOST of the change comes from vocalic-portion shortening - reinforcing that vocalic and intervocalic intervals are not symmetric with respect to rate effects, justifying treating them with different normalization procedures. *(p.17-18, p.34)*
- Chose Deterding's (1994) local-pair normalization procedure (dividing by the mean of each successive pair, i.e. the nPVI formula) over a global normalization (dividing by the whole-utterance average interval duration), specifically because local normalization is more sensitive to local speech-rate changes - argued to matter especially for languages like French whose rhythmic structure is "crudely, very similar intervals within rhythmic groups, with considerable phrase-final lengthening at the end of each group" (endnote 5, citing Post 2000 for a prosodic analysis of French intonation). *(p.34)*

## Testable Properties (continued)
- The vocalic nPVI reliably separates the prototypical stress-timed group (German, Dutch, English: nPVI ≈57-66) from the prototypical syllable-timed group (French, Spanish: nPVI ≈30-44), a gap of roughly 15-20 nPVI points with intermediate/unclassified languages filling the space between - a duration model choosing a target vocalic-nPVI value for a given accent should place "more stress-timed-feeling" accents in the 55-70 range and "more syllable-timed-feeling" ones in the 27-45 range, with intermediate values (46-54) sounding neither strongly stress- nor syllable-timed. *(p.35, Table 1)*
- Intervocalic rPVI does NOT reliably track the stress-timed/syllable-timed distinction (German/Dutch/English intervocalic rPVI ≈55-64 overlaps heavily with French/Spanish's ≈50-58); a duration model should NOT expect manipulating consonant-cluster/intervocalic timing alone to shift perceived rhythm class - this echoes Ferragne & Pellegrino (2004)'s finding for English dialects specifically. *(p.35, Table 1; cf. [[Ferragne_Pellegrino_2004_RhythmReadBritishEnglish]])*
- A language/accent with vowel reduction should show foot-level near-isochrony as an emergent effect of reduction, not as an independently-implemented timing rule - i.e., a duration policy that correctly reduces unstressed vowel durations for a stress-timed accent should get approximate foot-isochrony "for free" rather than needing a separate isochrony-enforcement mechanism. *(p.33, endnote 1)*
- %V (proportion of utterance time occupied by vowels) is NOT a safe proxy for vocalic nPVI (durational alternation variability) - a duration model should not assume that lowering the overall fraction of vowel-time in an accent's output (e.g., by shortening vowels globally) will by itself produce a higher-nPVI, more "stress-timed"-sounding rhythm; the two properties can move independently or even in opposite directions (Greek/Catalan/Welsh/Luxembourg/Japanese vs German). *(p.30)*
- Pause-adjacent intervals should be handled consistently: this paper deliberately did NOT exclude intonation-phrase-final syllables (unlike the authors' own earlier British/Singapore-English-specific work), because it lacked cross-language data on relative phrase-final-lengthening degree - a duration model with per-accent phrase-final lengthening rules should be aware that omitting or including phrase-final syllables in a rhythm-metric evaluation is a modeling choice with real, language-specific consequences, not a neutral default. *(p.15-16)*

## Relevance to Project
This paper is the direct methodological source (via Low, Grabe & Nolan 2000) for the nPVI/rPVI formulas already used by [[Ferragne_Pellegrino_2004_RhythmReadBritishEnglish]] and is the primary place to look for how a per-accent duration policy should actually differ across accents at the rhythm-class level. Concretely it offers: (1) the exact rPVI/nPVI equations plus a worked justification for WHY vocalic intervals should be rate-normalized while intervocalic/consonantal intervals should not, directly informing how a synthesizer's internal duration-variability metric should be computed and normalized per accent; (2) a large (18-language) reference table of target vocalic-nPVI/intervocalic-rPVI value ranges (Table 1) that a duration model could use to place a given accent's "rhythm class" on a continuous scale rather than a binary switch; (3) an explicit mechanistic account (endnote 1, Wenk & Wioland 1982; Brakel 1985) that vowel reduction is what produces near-isochronous foot durations in stress-timed varieties, meaning a correctly-implemented per-accent vowel-reduction rule (shortening AND spectrally reducing unstressed vowels, per Low et al. 2000's finding that BOTH duration and quality of reduction matter) should organically produce the desired rhythmic "feel" without a separate explicit isochrony mechanism; (4) a clear warning against using overall vowel-time proportion (%V) as a shortcut proxy for rhythm class, since it can diverge sharply from the pairwise-variability behavior that actually distinguishes accents perceptually; (5) explicit acknowledgment that pause-adjacent/phrase-final intervals are a separate durational phenomenon (final lengthening) that must be handled as its own rule rather than folded into steady-state rhythm-class variability computations - directly parallel to Ferragne & Pellegrino (2004)'s finding.

## Open Questions
- [ ] The paper explicitly calls for "future research using more speakers" to validate the intervocalic rPVI's role in rhythm classification, since Spanish's unexpectedly high intervocalic rPVI (similar to Dutch/German rather than lower as predicted) is based on only one speaker per language. *(p.21)*
- [ ] The paper does not fully explain WHY %V and vocalic nPVI diverge for Greek/Catalan/Welsh/Luxembourgish/Japanese versus German - it documents the divergence and its implications but leaves the underlying phonological cause (why these particular languages decouple the two measures) as an open question.
- [ ] The paper explicitly notes the Japanese intervocalic rPVI value is likely inflated by counting devoiced vowels as part of the intervocalic interval (an artifact of the acoustic, not phonological, interval-definition methodology) - the true (phonologically-defined) Japanese intervocalic rPVI is left unresolved. *(p.19, p.21-22)*
