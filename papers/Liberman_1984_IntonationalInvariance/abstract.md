# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

*This is a book chapter (Chapter 10 of* Language Sound Structure*, MIT Press 1984) and carries no printed abstract. The following is the authors' own statement of the work, quoted verbatim from the introduction (p.157) and conclusion (p.232).*

From section 1, Introduction (p.157):

> "The central question of phonological theory is the nature of phonological substance (primitive entities and relations) and of its connections with words and sounds. Taking the realization of abstract intonational categories as a case in point, we will argue that the links between phonological substance and sound are more complex and more consequential than usually assumed. An appreciation of this point will bring better linguistic descriptions in terms of simpler linguistic theories; or so we hope.
>
> The work reported here arises from our interest in the interaction of four factors in the description of English intonation: tune, prominence, declination, and pitch range."

From section 8, Conclusion (p.232):

> "Its descriptive particulars concern the phonetic parameters underlying English intonation and their role in relating abstract intonational categories to observed F0 contours. We have proposed to reinterpret F0 measurements in terms of a fixed 'baseline,' a 'reference line' that increases with pitch range, and a lowering effect specific to the domain of (certain) final pitch accents; this approach yields F0 implementation rules that do not require whole-phrase preplanning.
>
> Our theoretical proposals concern the general status of phonetic interpretation of phonological categories. We have suggested that nontrivial, (partly) language-particular phonetic interpretation should take over the function of most postlexical phonological rules."

---

## Our Interpretation

The paper asks how English F0 contours stay recognizably the same intonation pattern when a speaker changes overall pitch range or phrase length, and it answers with a scaling model rather than a description. Two production experiments, one varying pitch range across an answer-background sentence in both orders and one varying pitch range and list length across downstepping berry lists, establish that the bottom of a speaker's range is nearly invariant while peaks scale freely, that final accents are lowered by a constant fraction, and that downstep is a constant-ratio decay above a pitch-range-dependent reference line. This matters for speech synthesis because it yields a local, left-to-right F0 target algorithm with a handful of interpretable constants, and because it shows why the more familiar declining-baseline and global-declination-slope models predict the wrong terminal values.
