# Abstract

Ohala, J. J. (1984). An Ethological Perspective on Common Cross-Language Utilization of F0 of Voice. *Phonetica*, 41(1), 1-16.

## Verbatim Abstract

The author suggests that the following seemingly disparate phenomena have an underlying relationship: (a) cross-language similarities in the intonation contours for statements versus questions, (b) cross-cultural similarities in the vocal expression via intonation of attitude and affect, (c) cross-language patterns in the use of tone, vowels, and consonants in 'sound symbolic' vocabulary, (d) cross-species use of F0 in threatening or non-threatening vocalizations, (e) cross-cultural and cross-species use of certain facial expressions (involving distinct mouth shape), and (f) the existence of sexual dimorphism in the vocal anatomy of humans (and certain non-humans). He argues that all these arise due to an innately specified 'frequency code', which associates high acoustic frequency with the primary meaning of 'small vocalizer' and thus such secondary meanings as 'subordinate, submissive, non-threatening, desirous of the receiver's goodwill, etc.' and associates with low acoustic frequency the primary meaning of 'large vocalizer' and such secondary meanings as 'dominant, aggressive, threatening, etc.'

## Interpretation

This paper establishes the "frequency code" hypothesis -- a biologically grounded framework explaining why high F0 universally signals smallness/submission/questioning and low F0 signals largeness/dominance/assertion across languages, cultures, and even species. For a Klatt synthesizer, this provides the theoretical foundation for F0 range manipulation in prosody rules: questions should use higher F0 (not just rising contour), dominant/assertive speech should use lower F0 range, and speaker personality modeling can leverage the frequency code to convey authority vs. friendliness through base F0 and F0 range choices.
