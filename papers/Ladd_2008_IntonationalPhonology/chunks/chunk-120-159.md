## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Phrase Accent (pp.101-103)
- Borrowed from Bruce's (1977) analysis of Swedish. Pierrehumbert proposed that in English the last pitch accent of each phrase is followed by two distinct tonal events: a phrase accent and a boundary tone.
- **L phrase accent**: Low pitch following the final pitch accent; fall to low level takes place fairly soon after H* or any bitonal pitch accent, and pitch remains low until the final boundary tone.
- **H phrase accent**: High pitch following the last accent. The "calling contour" (stylised fall) is a well-localised example (H ... !H pattern).
- The extended span of low pitch after L phrase accent is described by Pierrehumbert (1980: ch. 5) as 'tone-spreading'.

### Boundary Tones (pp.103-104)
- **H%**: Always indicates a final rise, typically at the very end of the phrase/utterance. After L phrase accent it requires an 'upstep rule' to ensure the H% is higher than the immediately preceding H phrase accent. Identified with IPO's Type 2 Rise -- a 'non-prominence-lending' pitch movement at boundaries.
- **L%**: Not a final fall but merely the absence of final rise. After L phrase accent it indicates a gradual continued fall to the bottom of the range. After H phrase accent it indicates sustained level pitch. The sequence H_L% means 'sustained level phrase-final pitch'.
- Sustained level phrase-final pitch is transcribed as absence of boundary tone (0%) by some (Gussenhoven, Grabe), or by H_L% in Pierrehumbert's system. Arvaniti and Baltazani (2005) propose three boundary tones for Greek: H%, !H%, and L%.

### ToBI (Tones and Break Indices) (pp.104-107)
- Adapted from Pierrehumbert's analysis for standardized prosodic labelling of English speech databases (Silverman et al. 1992; Pitrelli, Beckman, and Hirschberg 1994).
- A complete ToBI transcription contains multiple 'tiers' -- strings of symbols anchored to specific time points in the waveform: orthographic transcription, tonal tier, break index tier, comments tier, alternative transcriptions tier.
- Two most important tiers: **tones** (F0 contour labels) and **break indices** (word boundary strength).
- Now officially referred to as **MAE_ToBI** (Mainstream American English ToBI).

### MAE_ToBI Inventory (p.105)
- **Five pitch accent types**: H*, L*, L+H*, L*+H, H+!H*
- **Two phrase accents** (edge tones): H- and L-
- **Two final boundary tones**: H% and L%
- **One initial boundary tone**: %H
- **Downstep diacritic**: /!/ applicable to any H tone (except H%) preceded by another H tone in the same phrase (i.e., !H*, L+!H*, L*+!H, and !H- are all possible but not as first accent in phrase)
- **Uncertainty markers**: X* for uncertain accent type; *? for uncertain accent presence

### Break Index Scale (pp.105-107)
- Labels every orthographic word boundary with a number 0 to 4:
  - **0**: Special closeness (e.g., *tch* in "Did you get your ticket?")
  - **1**: Normal expected disjuncture between words in sequence
  - **2**: Mismatch between tonal and other boundary cues; audible break without phrase boundary, or phrase accent without audible break
  - **3**: Intermediate phrase (ip) boundary
  - **4**: Intonation phrase (IP) boundary
- Break indices 3 and 4 take on specific meanings of ip and IP break respectively.
- Any well-formed transcription showing a phrase accent on a word must normally show a 3 break index between that word and the following word.

### Downstep in ToBI vs. Pierrehumbert (pp.105-106)
- Key modification from Pierrehumbert to ToBI: H*+L was eliminated as an accent type. What Pierrehumbert transcribed as H*+L is transcribed as H* in ToBI with the succeeding accent marked with the downstep diacritic /!/.
- The H+L* notation (downstepped accent stepping down from preceding unstressed syllable) was replaced by H+!H* -- making clear that the accented syllable level is not necessarily low in absolute terms, but only lower than what precedes, while still counting as 'High'.

### Nuclear vs. Prenuclear Accents (pp.131-134)
- British tradition divides intonation contour into: **prehead**, **head**, **nucleus**, and **tail**.
- Only the **nucleus** is obligatory (on monosyllabic utterances = entire contour). On longer utterances, nucleus occurs on the most prominent stressed syllable, normally the last stressed syllable.
- Pierrehumbert denied formal validity to the nuclear/prenuclear distinction, treating the nuclear accent as simply the last accent in the string.
- The special status of the nucleus is its 'primariness' -- it is the only obligatory pitch accent and signals pragmatically prominent words. This conception is made more plausible by the Beckman-Pierrehumbert revision where phrase accent is the edge tone for the intermediate phrase.
- ToBI training materials now explicitly define the nuclear accent as the last accent in the intermediate phrase, stated to be more salient than prenuclear accents.

### Identification of Tones (pp.134-138)
- Bruce's (1977) original concrete conception: tones = **turning points** in the F0 contour. Local maxima = H tone, local minima = L tone.
- Pierrehumbert abandons this: tones need not always correspond to turning points, and turning points need not always reflect tones.
- Example: the low turning point between two H* accents is not an L tone but a 'sagging transition'. The L in an H*+L accent triggers downstep but is never realised as a low target or turning point.
- The 'delayed peak' / 'scooped' contour: analysed as L*+H where peak is aligned late into the following unstressed syllable. The low accentual target L* is almost certainly present as a turning point aligned with the accented syllable.
- The 'sagging transition' between H* accents: pitch gradually sags after first H* then rises abruptly just before second H*. Ladd and Schepman (2003) showed the rise beginning is abrupt and simultaneous with the onset of the accented syllable, with F0 level consistent from context to context -- pointing to an actual L tonal target.

### Phonological Organisation of Tones (pp.138-140)
- The identification of tones vs. how they are organized into larger structures (pitch accents, tunes) are distinct questions.
- Pierrehumbert's theoretical constraint: pitch accents consist of at most two tones (one starred, one unstarred).
- Grice's analysis of Palermo Italian questions: proposes a pitch accent structure (PA) with a hierarchical tree of tones, accommodating three or even four tones in a single accent -- breaking with Pierrehumbert's flat, two-tone-maximum structure.
- Gussenhoven's 'tone linking': in sequences of accents, component tones can be moved or deleted. **Partial linking**: trailing tone of one accent reassociated as leading tone of next. **Complete linking**: trailing tone deleted entirely.

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### AM Theory Applied Cross-Linguistically (pp.107-113)
- AM theory provides a *framework of expectations* about what structures to find in any language's intonational system -- not a single 'correct' analysis.
- ToBI-style transcription systems now exist for ~12 languages: Dutch, German, Greek, Italian, Japanese, Korean, and others.
- The AM approach should not be equated with 'Pierrehumbert's analysis of English' -- within AM assumptions, there are different possible analyses of various phenomena.

### Taxonomy of Cross-Language Differences (pp.115-116)
Adapted from Wells (1982) for segmental phonetics, applied to intonation:
- **Semantic** differences: different meaning or use of the same tune
- **Systemic** differences: different inventory of phonologically distinct tune types
- **Realisational** differences: different phonetic detail in realising the same tune
- **Phonotactic** differences: different permitted tune-text association and structure of tunes

### The 'IPA Paradox' (p.113)
- Universally valid categories like [f] or [o] are difficult to define formally, yet theoretically rigorous definitions of language-specific categories precludes rigorous cross-language comparison.
- The same tension applies to intonation: using ToBI notation across languages enables comparison but risks masking real differences.

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### Phrase Accent Realisation (pp.101-103)
- **L phrase accent**: After the nuclear accent, F0 drops to low level and stays low for several syllables until boundary tone. The low stretch may be 'tone-spreading' or interpolation between two low targets.
- **H phrase accent**: Represents high pitch following the last pitch accent. In the sequences H*_H_H% and L*_H_H% there is no obvious phonetic basis for choosing where the H phrase accent 'occurs'.
- In the calling contour, the step down in pitch indicates the location of the (downstepped) H phrase accent.

### Boundary Tone Realisation (pp.103-104)
- **H%**: Final rise. After L phrase accent, requires upstep rule to ensure it exceeds the preceding H phrase accent level. After H phrase accent, the upstep rule ensures H% is higher than H phrase accent.
- **L%**: After L phrase accent = fall to bottom of range. After H phrase accent = sustained level pitch (H_L% = sustained level phrase-final pitch).

### Sagging Transition (pp.155-157)
- Between two H* accents (e.g., "Their mother's a lawyer"), pitch does not interpolate linearly. Instead it gradually sags/declines after first H*, then rises abruptly just before second H*.
- In H* ... L+H* sequence: smoothly dropping transition from first peak to fairly low level immediately before second peak.
- In H* ... H* sequence: 'sagging transition' that falls gradually then rises abruptly before second H*.
- Ladd and Schepman (2003, 2005) showed the beginning of the rise is abrupt and simultaneous with the accented syllable onset, with the F0 level of the rise onset consistent across contexts -- evidence for a tonal target (L tone).

### Calling Contour Patterns Across Languages (pp.116-119)
- Universal pattern: two sustained notes, stepping down from high to somewhat lower level, interval ~3 semitones.
- **English/German**: H ... !H sequence docked to the main lexically stressed syllable. If >1 syllable after nucleus, !H goes with the first postnuclear syllable.
- **Hungarian/French**: H ... !H sequence goes on the last two syllables, irrespective of accentual prominence.
- **Dutch**: First H is H*, associated with main accented syllable; subsequent postnuclear syllables can each carry a separate !H tone, yielding multiple downward steps.

### French/Italian Suspended-Fall Contour (pp.120-124)
- Phonetically: final syllable at fairly level mid-to-high pitch; immediately preceding syllable quite a bit higher; earlier syllables very low.
- Musical notation: three pitches, from low to raised-high to mid-high.
- AM analysis: L_H_!H* (preceded by low, rising to high, downstepped starred tone).
- Evidence from right-dislocated constituents in French: the dislocated constituent copies the last tone of the matrix sentence, supporting H+!H* analysis.
- Italian has a phonetically similar pattern with the same meaning, supporting the analysis since Italian stress is penultimate (providing direct docking site for the !H*).
- In Italian, the calling contour (LH* !H) is phonologically distinct from the suspended-fall (!H* !H) because the starred tone differs: H* in calling, !H* in suspended-fall.

### High Rising Terminal (HRT) / 'Uptalk' (pp.125-129)
- Statement contour used with superficially interrogative-sounding rising intonation, common in North America and Australia/New Zealand.
- AM analysis: H*_H_H% (high-rising nucleus).
- Pragmatically a linguistic choice (statement intonation can be either rising or falling); not simply 'question intonation applied to a statement'.
- Urban North British (UNB) English has a superficially similar rising statement contour but with key phonetic differences: rise on accented syllable followed by distinct fall, then gradually declining pitch to utterance end (rise-plateau-slump).
- UNB analysis: L*_H_L% -- a low or low-rising nuclear accent, high phrase accent, and low boundary tone. This is phonologically different from American/Antipodean HRT despite superficial similarity.

## Alignment and Timing

### Calling Contour Alignment (pp.117-118)
- The first H is a starred tone (H*), associated with the nucleus (main lexically stressed syllable).
- In English/German: if one syllable after nucleus, !H goes with it; if >1 syllable after nucleus, location of the step down is determined by metrical structure (Leben 1976; Haggo 1987). If the main stressed syllable is utterance-final, it is split into two parts to provide docking sites for both H and !H.
- In Hungarian/French: H ... !H sequence is placed on the last two syllables regardless of lexical stress.

### Delayed Peak Alignment (p.136)
- 'Scooped' or delayed peak contours: peak aligned considerably later, sometimes well into the following unstressed syllable.
- Ordinary fall: peak aligned early in the syllable.
- Both analysed as H*+L but differing in how the H tone is aligned with the accented syllable.
- Later revised to treat delayed peak as L*+H -- the low target L* is aligned with the accented syllable, followed by the high target.

### Sagging Transition Timing (pp.156-157)
- The beginning of the rise in all H*-to-H* contours (including those transcribed by ToBI labellers as L+H*) is abrupt and more or less simultaneous with the onset of the accented syllable.
- The F0 level of the beginning of the rise was extremely consistent from context to context and did not vary with the distance between the two accents.

## Cross-linguistic Data

### Languages with AM/ToBI Systems (p.108)
- Well-developed ToBI-style transcription systems for: Dutch, German, Greek, Italian, Japanese, Korean (and others).
- Additional AM work on: Bengali, Palermo Italian, French, Dyirbal, Spanish, Korean, Hungarian, Wolof, Bininj Gun-wok (Mayali), Chickasaw, Kuot, Papiamentu, Waima'a.

### Calling Contour Cross-Linguistically (pp.116-119)
- Present in many European languages (English, German, Dutch, Hungarian, French).
- ~3 semitone interval between the two levels appears universal.
- Systematic phonotactic differences across languages in how the two tones associate with text.
- English/German: accent-driven (H* on stressed syllable, !H on following).
- Hungarian/French: last two syllables regardless of stress.
- Dutch: accent-driven but with possibility of multiple !H steps on successive postnuclear syllables.

### French Suspended-Fall (pp.120-124)
- Dell (1984) represents as Low-High-Mid sequence.
- AM analysis: L_H+!H* (with the !H* being the nuclear accent).
- Right-dislocated constituents copy the last tone of the matrix sentence, providing evidence for the compositional tonal analysis.

### Italian Comparison (pp.123-124)
- Italian suspended-fall (same nuance as French): L_H_!H*
- Italian calling contour: L_H*_!H
- The distinction between calling and suspended-fall is clear in Italian because the starred tone differs (H* vs. !H*).

### HRT/UNB Comparison (pp.125-129)
- American/Antipodean HRT: H*_H_H% -- rising through to phrase end.
- UNB (Glasgow, Belfast, etc.): L*_H_L% -- rise on accented syllable, then gradual decline. Phonologically different contour type.
- Phonetic difference: American HRT keeps rising from accented syllable to end of phrase; UNB rise is on accented syllable, followed by distinct fall.
- Pragmatic difference: HRT is a choice (statements can be rising or falling); UNB rise is default statement intonation (not a choice).

## Implementation Notes

### For TTS F0 Generation

1. **Phrase accent implementation**: After the last pitch accent in a phrase:
   - L phrase accent: drop F0 to low level quickly after H* or bitonal accent; maintain low F0 until boundary tone. Duration of low stretch spans multiple syllables.
   - H phrase accent: maintain elevated F0 after last accent until boundary.

2. **Boundary tone implementation**:
   - H%: Execute a final rise at the very end of the phrase. After L phrase accent, upstep rule ensures H% > preceding H phrase accent level.
   - L%: After L phrase accent = gradual fall to speaker range bottom. After H phrase accent = sustained level pitch (no movement).

3. **Break index to prosodic boundary mapping**:
   - Break 3 (ip boundary) = intermediate phrase boundary, requires phrase accent edge tone.
   - Break 4 (IP boundary) = full intonation phrase boundary, requires both phrase accent and boundary tone.
   - Break 0-2 = no phonological boundary required; break 2 may still carry audible disjuncture.

4. **Downstep implementation**: ToBI uses the /!/ diacritic on H tones preceded by H. In synthesis, each !H should be scaled lower than the preceding H. The original Pierrehumbert system would trigger downstep from H*+L sequences; ToBI simplifies by marking downstep directly with /!/.

5. **Sagging transition**: Between two H* accents, do NOT use linear interpolation. Instead:
   - Pitch gradually sags/declines after first H*
   - Abrupt rise begins at the onset of the next accented syllable
   - The rise onset F0 level is consistent regardless of distance between accents
   - This pattern suggests an L target aligned with the accented syllable onset.

6. **Calling contour**: Implement as two level pitches:
   - H* on nucleus (main stressed syllable)
   - !H on first postnuclear syllable (or split final syllable if utterance-final)
   - Interval ~3 semitones between levels
   - Sustained (level, not falling) pitch on each tone

7. **HRT (uptalk)**: H*_H_H% -- pitch rises continuously from the accented syllable through to the phrase end. This is a statement contour choice, not a question marker.

8. **Tone-text association rules vary by language**: English/German associate tones with lexically stressed syllables; Hungarian/French associate with final syllables regardless of stress. For English TTS, tones should dock to metrically prominent syllables.

9. **Nuclear accent is the last accent in the intermediate phrase** and is more salient than prenuclear accents. The nucleus is the only obligatory accent -- in synthesis, every ip must have at least one pitch accent (the nuclear one).

10. **Tone linking** (Gussenhoven): When accents are close together, trailing tones may be reassociated (partial linking) or deleted (complete linking). This may need to be modeled in rapid speech or when accents are very close.
