# Abstract

## Original Text (Verbatim)

This chapter has no formal printed abstract (it is a book chapter, not a journal article). The paper's introduction serves the equivalent function:

"Intonation has been described as the most difficult aspect of a foreign language to acquire and is held responsible for numerous instances of miscommunication between native and non-native speakers... In this chapter, we provide evidence of the 'right' intonation produced by speakers of English from the British Isles. Our data include Southern British English or Received Pronunciation, the variety that is usually taken as a model by non-native speakers and data from two northern dialects spoken in Newcastle and in Belfast. The investigation was based on the IViE corpus... We concentrated on two aspects of intonation: the shape of pitch patterns and the location of accented syllables. We examined these in four utterance types frequently listed in textbooks: statements, wh-questions, polar (yes/no) questions and echo questions. For each dialect, we tabulated the frequency of particular intonation patterns and the location of accented syllables. Then we calculated intra- and inter-dialect variation scores. Variation scores for intonation were high... Variation scores for the location of accented syllables were substantially lower than for intonation, especially in nuclear position."

## Our Interpretation

This is a corpus-based quantitative study (not a controlled experiment) using the IViE sentence-reading data to test how much native-speaker intonational variation exists within and across Cambridge (Southern Standard), Newcastle, and Belfast English, and what that variation implies for non-native pronunciation teaching. Its central finding is an asymmetry: WHERE the nuclear pitch accent falls is almost perfectly consistent across speakers and dialects, while WHAT SHAPE that accent takes (fall, rise, rise-plateau, fall-rise, etc.) varies enormously both within a single dialect and especially between dialects. The paper argues, against standard EFL textbook practice, that the "standard" Southern British variety is not more internally uniform than regional dialects, and that pitch LEVEL (not tune shape) may be the more teachable and more reliable cue distinguishing questions from statements. Directly relevant to a rule-based intonation model as the most granular available source of full per-pattern frequency tables (not just dominant-pattern summaries) for Cambridge, Newcastle, and Belfast.
