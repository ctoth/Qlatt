## Key Concepts and Definitions

### Compound Prosodic Domains (CPD)
- The notion of Compound Prosodic Domains allows treating each emphatic accent as the primary accent of a **local** intermediate phrase, while simultaneously treating the entire sentence as a compound intermediate phrase (p. 302)
- This resolves the problem of focus interpretation in sentences like "The butcher charged me a thousand bucks!" where the epithet reading requires each content word to bear primary accent in its own local ip

### Compound Contours
- When the same abstract tune (e.g., the "surprise-redundancy" tune) can manifest either as a sequence L* ... H* LL% on a single phrase or as L* H ... !H* L L% across two constituent phrases of a compound phrase, the surface differences between the two tone sequences must be low-level differences conditioned by prosodic structure (p. 304)
- This demonstrates that tonal specifications are abstract properties of intermediate phrases (ips), and that the surface string of accents and edge tones is a predictable consequence of tune-text association

### Three Kinds of Tonal Target Scaling Factors
Ladd proposes three distinct categories of factors involved in the scaling of tonal targets (p. 306):

1. **Intrinsic** -- Different specifications relative to the tonal space (e.g., the difference between M tone and H tone)
2. **Extrinsic** -- Overall modifications of the level and span of the tonal space (e.g., raising the voice for paralinguistic emphasis)
3. **Metrical** -- Localisable, linguistically meaningful modifications of the tonal space (e.g., downstep) controlled by relations in prosodic structure

### Metrical Pitch Range Effects
- Downstep involves a **syntagmatic relation** of pitch level between two accents or other prosodic constituents (p. 305)
- Inspired by African tone language work (Clements 1983, Huang 1980), Ladd proposed that prosodic constituents can be related as either **high-low** (downstepped) or **low-high** (non-downstepped), analogous to how they are related as strong-weak in prominence (p. 305-306)
- The h-l and l-h relations between tones at the accent level can be extended to the phrase level to account for **nested downstep** -- downstep within phrases superimposed on an overall downward trend across phrases in an utterance (p. 307)

### Declination Reset and Paragraph Cues
- "Declination reset" (upward modification of pitch range at the beginning of a new stretch of declination) and "final lowering" (downward modification of pitch range at the end) are related to "paragraph cues" (Lehiste 1975; Silverman 1987) -- paragraphs or other larger chunks of text often begin quite high and end quite low (p. 308)
- These are related to discourse-level resettings of pitch range noted by Brown, Currie, and Kenworthy (1980), whereby speakers begin new topics high in their speaking range

## Formal Models and Frameworks

### Metrical Interpretation of Pitch Range
- The metrical model treats pitch range effects (downstep, Anna/Manny relations, upstep at phrase boundaries) as being **orthogonal to the tonal string** -- they are linguistic but not represented in the sequence of tones; rather, they are abstract relations between tones and between higher-level phonological constituents (p. 308)
- This allows pitch range relations to be **nested** like prominence relations: at the accent level (h-l between two accents) and at the phrase level (h-l between two phrases), as shown in diagram (33) on p. 307

### Relationship Between Linguistic and Paralinguistic
- The book concludes where it began: trying to untangle the linguistic and the paralinguistic (p. 309)
- Ladd proposes that the three-way classification of pitch range effects (intrinsic, extrinsic, metrical) provides the theoretical basis for designing empirical studies, and the AM approach to pitch range as target scaling will help interpret such studies (p. 309)
- The boundary between language and paralanguage remains unclear, but the existence of an intermediate "metrical" category may represent a kind of phonologisation of basic paralinguistic functions (p. 308-309)

## F0/Pitch Rules and Patterns

### Downstep as Syntagmatic Relation
- Downstep is a scaling factor **orthogonal to accent type** that modifies the pitch range at specific points in the contour (p. 305)
- Pierrehumbert (1980) treated downstep as a local phonological rule operating on specific sequences of tones; Ladd (1983a) argued this fails to capture the functional unity of downstep across accent types
- Ladd proposed instead that downstep is a syntagmatic relation between two accents or prosodic constituents that can be represented metrically:
  - (32a): h over l (T* T*) = downstepped relation
  - (32b): l over h (T* T*) = non-downstepped relation (p. 305)

### Nested Downstep
- Downstep can be "nested" (Truckenbrodt 2002, 2004; Fery and Truckenbrodt 2005): downstep within a series of short phrases can be superimposed on an overall downward trend from phrase to phrase across the utterance as a whole (p. 307)
- The metrical relation h-l at the accent level can be extended to the phrase level:
  - Diagram (33): A tree structure showing h-l relations at both the accent level (between T* and T* within a phrase) and the phrase level (between two phrases), enabling nested register effects (p. 307)

### Pitch Range Independence
- Metrical pitch range effects (downstep, Anna/Manny relations) can apparently be controlled independently of clearly extrinsic global range modifications -- when speakers modify their overall range, the pitch range relations involved in downstep and the Anna/Manny construction remain the same (p. 306-307)

## Pitch Accent and Tone Inventory

### Focus and Primary Accent
- Focus is signalled by **primary accent** -- the prominence of the focused word is evaluated relative to the accents elsewhere in the sentence (p. 301)
- Example (26): H* L H* L H* L H* LL% on "The butcher charged me a thousand bucks!" -- primary accent on all four content words signals the epithet reading (p. 301)

### Compound Contour Examples

## Implementation Notes

### F0 Generation: Three-Layer Pitch Scaling Model
For a TTS system, the three-way pitch range classification suggests three independent layers of F0 scaling:

1. **Intrinsic layer**: The tonal string itself (H, L, M targets) determines the basic pitch target values relative to the current local tonal space
2. **Extrinsic layer**: Global pitch range modifications (overall level and span) for paralinguistic effects like emphasis, emotion, or speaker characteristics -- this scales the entire tonal space up/down and wider/narrower
3. **Metrical layer**: Localized, structurally-conditioned pitch range shifts controlled by prosodic structure:
   - **Downstep**: Apply h-l relation between successive accents, compressing the tonal space for the second accent
   - **Nested downstep**: Apply h-l relation between successive phrases, creating a phrase-level declination superimposed on accent-level downstep
   - **Declination reset**: Reset pitch range upward at discourse/paragraph boundaries
   - **Final lowering**: Compress pitch range downward at utterance-final positions

### Focus Implementation via Compound Prosodic Domains
- Focus can be signalled not only by making the focused word's accent the nuclear (last/strongest) accent in the phrase, but also by adjusting prosodic boundary strength between the focused word and the rest of the sentence
- For emphatic/contrastive focus (e.g., "The BUTCHER charged me..."), treat each accented word as having its own local intermediate phrase boundary, creating a compound ip structure
- This means the TTS system needs to be able to generate variable-depth prosodic trees and assign boundary strengths accordingly

### Compound Contour Handling
- When a tune spans a compound phrase (two or more ips), the realization differs from when it spans a simple ip
- Example: L*...H* LL% (simple ip) vs. L* H ... !H* L L% (compound: the same underlying surprise-redundancy tune split across two ips)
- The TTS system must handle tune-text association that distributes a single abstract tune across multiple prosodic domains
